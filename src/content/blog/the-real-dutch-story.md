I promised this one back in the Salt Rising Bread post. The short version of that story was: my mother's side of the family has real Dutch roots, but they never went anywhere near Pennsylvania — they went the other way entirely, from the Netherlands to Malacca and Singapore, and then, eventually, to the American West. I said it deserved its own post instead of a paragraph riding along on someone else's bread. Here it is.

My mother put a lot of this down on paper herself, in a family memoir she published back in 2013. I'm not going to lift her pages wholesale — that's her book, her voice, and her right to it — but I've been given the tour of it, and I want to walk you through it the way she walked me through it growing up, with a little of my own digging into the history layered underneath, the same way I did for the San Francisco post and for Sami's trip to Mammoth Cave.

**It starts with a ship's pilot, maybe seven years old.** The family legend runs through my mother's grandfather, Grandpa Alex, who used to tell his kids about his own great-great-grandfather: a Dutchman named Sjouwke Westerhout, born in 1717, who worked as a pilot on a Dutch East India Company ship. Grandpa Alex passed down a rumor — and it's presented as exactly that, a rumor, which I appreciate — that Sjouwke signed on as a cabin boy at only seven years old. Whatever the truth of his start, he first landed in Malacca at twenty, and by every account that followed, the place got its hooks into him immediately: flowering trees so vivid from the water they looked like the jungle was on fire (they were called, appropriately, Flame of the Forest), hundred-foot ansonia trees blooming gold, exquisitely plumed birds, and dockside talk of pirates, headhunters, sultans, and curved krisses. Sjouwke came back a few years later as captain of the same ship, fell for a young woman there, and stayed on as sheriff. According to the family story, four generations of Westerhouts held some version of that post after him.

**The next story is the one Grandpa Alex told most, and it turns out to be a real war.** This is where family legend and the historical record actually meet, which doesn't happen often enough to pass up. Grandpa Alex's account centers on his own grandfather, known as JB Westerhout, and a dramatic field negotiation with the Penghulu (chief) of a small territory called Naning — two men meeting under a tree, swapping coats as a show of good faith, the Penghulu eventually surrendering his claim to a disputed fort. It's a good story, and J.B. Westerhout was a real person: British colonial records confirm a Dutch-descended administrator by that name was installed as superintendent of Naning. But the fuller history is messier, and more interesting, than a tidy negotiation under a tree.

What actually happened was the Naning War of 1831–32 — a real conflict between the British East India Company and Naning's chief, Dol Said, over whether Britain had inherited the Dutch right to tax Naning's rice harvest at ten percent. The British badly overestimated how much rice Naning could produce (they guessed at roughly 750,000 gantangs a year; the real figure was closer to 130,000), sent an underprepared expedition of about 150 troops that got bogged down by felled trees and sniping and had to retreat in humiliation, then came back seven months later with ten times the force and finally took Dol Said's village. Dol Said held out for another two years before surrendering under a pardon. Today, in Malaysia, he's remembered as a resistance hero, not a man who lost gracefully at a negotiating table. J.B. Westerhout stepping in afterward as the tax-collecting superintendent is accurate — it's just the epilogue to a war, not a substitute for one. I'd rather tell you both halves than let the family version stand as the whole picture; the real story, as usual, has more to it and isn't any less compelling for the correction.

**Then the story turns toward romance, tragedy, and a very inconvenient felony.** Grandpa Alex himself — the fourth of seven brothers and one sister — grew up an entrepreneur's entrepreneur, the type who fell hard for each new business idea and moved on to the next one just as fast. He fell just as hard for a young woman named Eva in Malacca, and the courtship was a two-year fight against her father, a strict, close-fisted man who considered the theater (where Alex acted and sang) a waste of time and quietly preferred his own nephew, freshly returned from England as a lawyer, as a son-in-law. It only got resolved when Eva's father and mother died in a shipwreck returning from Singapore, an overloaded ship that "went down with all hands" on a cut-rate passage timed badly against the monsoon. Eva's aunt, made her legal guardian, held out against the marriage for two more years before relenting. Alex and Eva then discovered, two days after their wedding, that they'd committed a literal felony — marrying a ward of the court without the judge's permission — and had to appear before that judge to be talked down from it. They went on to have six sons before Eva, a recent convert to the Seventh-day Adventist church, died shortly after the birth of the sixth. Her dying wish, extracted as a promise, was that all six boys receive a Christian education.

**That promise is what got the family to America — almost by accident.** Alex spent years trying to make good on it in Singapore and Malacca: hosting Adventist missionaries in his own home, giving over the ground floor to a mission school, praying for a lady teacher who'd be "a mother to my six boys." When her health finally failed in the equatorial climate and she was sent home, Alex considered following her — or, in one version of the rumor mill, considered England or Australia purely for his sons' education. His sister Edith talked him out of Australia (her verdict on the people there was not kind), so he booked passage to England instead. It was 1916. World War I was already underway, and the shipping company ended up swapping his tickets for a voyage to Canada instead — a substitution that, as it turned out, saved his life and his sons': the ship bound for England was lost at sea with no survivors. Alex gave away nearly everything he owned in Malacca and Singapore before he left — a fleet of ships, a newspaper, an ice factory, a soda factory, a tin mine, several plantations, a sawmill — to his brothers and his church, so his sons would arrive in America unencumbered by anything to manage from a continent away.

**He landed, by way of that swapped ticket, at Walla Walla, Washington** — home, as it happened, to a Seventh-day Adventist college that had been standing since 1892. Six sons in tow, ages six to fifteen, no wife and no nanny, Alex crossed the ocean in a white linen suit and a straw hat, carrying a malacca cane that's apparently still hanging on a wall in the family somewhere. He sewed the boys' buttons and mended their clothes himself along the way, and by his own account, his first real culture shock in America involved a barber he mistook for eating lunch on the job — the man was chewing gum, a habit so unthinkable in Singapore that people there were reportedly caned for it. That struck me as worth checking, and it turns out gum-chewing goes back a lot further than the family account gives it credit for: the oldest confirmed evidence is a wad of birch-bark tar, chewed by a teenager in Scandinavia somewhere between 9,500 and 9,900 years ago. So it's an old habit — just not one that had made it to Singapore by the 1910s.

**The America chapter didn't get easier once they'd arrived.** Alex settled the family in College Place, brought over one of the Adventist missionary teachers, Mame, to remarry and mother his six boys (who, by every account, never entirely forgave her strict housekeeping standards), and then watched the rubber fortune he'd left behind evaporate anyway — the price of rubber, which had boomed through 1910 as the world went mad for automobile tires, collapsed through the 1910s and 1920s as Malayan plantations matured and flooded the market, the same overproduction story that shows up in the real economic record of the period. An offer of sixty thousand dollars for his old Malacca rubber plantation — a small fortune at the time — shrank considerably by the time commissions and go-betweens took their cut. Looking to reinvest closer to home, Alex was talked into buying a ranch near Touchet, Washington, on the promise of good neighbors and a nearby school, despite having zero experience with alkali soil or Pacific Northwest weather. It went about as badly as his instincts warned him it would: nighttime trips to unclog an irrigation well, a crop that never paid off, and a resigned verdict, in his own words, that "I know the Lord disapproved of that investment."

**Here's where the story loops back to me in a way I didn't expect until I sat down to write this.** Alex crossed an ocean and swapped a fortune in Malacca for a house in College Place so his six sons could get a Christian education at the Adventist college there. Generations later, with no particular plan to follow in his footsteps, I spent my own first year of college at what's now Walla Walla University — the same institution, the same small town, a coincidence of family geography I only fully understood while writing this post. My mother has spent years making sure this story didn't disappear the way so many family stories do, told at gatherings and then never written down. I'm glad she wrote it down. Consider the notice from the Salt Rising Bread post served — the real Dutch story, delivered, jungle fires and felony weddings and a chewed stick of gum included.
