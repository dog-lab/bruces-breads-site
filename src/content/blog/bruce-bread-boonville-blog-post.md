Every so often I scroll through my site logs to see what people typed into a search box before landing here, and one phrase kept turning up: **"Bruce Bread."** Not "Bruce's Breads," the way it says on my banner, but the shorter, blunter version, as if somebody out there had a loaf by that name in mind. I'm a retired database guy, so an unexplained pattern in the logs is catnip. I had to know whether there was another Bruce baking bread somewhere, and whether I'd been collecting his customers by accident.

There was. He turned out to be a better story than I had any right to expect.

**First, a detour through Indianapolis.** On my recent trip through Indiana I'd already stumbled on [Bruce's Bakery, founded in Indianapolis in 1875](https://indianaalbum.pastperfectonline.com/photo/31574945-9959-4046-AE19-663655482549). That one is a real Bruce's Bakery with a real photo archive and a real century and a half of history behind it, and I'd assumed it was the end of the trail. It isn't related to this story, but hold onto that year, 1875, because it comes back around in a way I couldn't have scripted.

**The Bruce I was actually looking for is from Boonville, California.** I know this part of the world. Boonville sits in Anderson Valley in Mendocino County, tucked right up against Sonoma County, where I lived before Georgia. It's also on the road to my favorite Mendocino winery, Navarro, where I'm still a wine club member all these years and a few thousand miles later, so I've driven through this town more times than I can count without ever knowing a bakery story was hiding in it. It's famous for apples, wine, and a homegrown local slang called Boontling, which tells you something about the kind of place that decides it needs its own language. Bruce Bread was born there.

**The baker was Bruce Hering,** born in Buffalo, New York, in 1935, to a family of mostly German descent. His father worked in building-materials manufacturing and was in demand enough that the family kept moving, so Bruce grew up in a lot of places before landing in Boonville in 1981. By 1984 he'd started a bakery with his daughters Diane and Ellen. He didn't have a business plan. What he had was an oven, a mixer, and a local business owner named Burt who believed in his bread.

The origin story is great. Bruce had been making bread informally, a local business owner (Burt) wanted to sell it, and Burt pointed out that it would need to come out of a "legal" commercial kitchen. So Burt put an oven and a mixer in his own kitchen, and every afternoon around four, Bruce and Ellen showed up and baked through the night. The bread hit Burt's shelves and, as the story goes, it "just took off." Within a few years they'd outgrown the borrowed kitchen, moved into three rooms behind a shop down the street, and eventually into the unimproved rear of a place called the Farrer Building. Remember that building.

**Why people lined up for it.** The 1980s were a golden age of the whole-grain health-food loaf, and many of those loaves were, to put it kindly, structural. Dense, heavy, and carrying what locals described as a medicinal flavor, they were bread you ate as penance. Bruce Bread went the other way: deep-brown loaves studded with raisins and nuts, wholesome without being a chore. If you've read my posts on [flour and hydration](/guides/ph-flour-hydration/), you know why that's harder than it sounds. Whole grain soaks up water and chews up gluten, and keeping a nutritious loaf soft takes real technique. This was a family doing it on a schedule, at night, in someone else's kitchen.

**The delivery routes are the part that got me.** Bruce personally delivered his bread six days a week, across the valley, north and south, and all the way out to the coast. He freely admitted it made no business sense. Driving to the coast to drop off two loaves, he said, "was not a moneymaker." He did it anyway, because he liked the "cool people" working in the back of grocery stores. The family was woven into the valley in other ways too. Diane hosted a bluegrass show on the local public radio station and went on to found the Wild Iris Folk Festival, and the bakery ran on friends and friends of friends. People weren't just buying bread. They were backing a family they liked. Bruce also rode his bike, hiked, danced, played Scrabble, and doted on his grandchildren. He lived in Boonville until he died in February 2014, at 78.

I don't mind admitting that reading this made me feel something. A bread business built on stubbornness, goodwill, and a refusal to do the math on gas money is a lot closer to the spirit of this website than most of what shows up when you search for "sourdough business plan."

**Now the part where beer walks in.** After more than a dozen years, the Herings sold Bruce Bread in the late 1990s to Mary Loftsgaard-Trevino, who'd worked with them for several years. Under her it kept growing, delivering across Mendocino County and much of Sonoma. And that's where the small-town drama starts, courtesy of the AVA's [Boonville Beer vs. Bruce Bread](https://theava.com/archives/254786), which is the best local-history read I've had in months.

A quick cast list, because this story has one. The AVA's author is **Bruce Anderson**, which makes this the third Bruce in a story about a bread. The building is the **Farrer Building**, built in **1875** as a general store and community center. Yes, the same year as the Indianapolis bakery, a coincidence I'm choosing to treat as a sign from the universe. Over the years it also had a second-floor dance hall and occasionally doubled as a courtroom, and the Ukiah vintner John Parducci renovated it and rented out shop space. The landlord when our story kicks off was **Ken Allen**, who arrived in Boonville in the late 1970s as a chiropractor and went on to co-found the Boonville brewery in the mid-1980s with David Norfleet. That brewery grew into a nationally distributed premium beer, and Allen later built a brewpub next door to the Farrer Building. The bakery was his tenant, renting the rear of the building.

**And here's where the story gets personal for me.** The brewery Allen co-founded is, as far as I can tell, **Anderson Valley Brewing Company**, the one behind Boont Amber and the oatmeal stout, and I've been there. I've had more than a few of both, in the town where this whole drama played out, without any idea that the guy whose name is on the brewery's origin story once changed the locks on a bakery mid-bake. The details line up: the brewery started in the lower level of the Buckhorn Saloon brewpub in downtown Boonville (the brewpub next to the Farrer Building), then moved its production out to a bigger facility on Highway 128, just as the AVA describes. The AVA calls it "the Boonville brewery," and I haven't found a source that says "Anderson Valley" in so many words, so call it a strong inference rather than a certified fact. Either way, Bruce Bread and Boont Amber shared a zip code, a landlord, and, if the AVA is right, at least one very bad week.

It's also worth pausing on how improbable all of this is. Boonville is tiny, tiny, tiny, a couple of blocks of main street and roughly a thousand residents, and it somehow produced a nationally distributed craft brewery, a regional bakery with delivery routes to the coast, a folk festival, and its own slang. Small towns don't usually have the customers for that, which is exactly why the story is so good: the ones who make it work are people willing to drive two loaves to the coast, or haul a brewery's worth of beer well beyond town lines, because the place was worth the trouble.

**Per the AVA, the trouble started on a Wednesday, mid-bake.** Allen showed up with a workman, changed the locks, and told the bakery to close. Mrs. Loftsgaard-Trevino called the sheriff's office, and Deputy Squires explained that a landlord can't just order a business shut. Allen left. The bakery reopened Thursday. Allen came back, and by the AVA's account shouted at employees and confronted several of the women working there, using language I'm not going to repeat on a bread blog. Deputies escorted him out a second time, and he reportedly told one tenant, "You're next!" Later that day a property manager delivered Mrs. Loftsgaard-Trevino a letter giving her three days to vacate.

**So what was the fight about?** Repairs, in a word, or the lack of them. The bakery said Allen wouldn't make the repairs he was obligated to make, and the list is a landlord's nightmare: a floor on the verge of collapse, wiring that didn't inspire confidence, unreliable water, fire scorching on the ceiling from a recent incident, and a mouse problem. Rent was $1,450 a month, with a 5% increase coming due. Mrs. Loftsgaard-Trevino withheld the increase until repairs happened. Allen's reported answer was, "If you don't like the space, leave."

Her reply, as quoted in the piece, is the line I'd put on a mug: *"I just decided he's not going to do that to me."* And when it came to finding somewhere else to bake, she pointed out that **"there isn't that kind of space."** Anyone who's tried to find a commercial kitchen in a small town knows exactly what she meant.

The bakery did eventually leave. Bruce Bread relocated to Redwood Valley, and the AVA says the building's essential repairs were finally made later on by a subsequent owner. A bread business, in other words, got pushed out of a building that needed fixing, and the fixing happened after it left.

**Why do beer and bread always end up in the same story?** The AVA's headline asks it, and it's a good question with a fun answer. Beer and bread are fraternal twins. Both are grain, water, and yeast, and both are really just fermentation with different marketing. Brewers have been handing off their spent grain to bakers for centuries, and bakers have been borrowing the wild-yeast tricks that brewers perfected. Some historians even argue that humans may have started farming grain because they wanted beer as much as bread. It makes sense that they'd end up as neighbors, and, in the Farrer Building, as landlord and tenant. It also makes sense that when the two collide, the result is more entertaining than either would be alone.

**So, back to my logs.** Was I stealing Bruce Bread's search traffic? A little, probably, and I'm fine with it. If somebody typed "Bruce Bread" looking for a family-run Boonville bakery and landed here on a site that tells his story, I think Bruce Hering would call that a win. He cheerfully admitted he wasn't a businessman. He was a guy who drove two loaves of bread to the coast because he liked the people at the other end.

That's a better business philosophy than most I've read, and it's the one I'd hang on the wall of any bakery, mine included. If you ever knew Bruce Bread, or knew the Herings, or have a Farrer Building story of your own, I'd love to hear it.
