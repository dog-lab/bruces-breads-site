Bread doesn't fail silently. A dense crumb, a blown-out score, a crust that's gone leathery by dinnertime — every one of those is the dough telling you exactly which decision upstream needs to change. The trouble is that the fingerprint and the crime scene are rarely in the same place: a crumb problem was usually committed hours earlier, back in bulk fermentation, and a lot of home bakers spend their troubleshooting energy on the stage where the symptom *showed up* instead of the stage where it actually *happened*.

Several of this site's other guides already carry their own troubleshooting tables, scoped tightly to their own subject — the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) covers pH, flour, hydration, and temperature failures; [Proofing and Shaping](/guides/proofing-and-shaping) and [Braiding](/guides/braiding) cover shape-specific ones; [Flour Types and Blending](/guides/flour-types-and-blending) covers flour-specific ones; [Salt](/guides/salt-timing-and-gluten) covers, well, salt; and [Discard](/guides/discard) covers what's actually different about baking with discard. Rather than repeat those in full, this page does two things: it indexes them by what you'd actually *notice* first, in roughly the order a bake happens, so you can jump straight to the deep-dive guide that owns your problem — and it covers the territory that doesn't have a natural home in any of them. Nobody else on this site talks about scoring, burnt bottoms, what your crumb is actually telling you after the loaf cools, laminated dough falling apart, or how to keep a good loaf good for more than a day. That's all here.

> Work backward. Crust and oven spring point to the bake itself; crumb structure points to fermentation and shaping, hours earlier; a starter that won't cooperate points further back still. Diagnose from the outside in, and check the earliest stage first — it's usually the real culprit, even when a later stage is where you noticed.

## Part 1: Starter Problems

If the starter isn't right, nothing downstream has a chance — which is exactly why this site gives it two full guides of its own rather than a paragraph here. What follows is the short list; for the real depth (the day-by-day build, the float test, reading ripeness by starter type, dehydrating and reviving), go to [Creating a Starter](/guides/starter/creating-a-starter) and [Maintaining a Starter](/guides/starter/maintaining-a-starter) directly.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No bubble activity at all in the first week | Chlorinated water, too little whole grain, or too cold a spot | Switch to filtered/dechlorinated water; use the 50/50 whole wheat blend; move somewhere warmer (see [Creating a Starter](/guides/starter/creating-a-starter)) |
| Vigorous, deceptive rise around day 2–3, then goes quiet | The "false rise" — normal, not a failure | Don't feed early. Wait out the full 72 hours before the first discard-and-feed |
| Smells strongly of vinegar or acetone, weak rise | Over-acidified (pH below ~3.5) | Discard most of it, feed at a high ratio (1:10:10) every 8–12 hours until it recovers |
| Clear or brownish liquid pooling on top | Hungry starter (true hooch) | Pour off, stir, feed as usual; feed more often if it recurs |
| Classic gray, fuzzy mold | Contamination | Discard the whole batch, sterilize the jar, start over |

## Part 2: Mixing and Bulk Fermentation

This is the stage the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) and [Flour Types and Blending](/guides/flour-types-and-blending) already cover in real depth — pH drift, hydration ceilings, whole-grain timing, rye's narrow window. The short version, for a lean or moderately enriched dough:

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dough spreads flat, won't hold a dome | Overproofed and/or hydration too high for the flour | Shorten bulk; drop hydration 5%; step up to bread flour (Four Levers guide) |
| Dense, tight crumb despite a normal-looking bulk | Underproofed, or a whole-grain dough handled on white-flour timing | Extend bulk; judge by percent rise, not the clock (Four Levers guide, Part 4) |
| Dough unmanageably sticky and slack | Hydration too high for the flour's strength | Reduce water; longer autolyse; blend in white flour (Flour Types guide) |
| Same recipe behaves differently by season | Dough temperature drifting with the kitchen | Measure dough temp after mixing; hit DDT with water temperature |

### Enriched dough plays by different rules

Butter, milk, sugar, and eggs slow fermentation down and weaken the gluten network, so an enriched dough (brioche-style, milk-bread-style) shouldn't be chasing the same doubling target a lean loaf does. As a rule of thumb: lightly enriched doughs want roughly a **30–50% rise** during bulk before a cold retard, and heavily enriched doughs (high butter, high sugar, eggs) want closer to **20–40%** — well short of doubling either way, because the dough keeps expanding slowly through the first hour or two in the fridge as it cools. Watch the dough rather than trusting a percentage in isolation: a domed top, visible bubbles through the sides of the container, and a buoyant jiggle when you shake the vessel all say "ready," regardless of what the clock says.

The other enriched-dough-specific failure point is *when* the butter goes in:

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dough turns greasy, slick, won't come back together | Butter added too warm, emulsion broke | Chill the dough 15 minutes, then continue incorporating in smaller additions |
| Dough tears into lumps instead of smoothing out | Butter added too cold and hard | Let butter soften a few minutes more before adding; add gradually |
| Dough runs noticeably slower than expected even at the right temperature | Normal — fat and sugar are throttles, not stalls | Give it more time, not more starter; re-check at 30–50% rise rather than a fixed hour count |

## Part 3: Shaping and Final Proof

[Proofing and Shaping](/guides/proofing-and-shaping) covers the full shape library and its own troubleshooting table — torn surfaces, tunnels, dough that won't hold tension, the poke test. One common frustration that doesn't fit neatly into that guide: getting the loaf *out* of the banneton in one piece.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dough sticks and tears coming out of the banneton | Not enough flour worked into the weave, or a brand-new unseasoned basket | Dust generously — a 50/50 rice flour and all-purpose blend releases better than all-purpose alone, since rice flour doesn't absorb as much moisture; a banneton also releases more easily after a few uses as it seasons |
| Dough sticks even though it was well-floured | Proofed too warm and too long, softening back into stickiness | Chill the shaped loaf longer before unmolding — a firm, cold surface releases far more cleanly than a soft, warm one |
| Loaf deflates or drags badly turning out | Handled too roughly, or unmolded straight from a warm proof | Turn out onto a lightly floured peel or board in one confident motion; run a thin bench scraper around the edge first if it's really stuck |

## Part 4: Scoring

Scoring gets a single line in most beginner recipes and deserves its own section, because a badly-opened score isn't a cosmetic problem — it's usually telling you something about the dough itself.

**Depth and angle matter less than people think.** Somewhere around 1/2 inch (1 cm) deep, cut in one confident, unhesitating stroke, works for almost every shape on this site — a shallow hesitant score is the more common beginner mistake, so err deeper rather than shallower. The blade angle (straight down versus tilted) matters less than the confidence of the stroke.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Blade drags instead of gliding through | Dull blade, or dough too warm and soft | Swap in a fresh blade; chill the loaf 15–20 minutes in the freezer right before scoring for a firmer surface |
| Score doesn't open at all, or the cut is too shallow to matter | Cut too shallow, or dough overproofed and losing structure | Cut a full 1/2 inch; if it's overproofed, there's no scoring fix — shorten the proof next time |
| Dough spreads and deflates the moment you cut it | Overproofed — the score just revealed what was already true | Bake it anyway; note the proof time in the [Baking Log](/resources/baking-log) and pull it back next time |
| Score tears raggedly on a seeded or inclusion-heavy loaf | A blade catches on nuts, seeds, or dried fruit right at the surface | Use kitchen scissors instead of a blade for heavily seeded doughs |

## Part 5: The Oven — Spring, Crust, and Color

Steam is doing two jobs at once in the first 15–20 minutes of a bake: it keeps the loaf's surface soft and stretchy so oven spring can actually happen, and it gelatinizes the starch on that surface into the glossy, blistered crust this site's photos are full of. Both jobs need steam early and *neither* wants it late — venting it out around the 15–20 minute mark (removing the Dutch oven lid, or the towel/pan setup for an open bake) is what lets the crust actually brown and firm up instead of staying pale and soft.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pale, soft, underdeveloped crust | Not enough steam, or vented too early | Extend the covered/steamed portion of the bake; a preheated Dutch oven traps steam more reliably than a towel-and-pan setup |
| Thick, leathery, tough crust | Steam vented too late, or baked too long past set | Uncover closer to the 15–20 minute mark; pull the loaf once color and internal temp both say done, not later |
| Bottom crust burnt or nearly black while the top looks fine | Direct contact with a dark, heat-absorbing pot, or too much bottom heat | Bake on a lighter-colored enamel surface if you have one, slide a second baking sheet onto the rack below, or add a layer of parchment between dough and pot |
| Weak oven spring despite a good-looking proof | Oven (and Dutch oven, if using one) not fully preheated, or the door opened too early | Give a Dutch oven a full 45–60 minutes of preheat; resist checking in the first 15–20 minutes |

## Part 6: The Cut Crumb — What the Bread Is Telling You

Once it's out of the oven, the crumb itself is the most honest record of everything that happened upstream — but two things can produce the exact same "gummy" complaint for completely different reasons, and it's worth telling them apart before changing anything.

**Give it real time before you cut.** A loaf that seems gummy or wet ten minutes out of the oven is very often just a loaf that hasn't finished setting — the crumb structure firms up as it cools, and slicing into hot bread interrupts that process and reads as underdone even when it isn't. An hour minimum, ideally two, before the first slice.

**Internal temperature is a good check, but not the whole story.** Most sourdough reaches a fully set crumb somewhere between 203–210°F (95–99°C). Pulled much earlier than that (under roughly 185°F), the center is still essentially liquid — genuinely underbaked, and a loaf like that also goes stale unusually fast. But temperature alone doesn't guarantee a great result either — two loaves can both read 210°F and differ in crust color and crumb quality depending on total bake time, so use the thermometer alongside color and sound (a hollow knock on the bottom), not instead of them.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Gummy, wet crumb straight out of the oven | Sliced too soon — the crumb hasn't finished setting | Wait the full hour-plus before cutting; check internal temp next time to confirm it wasn't also underbaked |
| Gummy crumb even after a full rest and a good internal temp | Enzyme activity outran the acidity — common in rye and whole-grain loaves | Use a more sour levain; see the rye/amylase discussion in the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration), Part 2 |
| Large tunnel just under the crust, tight crumb everywhere else | Shaping trapped a seam of big bubbles, or uneven fermentation | Gentler, more even pressure during final shape — see [Proofing and Shaping](/guides/proofing-and-shaping) |
| Crumb dry and crumbly | Overbaked, or too little water for the flour | Pull earlier next time; check hydration against the flour in use |
| Loaf deflates or the crumb collapses after cooling | Overproofed, or cut before it finished cooling and setting | Shorten the proof; give it the full rest before slicing |

## Part 7: Laminated and Enriched Dough Failures

Butter is the whole game in a laminated dough, and nearly every lamination failure traces back to a mismatch between how cold the butter is and how cold the dough is around it.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Butter cracks and breaks into a marbled, uneven layer instead of a smooth sheet | Butter too cold and hard when rolled | Let it temper a few minutes at room temperature until malleable but still cool |
| Butter leaks out during rolling or baking | Butter too warm, or the dough hasn't rested enough between folds | Chill the whole package 20–30 minutes between folds; work on a cool surface |
| Layers vanish — dense, bready result instead of flaky | Rolled with too much pressure, or the gluten fought back and got overworked | Handle gently; if the dough resists rolling, chill it 10–20 minutes to let the gluten relax before continuing |
| Flat, under-risen laminated pastry | Underproofed — commercial or wild yeast didn't get there | Proof warm (76–79°F) until visibly puffed and slightly wobbly before baking, not on a fixed clock |

**If it's already gone wrong, it's not wasted dough.** A laminated dough that's torn, leaking, or lost its layers is still a rich, buttery, high-fat dough — which makes it a strong candidate for a rescue rather than the trash. Chill it if it's warm and sticky, then repurpose it: work it back together with a mixer and treat it as an enriched brioche-style dough for braids or buns; press it into a loaf pan for a "croissant loaf" that's excellent sliced thick for French toast; stuff pieces into a muffin tin with cinnamon and sugar; or roll thin scraps in sugar and fold into rough palmiers. None of these need pristine layers to taste good.

## Part 8: Storage and Staling

Staling isn't the same thing as drying out — it's **starch retrogradation**, a re-crystallization of the starches in the crumb that happens regardless of humidity and speeds up at cooler temperatures. That last part is the counterintuitive one: the refrigerator, which feels like it should preserve bread, actually accelerates staling compared to leaving a loaf at room temperature.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Goes hard and stale within a day or two | Stored in the fridge, or left uncovered too long at room temp | Never refrigerate; switch to airtight storage (a bag, a breadbox) once past the first 12–24 hours |
| Crust turns soft and leathery in storage | Normal trade-off of airtight storage protecting the crumb | A few minutes in a hot oven re-crisps the crust if it matters for that meal |
| Interior stale well before the crust shows it (or vice versa) | Moisture migrating from crumb to crust, or just time | Slice and freeze what won't be eaten in 2–3 days — freezing halts retrogradation entirely |
| Mold sooner than expected | Storage too humid, or a less-acidic bake | Sourdough's acidity should outlast straight-yeast bread's shelf life; check the bag/box for trapped moisture |

**To freeze:** slice first, separate layers with parchment, and freeze in an airtight bag — individual slices go straight into a toaster from frozen. **To revive a whole thawed or day-old loaf:** mist it lightly with water and reheat uncovered at 350°F for about 15 minutes; the added moisture plus heat re-gelatinizes the crust the same way the original bake's steam did.

## Part 9: Discard-Specific Wrinkles

Discard behaves differently enough from active starter that it earns a guide of its own now — see [Discard](/guides/discard) for the real depth (why its gluten is already partly broken down, why it tastes different depending on when you made it, and which recipes actually need it to leaven versus just flavor and tenderize). The short list, for anything baked with discard rather than active starter:

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Discard bake (pancakes, waffles, crackers) turns out dense or gummy | Discard's own water and flour weren't counted toward the recipe's total hydration | Treat discard as part of the recipe's flour-and-water total, not a free extra ([Discard](/guides/discard)) |
| Discard tastes unusually sour or funky in a mild recipe | Discard was old or very ripe | Use fresher discard (within a few days of the fridge), or dilute it with a little fresh flour and water first ([Discard](/guides/discard), Part 4) |
| Saved discard smells off, looks pink, orange, or streaky | Contamination — different from ordinary hooch or normal browning | Discard it (the whole point of the name, this time); see [Maintaining a Starter](/guides/starter/maintaining-a-starter) for the difference between hooch and actual spoilage |

## When to Start Over (and When Not To)

Most of what lands on this page isn't a reason to throw anything out — it's a reason to write down what happened and adjust one variable next time. A slightly overproofed loaf still makes good toast. A blown-out score still tastes the same under the crust. Even a genuinely wrecked laminated dough turns into something worth eating, per Part 7 above. The only real dead ends on this list are contamination (actual mold, not hooch) and a starter with no bubble activity at all after a full week — everything else is a data point, not a failure. That's what the [Baking Log](/resources/baking-log) is for: the fastest way to stop making the same fix twice is writing down what actually worked the first time.
