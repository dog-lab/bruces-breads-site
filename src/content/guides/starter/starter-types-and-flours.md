Every recipe on the live site today uses the same basic starter type: a loose, 100%-hydration culture fed with a white/rye flour blend. That's still the right default for lean and moderately enriched doughs — but it's one point on a spectrum, not the only one. Two of the three approved enriched/laminated bases — the Brioche and Laminated Pastry bases — already build on a **stiff** starter instead, while the Soft Sandwich Loaf base was reviewed alongside them and deliberately kept the standard starter (see the note at the end of this page for all three).

## Hydration: Liquid, Regular, and Stiff

"Hydration" here means the ratio of water to flour in the starter itself, same as it does for a bread dough.

![Liquid starter (left) vs. stiff starter (right), side by side](/images/guides/05_liquid_vs_stiff_comparison.jpg)

| Type | Hydration | Texture | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Liquid** | ~100–150% (as much water as flour, or more) | Pourable, loose, batter-like at the high end | Fast, highly visible fermentation — easy to read bubbles and rise; common for rye-heavy or very active starters |
| **Regular / Standard** | 100% (equal parts flour and water) | Thick but spoonable, the "classic" starter texture | This site's default so far — every recipe published to date uses this |
| **Stiff** | 45–65% (about half as much water as flour — this site's bases run 45–50%, the firm end) | Dense, moldable, closer to a firm dough ball than a batter | Slower fermentation, more yeast-dominant and less acidic character, easier to handle in rich or delicate doughs |

### Reading ripeness differs by type

A **liquid** starter shows ripeness the way most people picture it: lots of surface bubbles and aeration, a visibly loose, broken-down texture when stirred or pulled back.

A **stiff** starter looks different at peak. Mixed and shaped into a rough ball, it relaxes to fill the jar and domes upward as it ferments. When mature, that dome stops looking like a ball and starts looking more like a plateau — it flattens and begins to recede, the surface turns soft and slightly cracked, and pulling it back reveals a pungent, sour smell with a mixture that's noticeably softened all through. If you're used to reading a liquid starter's obvious bubble-and-rise signs, a ripe stiff starter can look deceptively quiet at first glance — the cracked, collapsing-dome texture is the tell, not surface foam.

### Why reach for a stiff starter specifically

A lower-hydration culture ferments more slowly and tends to favor yeast activity over the acid-producing bacteria that make a starter taste sour — practically, that means a milder flavor and a firmer, easier-to-manage mass. That combination matters for doughs where too much acidity or too much added liquid works against the goal: rich, butter-and-egg-heavy enriched doughs (brioche-style) and laminated doughs (croissant-style) both benefit from a starter that isn't fighting the dough's own structure or diluting a carefully balanced hydration. This is exactly the reasoning behind Bruce's stiff-starter preference for the site's Enriched & Sweet and Pastry & Lamination bases — see the note at the end of this page.

A stiff starter may also stay stable longer when feedings get sparse. In a 2026 Belgian study, a firm wholemeal sourdough (dough yield 160, about 60% hydration, a little wetter than this site's 45–50%) kept its *F. sanfranciscensis* and *M. humilis* pairing through months of refreshing only every three weeks, while a semi-liquid one (dough yield 200) lost both organisms within 12 to 15 weeks. The two came from different bakeries with different routines, so it points the direction rather than proving the cause; the full details are in [Maintaining a Starter](/guides/starter/maintaining-a-starter).

### Converting your standing starter to a stiff one

You don't need to maintain a second, permanently separate culture from scratch — a stiff starter is easy to build as an offshoot of the standard 100%-hydration starter this site already maintains:

1. Take a small amount of ripe standard starter as your carryover.
2. Feed it flour and water at roughly **half the water weight relative to flour** — e.g., 20g carryover, 100g flour, 50g water (a 1:5:2.5 ratio, landing around 50–55% hydration).
3. Mix thoroughly — this takes more effort than stirring a loose starter, since it's closer to kneading a stiff dough than stirring a batter. Expect to really work it with a spatula or by hand.
4. Let it ferment at the same warm room temperature as usual. It'll rise more slowly than the liquid version did at the same ratio — that's expected, not a sign of weakness.

If you want to keep a stiff starter going as its own maintained culture (rather than building it fresh each time from the standard starter), feed it the same way at each refresh, same hydration ratio every time.

### Multi-feed levain builds don't compound toward drier and drier

A natural worry when building a levain across two or more feedings (common for enriched or laminated doughs, which want more leavening power than a single feed provides): does re-feeding a stiff starter at the same 1:5:2.5 ratio each time make it progressively drier with every feed? It doesn't — the math actually runs the other way.

Each feed adds flour and water at a fixed 2:1 ratio (5 parts flour to 2.5 parts water), which is itself exactly 50% hydration. Since the added portion outweighs whatever came before it (roughly 8.5× the carryover's weight each time), every feed pulls the whole mass's hydration toward that 50% figure — not away from it. Tracked out from a 100%-hydration mother starter: first feed lands around 54.5% (matching the 50–55% figure above), a second feed at the same ratio brings it to about 50.5%, and a third lands within a rounding error of 50% flat. It converges to and settles at 50% — it can't be fed past that floor into something stiffer, no matter how many times the same ratio is repeated, because the added mix's own hydration (50%) is the limit the whole system is being pulled toward, not a moving target. Two or three feeds at 1:5:2.5 is, if anything, a way to correct a slightly-too-wet first feed toward true 50% rather than a way to accidentally over-dry it.

The flip side is worth knowing too: if a bake calls for something firmer than 50% (down toward the 45% bottom of this site's stated 45–50% range), this specific ratio can't get there by repetition — reaching a genuinely stiffer target means using a drier added ratio (more flour relative to water in the feed itself, not more feeds at the same ratio).

### Rye and whole-grain content skew the "feel" of a given hydration number

The hydration percentage math above holds regardless of flour blend, but the *handling* of a stiff starter at any given percentage isn't flour-blend-neutral. Rye has essentially no functional gluten and instead builds structure from starch and pentosan gums (see [pH, Flour, Hydration, and Temperature](/guides/ph-flour-hydration)), and those pentosans bind water that a wheat-only dough would leave free. The practical result: a rye-inclusive starter (this site's maintenance blend runs up to 30% rye) will feel firmer and drier than an all-white starter at the *same* nominal hydration percentage — the same effect the pH/Flour/Hydration guide already notes for whole-wheat dough generally (roughly 10–20% more water needed than the white-flour equivalent to reach the same workable consistency), and rye's higher pentosan content makes the effect at least as strong.

This isn't a compounding-with-feed-count problem the way the section above might suggest at a glance — it's a constant offset present from the very first feed, not something that gets worse the more times you build. The practical takeaway: don't rely on the percentage number alone to judge whether a rye-blend stiff starter has hit the target 45–50% feel. Check it by hand the way the rest of this site's ripeness cues work — firm and moldable like a dense dough ball, not crumbly-dry or stiff enough to resist working with a spatula. If a rye-blend stiff starter feels tighter than that at the nominal 50% floor described above, nudging the feed's water ratio up slightly (e.g., 1:5:3 instead of 1:5:2.5) is a reasonable first adjustment to try before assuming something else is off.

## Flours in a Starter

### For creating a new starter

See [Creating a Starter](/guides/starter/creating-a-starter) for the full rationale, but in short: whole-grain flour (wheat or rye) carries the wild yeast and bacteria that live on the grain's hull, while white flour is more easily broken down into the sugars those microbes eat. A 50/50 whole wheat/bread flour blend, or a partial-rye blend, gets a new culture established reliably — starting from 100% white flour alone has a meaningfully higher failure rate.

### For ongoing maintenance

Once established, the flour choice becomes more about flavor and vigor than survival:

- **All-purpose or bread flour** feeds the culture efficiently and keeps a starter's texture predictable — this is the bulk of most maintenance feedings.
- **A percentage of whole-grain rye** (commonly 20–30% of the feeding), even after the starter is mature, adds nutrients that help it ripen reliably on a consistent schedule and contributes a rounder, more complex flavor than an all-white maintenance routine.
- **Whole wheat** works similarly to rye if that's what's on hand, though rye tends to be the stronger performer for the same percentage.
- **Spelt and einkorn** can be used in a starter the same way, though they're less commonly the default — worth experimenting with if a specific bread calls for that flour's character to carry through into the starter itself.
- **White flour alone**, once a starter is already mature and stable, is a legitimate choice for a specific purpose: it lowers the mixture's buffering capacity, which means pH drops faster and bacterial (souring) activity gets limited sooner — in practice, a starter or levain fed purely white flour tends to produce a **milder, less sour result**. See [Starter vs. Levain](/guides/starter/starter-vs-levain) for where this comes up in practice (a white-flour levain for a delicately flavored, enriched bake).

### Two blends from two sources — why both are right

A sharp-eyed reader will notice this guide's own sources don't agree on a flour mix. The Sourdough Journey builds a starter on **50% whole wheat / 50% bread flour**; The Perfect Loaf maintains one on **70% white flour / 30% rye**. Neither is a typo, and neither author is wrong — the two blends are answers to two different questions.

**The 50/50 whole wheat blend is a creation blend.** During a starter's first two weeks, the whole job is *inoculation* — getting enough wild yeast and bacteria off the grain's hull and into the jar to establish a culture at all. Microbes ride in on the bran, so during creation you want a lot of bran, and half whole-grain flour delivers it. Nothing about that generosity is needed once the culture exists.

**The 70/30 rye blend is a maintenance blend.** A mature starter already has its population — millions of times more cells than any feeding contributes — so the flour's job shifts from *seeding* microbes to *feeding* them on a schedule. What maintenance wants is a predictable clock and a flavor you like, and a mostly-white feed with a modest whole-grain kicker delivers exactly that: the white flour is clean, efficient food; the rye is the vitamin shot that keeps the culture ripening reliably every 12 hours.

**And why rye at only 30%?** Because rye punches far above its weight. Gram for gram it carries more minerals, more ash, and more readily available sugars and enzymes than whole wheat, so it drives fermentation noticeably harder at the same percentage — 30% rye lands in roughly the same vigor territory as a much larger share of whole wheat. The two blends are closer cousins than the numbers suggest; one just uses a more concentrated ingredient. (The trade-off runs through buffering, too: more whole grain means more buffering capacity and more total acid carried at the same pH — see [pH, Flour, Hydration, and Temperature](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) — which is part of why a whole-grain-heavy maintenance feed pushes a starter tangier and faster than a mostly-white one.)

**When to reach for a different mix.** The percentages aren't sacred; they're dials:

- **Starter running sluggish** → temporarily raise the whole-grain share (or switch its whole-grain component to rye). You're feeding the culture harder until it perks up.
- **Starter peaking too fast, or tasting too sour** → lower the whole-grain share, or raise the feeding ratio. Both slow the acid clock.
- **Peak keeps missing your feeding time** → nudge the whole-grain percentage up (peaks sooner) or down (peaks later) until ripeness lands on your schedule. This is the practical reason The Perfect Loaf's 30% exists — it was tuned to hit a 12-hour cadence.
- **Baking a rye bread** → shift the starter or its levain rye-heavy, so the culture is already adapted to the flour the dough will use.
- **Baking something delicate or enriched** → build an all-white levain for that bake (see [Starter vs. Levain](/guides/starter/starter-vs-levain)); leave the maintenance blend alone.
- **Flavor preference** → rye reads rounder and earthier, whole wheat nuttier, white cleanest. The maintenance blend's whole-grain component carries a surprising amount of the finished bread's background flavor.

The meta-lesson both sources agree on completely: whole grain drives vigor, white flour is the food supply, and the right ratio between them depends on what stage your starter is at and what you're asking of it this week.
