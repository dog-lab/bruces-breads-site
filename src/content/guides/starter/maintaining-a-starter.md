*Part 2 of the Bruce's Breads Starter Guide. See also: [Creating a Starter](/guides/starter/creating-a-starter), [Starter Types and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours), and [Starter vs. Levain](/guides/starter/starter-vs-levain).*

Once a starter is established (see [Creating a Starter](/guides/starter/creating-a-starter) if you're not there yet), keeping it alive and strong is the easy part — a few minutes a day, most days, forever. This page covers the ongoing care and feeding: ratios, schedules, reading ripeness, storage, and the handful of things that go wrong along the way.

## What "Feeding" Actually Means

Feeding (also called refreshing) a starter means discarding some — not all — of the fermented mixture, then adding fresh flour and water and letting it ferment again. The portion you don't discard is called the **carryover**. You always discard *something*; skip this step and the jar just keeps growing and getting more acidic without ever refreshing the food supply.

The basic cycle, every time:

1. Stir the starter down.
2. Discard to your target carryover weight (compost it, trash it, or save it for a discard recipe once your starter is established — see the note on discard below).
3. Add fresh flour and water, mix until fully incorporated.
4. Cover loosely (gasses need to escape; nothing needs to get in) and let it rest until the next feeding.

## Feeding Ratios — and What They Actually Change

A feeding ratio is written as **carryover : flour : water**, always by weight. A few common ones:

| Ratio | What it means | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1:1 | Equal parts starter, flour, water | Fast, ready in 4–6 hours at warm room temp; more acidic sooner |
| 1:2:2 | Double the flour/water relative to starter | A common daily maintenance ratio; moderate pace |
| 1:5:5 | 5x the flour/water relative to starter | The Perfect Loaf's standard twice-daily ratio; stretches the interval to about 12 hours |
| 1:10:10 | 10x the flour/water relative to starter | Slower, less acidic; useful for rescuing an over-acidic starter (see Troubleshooting below) |

The pattern: a **higher ratio** (more flour/water relative to starter) dilutes the acid built up from the last feeding, giving you a longer, gentler rise and a milder, less sour starter. A **lower ratio** concentrates the carryover's acidity and shortens the time to peak, giving a tangier starter faster. Same starter, same flour — the ratio alone shifts both timing and flavor.

## A Reliable Maintenance Routine

The Perfect Loaf's long-running routine is a solid default for a starter you bake from regularly:

| Ingredient | Weight | Baker's % |
|---|---|---|
| White flour (all-purpose or bread flour) | 70g | 70% |
| Whole-grain rye flour | 30g | 30% |
| Water | 100g | 100% |
| Ripe starter carryover | 20g | 20% |

Fed twice a day (roughly 12 hours apart — e.g., 9am and 9pm) at this **1:5:5 ratio**, this keeps a starter strong and gives you two opportunities a day to pull some off for a levain if you're baking (see [Starter vs. Levain](/guides/starter/starter-vs-levain)). The small percentage of whole rye in every feeding — not just during creation — helps it ripen reliably on a 12-hour clock and adds a little extra vigor and flavor complexity versus an all-white-flour maintenance routine.

If you're wondering why this blend (70% white / 30% rye) looks so different from the 50/50 whole wheat mix used to *create* a starter, that's not an inconsistency — creation and maintenance are different jobs asking different things of the flour. The full reconciliation, including when to deliberately change your mix, is in [Starter Types and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours).

**Once a day instead of twice?** Fine — just leave less carryover in the jar (below the 20g used above) to stretch the ripening time out to once daily. Keep reducing the carryover until the starter consistently peaks right around your preferred single feeding time.

**Smaller starter, less waste?** Also fine — scale the whole ratio down proportionally (e.g., 10g carryover, 35g white flour, 15g rye flour, 50g water). The ratio matters more than the absolute quantity.

## Reading Ripeness

![The float test — a spoonful of ripe starter floating in water](/images/guides/04_float_test.jpg)

A **ripe** starter — ready to bake with, or to build a levain from — shows some combination of:

- Visible rise in the jar
- Bubbles on the surface and up the sides
- A sour aroma
- A looser, softer consistency than right after feeding

The classic quick check is the **float test**: drop a small spoonful of ripe starter into a glass of room-temperature water. If it floats, there's enough trapped gas to suggest it's active and ready. It's a useful quick read, but not infallible — a starter can be perfectly ripe and still sink if it's a touch past peak or on the stiffer side, so treat a fail as "double-check the other signs," not an automatic verdict. Visual and aroma cues (rise, bubbles, smell) are the more reliable primary read; the float test is a nice secondary confirmation.

Ripeness looks a little different depending on hydration — see [Starter Types and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours) for how a liquid starter's ripeness signs (loose, broken-down, aerated) differ from a stiff starter's (a collapsing dome, a soft cracked top).

### A note on pH

A starter's pH generally runs **3.5–5.0**, with **3.8–4.3** the sweet spot for peak activity and flavor in most everyday baking. (For what pH does on the *dough* side — gluten integrity, flavor balance, crust color, and how it interacts with flour and hydration — see [pH, Flour, Hydration, and Temperature](/guides/ph-flour-hydration).) Different goals call for different acidity, controlled through feeding ratio and timing rather than anything added directly:

| Goal | Target pH | How to get there |
|---|---|---|
| Mild sandwich bread | 4.5–5.0 | Frequent feedings (~12hr intervals), higher ratio (1:5:5) |
| Tangy artisan loaf | 3.8–4.2 | Longer fermentation (~24hr), lower ratio (1:2:2) |
| Rye bread | 3.8–4.2 | Same as above — also helps control rye's naturally higher enzyme activity |
| Pizza dough | 4.5–5.0 | Use a young starter, shortly after feeding |

If a starter drops below pH 3.5, it'll smell strongly of vinegar or acetone and may struggle to rise — that's over-acidified, not dead. See Troubleshooting below to bring it back.

## Storing and Reviving

**Short breaks (up to a few weeks):** the fridge is the easiest hibernation. Cold slows fermentation way down, so a starter can go 1–3 weeks between feedings in the fridge without real harm. Some clear liquid ("hooch," true alcohol this time, unlike the water separation seen in a brand-new starter — see [Creating a Starter](/guides/starter/creating-a-starter)) may collect on top if it goes a while between feedings; just pour it off, stir, and feed as usual.

**Reviving from the fridge:** take it out, let it warm on the counter for a few hours, then feed it with your usual maintenance flour. Let that ferment through the day or overnight, then feed again. Repeat for about two feedings over two days before trusting it for a bake — the fridge is a stressful environment for the culture, and it needs a couple of cycles at full warmth to bounce back to full strength.

**Does hydration change how long a fridge starter holds up?** Possibly, and the one real data point we have agrees with the old folklore that stiffer is more forgiving. A 2026 study from Vrije Universiteit Brussel followed two wholemeal wheat sourdoughs from Belgian industrial bakeries, both built on the classic *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis* and *Maudiozyma humilis* pairing (the same bacterium-and-yeast duo behind San Francisco sourdough), through months of reduced refreshment. Refreshed weekly, both held onto the pair for the month they were tracked. Refreshed only every three weeks (the far end of the 1–3 week fridge window above), the firm one (dough yield 160, which works out to roughly 60% hydration) kept both organisms stable, while the semi-liquid one (dough yield 200, or 100% hydration) lost the bacterium after 12 weeks and the yeast after 15, with other microbes moving in. Two honest caveats: the two sourdoughs came from different bakeries with different backslopping conditions, so hydration isn't cleanly isolated (the authors credit the combination of dough yield, refreshment interval, and process rather than water content alone), and an industrial 4 °C schedule isn't a home fridge. Treat it as a hedge, not a rule: if you tend to go long stretches between bakes, a stiff starter (see [Starter Types and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours)) is the better bet for staying put, though the liquid one will most likely survive a few missed weeks just fine.

**Longer breaks — dehydrating a starter:** a fully dried starter is a more durable backup than a fridge jar — shelf-stable for years, doesn't need monthly upkeep, and takes up a fraction of the space. It's worth keeping a batch of this on hand as insurance even if the fridge backup jar (see [Creating a Starter](/guides/starter/creating-a-starter)) is the day-to-day fallback.

**To dry a starter:** feed the starter at a 1:1:1 ratio (equal parts starter, flour, water) and let it ferment for about 2 hours, until visibly bubbly and active — you want it freshly fed and lively going into the drying process, not past peak or sluggish. Spread it in a thin, even layer on parchment paper or a dehydrator tray. Air-drying at room temperature can take up to **72 hours**; a dehydrator set to a low temperature with the heat off and just the fan running speeds this up considerably (the goal either way is evaporating water without cooking the culture — an oven light-only setup works on the same principle if a dehydrator isn't handy). Once it's completely dry and crisp all the way through, break it into flakes or grind it into a powder — either form works. Store it in an airtight container at room temperature.

**To revive a dehydrated starter:** mix **5–10g** of the dried starter with **20–50g** of lukewarm water, and let it sit anywhere from 30 minutes to several hours, until the pieces have fully dissolved or softened. Stir in an equal weight of flour (**20–50g**, matching whatever water amount was used) and cover the jar. Repeat this daily — discard down and feed with fresh flour and water every 24 hours — until the starter is reliably doubling in volume and bubbly again, which typically takes **3–7 days**. That's a longer runway than reviving a starter straight from the fridge (see above), since a dried starter is starting from a fully dormant state rather than just a cold, slowed-down one — budget the time accordingly rather than expecting it to bounce back overnight.

## Using (and Saving) Discard

Once a starter has passed its readiness test (see [Creating a Starter](/guides/starter/creating-a-starter)), its discard is safe to save and use rather than tossing it every time. A container in the fridge can hold discard for a couple of weeks and feed into pancakes, waffles, crackers, or any of the recipes queued in this site's own **Discards** section.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Smells strongly of vinegar or acetone, weak rise | Over-acidified (pH below ~3.5) | Discard most of it, feed at a high ratio (1:10:10) every 8–12 hours until it stabilizes back in the 3.8–4.3 range |
| Clear or brownish liquid on top | Hungry starter (true hooch) — it's been a while since the last feeding | Pour off, stir, feed as usual; if this happens often, feed more frequently or leave more carryover |
| Sluggish, slow to rise even when warm | Could be a weak feeding ratio, a cool kitchen, or chlorinated water | Check water source first (see Creating a Starter), try a slightly higher ratio or a warmer spot |
| Starter isn't falling after it peaks | Not necessarily a problem — some starters (depending on flour) plateau instead of visibly collapsing | Judge readiness by rise speed and bubble activity rather than waiting for a fall that may never come |
| Mold (classic gray, fuzzy) | Contamination | Discard the whole batch, sterilize the jar and tools, start over from Creating a Starter |
