Water shows up in a sourdough loaf's story twice, in two completely different roles. First it's an ingredient — it hydrates the flour, wakes up the starter, and sets how the dough handles and ferments. Then, the moment that same loaf hits a hot oven, its water changes jobs entirely: it turns to vapor and spends the first twenty minutes of the bake deciding how much the loaf will rise and how its crust will look. The Four Levers guide already covers hydration percentage and dough temperature in depth, because those are baked into every recipe on this site. What's left — and what this guide is actually about — is the part in between: what's dissolved in the water before it ever touches flour, and what happens to it after it leaves the dough as steam.

---

## Part 1: What's Actually in Your Tap

The most persistent myth in home sourdough is that a healthy starter needs bottled, spring, or distilled water. It doesn't. Ordinary municipal tap water bakes good bread the overwhelming majority of the time, and King Arthur Baking says as much directly — most bakers should leave their water alone and look elsewhere first when something's off. Two things in tap water are worth knowing about anyway, because when they *are* the problem, no amount of adjusting flour or fermentation time will fix it.

**Chlorine** is added to municipal water as a disinfectant, and at high enough levels it can suppress the yeast and bacteria in a starter the same way it's meant to suppress things in a water treatment plant. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple: chlorine is a gas dissolved in water, and it off-gasses on its own. Leave a container of tap water uncovered on the counter overnight and most of it is gone by morning.

**Chloramine** is the newer disinfectant many municipalities have switched to specifically *because* it doesn't dissipate the way chlorine does — that's a feature for keeping water clean all the way to the tap, and a problem for a baker who assumed the overnight-on-the-counter trick would work. It doesn't. Chloramine needs an actual intervention: a carbon filter rated for chloramine removal (a basic charcoal pitcher filter isn't necessarily enough — check that it's rated for it), reverse osmosis, or a pinch of ascorbic acid (plain vitamin C powder) stirred in, which neutralizes it chemically.

The practical diagnostic: if your starter is sluggish, smells faintly of a swimming pool, and improves after you switch to water that's sat out overnight, that was chlorine. If it stays sluggish even after the overnight sit, suspect chloramine and reach for a filter instead. This is exactly the failure mode the Troubleshooting guide's starter table already flags under "no bubble activity at all in the first week" — this is the mechanism behind that line.

## Part 2: Hard Water, Soft Water, and the Sweet Spot

Water "hardness" is a measure of dissolved calcium and magnesium, given in parts per million (ppm), and it isn't just a dishwasher-and-shower-scale concern — those same minerals interact directly with your dough.

| Hardness | Approx. ppm | Effect on dough |
|---|---|---|
| Very soft | Under 50 | Minerals feed fermentation fine, but the dough tends slack and sticky — there's no ionic support for the gluten network |
| Soft | 50–100 | Workable for most breads |
| **Ideal** | **100–150** | The sweet spot — calcium and magnesium ions cross-link gluten proteins, tightening the network, while still supplying enough minerals to feed the yeast |
| Moderately hard | 150–200 | Still fine; dough runs a little tighter |
| Hard | 200–300 | Gluten tightens further and fermentation measurably slows |
| Very hard | Over 300 | Worth filtering or cutting with soft/filtered water |

The mechanism is straightforward: calcium and magnesium are positively charged, and they shield the negatively charged sites on gluten proteins that would otherwise repel each other — the same charge-shielding trick salt plays, covered in the Salt guide. Too little and the network has no reinforcement; too much and it over-tightens, the same way an overdose of salt does.

Water pH plays a smaller supporting role — King Arthur notes a slightly acidic water (just under neutral 7) is preferred, since the alkalinity that comes with very hard, mineral-heavy water can mute yeast activity a bit further on top of the tightening effect.

**The practical hierarchy, and it matters:** if your starter is active and your loaves are coming out the way you expect, don't go looking for a water problem — you don't have one. Flour, desired dough temperature, and technique (all covered in the Four Levers guide) are doing far more work than your water's mineral content ever will. Reach for a filter or a hardness test only after you've ruled out the more common suspects, and even then, cutting hard tap water 50/50 with filtered or distilled water is usually enough correction — there's rarely a reason to go all the way to bottled.

## Part 3: Water Becomes Steam

Here's where water's second job starts. Once a shaped loaf goes into a hot oven, the water already inside the dough begins vaporizing — there's no way around that part. The only question a baker actually controls is: *how long does that vapor stick around the loaf before it escapes?* That question is what steaming is.

Steam does three things during roughly the first twenty minutes of a bake, and all three depend on keeping the dough's surface wet and flexible rather than letting it dry and set immediately:

- It **delays crust formation**, which is the whole ballgame — a crust that sets too early physically caps the loaf and shuts down oven spring before it's finished.
- It **extends how far the loaf can expand**, letting the score open cleanly into an "ear" instead of tearing or staying shut.
- It **gelatinizes the starch on the surface**, which is what produces a glossy, blistered, professional-looking crust rather than a dull, dry one.

Underneath that is a two-phase story. While the surface stays moist, sugars keep reacting slowly and browning barely happens — the loaf is still soft and pale. Once the steam is gone and the surface finally dries out, the oven's dry heat takes over and Maillard browning and caramelization happen fast. That's why flooding a loaf with steam for the entire bake backfires: you'd be holding Phase 1 open indefinitely and never getting to Phase 2. Both King Arthur Baking and The Perfect Loaf land on the same number for the handoff — steam does its job in roughly the first **twenty minutes**, after which continuing to steam only delays the crust you actually want.

**Steam early. Dry later.** If this guide has one sentence worth remembering, that's it.

## Part 4: The Methods, Compared

| Method | Where the water comes from | How it's retained | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dutch oven** | The dough's own moisture | The lid traps it — the loaf steams itself | One loaf, most forgiving |
| **Dutch oven + ice cube** | Dough + a tossed-in ice cube | Lid traps it, with a small extra boost | One loaf, slightly drier doughs |
| **Hot pan + boiling water** | Water poured in at load time | Whole oven cavity | A fast, strong burst — with real hazards |
| **Ice cubes in a hot pan** | Melting ice | Whole oven, released over ~8–10 minutes | A gentler, safer version of the above |
| **Lava rocks + water/ice** | Water flashed across a large hot surface | Whole oven, a strong burst | Multiple loaves on a stone or steel |
| **Wet towels in a pan** | Water held in cotton fibers | Whole oven, released steadily over ~20 minutes | Sustaining humidity alongside a burst method |
| **Spray bottle alone** | Misted directly on the loaf | Barely — dissipates almost immediately | A supplement only, never the whole plan |

A Dutch oven is the elegant case, and it's worth understanding *why* it works so well before moving past it: you don't add any water at all. The dough already contains plenty, that moisture evaporates the instant the dough hits the hot, covered interior, and the lid simply traps what would otherwise escape. It's a sealed system re-using its own output, which is exactly why it's the most forgiving method for a single loaf and the one that made Dutch-oven bread baking a home-kitchen staple in the first place — the technique got its real popular boost from Jim Lahey's no-knead method, which the *New York Times* ran in 2006 and which sent covered-pot bread into kitchens that had never fermented anything before.

The methods that add water directly split into fast-and-risky versus slow-and-gentle. Pouring boiling water into a screaming-hot pan makes a huge burst of steam almost instantly, but it's a genuine safety hazard — never pour it onto oven glass or a heating element, and lay a towel over the glass first if there's any chance of a stray splash, because a cold-water-on-hot-glass crack is a real and well-documented failure. Ice cubes do the same job far more gently, melting and evaporating over roughly the first 8–10 minutes instead of flashing all at once.

Lava rocks and wet towels solve the same problem from opposite directions. Lava rocks work because they're almost all surface area — spread water or ice across a pan of preheated rocks and it vaporizes nearly instantly across dozens of hot points at once, producing a strong, fast burst. Wet towels do the reverse: instead of a burst, water held in the fibers releases slowly and steadily for close to the full twenty-minute window. Used together, the rocks give you the initial punch and the towels keep the humidity from collapsing before the twenty minutes are up — a combination The Perfect Loaf specifically recommends for open-oven baking.

Spraying, on its own, is the method to be honest about: King Arthur Baking's own guidance is direct that it's "a less effective method," because the mist dissipates almost as fast as you create it and every time the oven door opens to spray again, heat and whatever steam you'd already built both escape with it. It isn't useless — a spritz on the way in adds a little extra moisture — but it can't do the job by itself.

The organizing principle behind all of this: **you're steaming the oven, not the bread.** The goal is a humid room around the loaf, not a wet loaf.

## Part 5: Steaming More Than One Loaf at a Time

This is the part that actually matters for how bread gets baked around here, because a single Dutch oven caps a bake at one loaf, and most home ovens don't comfortably fit more than two pots on a rack even if you own several.

The setup that scales is the open-bake combination from Part 4: a baking stone or steel for the loaves themselves, and a lava-rock pan (or a plain heavy pan of boiling water, if you're comfortable with the risk) on a rack below. Preheat everything together — stone, rocks, and oven — for a full hour if you can manage it, so the rocks are genuinely saturated with heat rather than just warm. Add the ice or water to the rocks right as you load the loaves, not before, so the burst lines up with the moment the dough actually needs it.

If a lava-rock burst alone isn't holding up humidity for a leaner, lower-hydration dough, or you're running enough loaves that the oven's air volume dilutes the steam more than a single-loaf bake would, add a pan of saturated cotton towels alongside the rocks on the same lower rack. The rocks supply the initial punch; the towels keep releasing steam through the rest of the twenty-minute window so the middle loaves aren't shortchanged just because they're farther from the burst.

There's a real, easy-to-overlook wrinkle specific to open baking multiple loaves: **a home oven isn't airtight.** A Dutch oven's lid seals in what little steam a single loaf produces, but an open bake is fighting the door gasket and the oven's vent the entire time, which is exactly why open methods throw so much more total steam than a spray bottle ever could — you're compensating for a leaky room, not a sealed pot. If loaves are coming out consistently pale and soft despite a properly set-up rock-and-towel pan, the fix is almost always more total steam volume (a bigger pan, more rocks, an added towel pan) rather than a more elaborate technique.

Loading speed matters more here, too. With a Dutch oven, an open door for a few extra seconds costs one loaf some heat. With an open bake, every second the door's open is heat and steam escaping for *every* loaf on the stone at once. Score everything and stage it on peels or parchment before the oven door opens at all, and get all the loaves in in one continuous motion rather than one at a time with the door swinging open and shut in between.

A last practical note on sourcing: lava rocks (the same "volcanic lava rock" sold for gas grills, not landscaping rock, which can be chemically treated) are available at garden centers and grill-supply shops. Rinse them before their first use, and expect them to slowly crumble and need replacing after a handful of bakes — they're a consumable, not a one-time purchase.

## Part 6: Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Starter shows no bubble activity at all in the first week | Chlorinated or chloraminated tap water | Let water sit uncovered overnight (fixes chlorine); if that doesn't help, switch to a carbon-filtered or bottled water for a week to test for chloramine |
| Dough unusually tight and slow to ferment despite correct hydration and dough temperature | Very hard water (over 300 ppm) | Cut tap water 50/50 with filtered or distilled water |
| Dough slack and sticky even though the recipe's hydration looks right | Very soft water (under 50 ppm, common with softened or reverse-osmosis water) | Nothing to fix chemically — compensate with slightly less water or a stronger flour |
| Loaf pale, soft, underdeveloped crust despite a steaming setup | Not enough total steam volume for the oven's leak rate, especially in a multi-loaf open bake | Add a towel pan alongside the lava rocks; extend the steamed portion a couple of minutes next time |
| Thick, leathery, tough crust | Steam held past roughly the 20-minute mark | Vent (remove the lid, or the rock/towel pans) closer to 18–20 minutes |
| Burned hand, warped pan, or a cracked oven door glass | Boiling water poured directly onto hot glass, a heating element, or too thin a pan | Switch to ice cubes for a gentler release, and lay a towel over any glass in range before pouring anything |
