Every inclusion is a small act of aggression against a perfectly good dough. Gluten is a delicate net of stretchy protein sheets, and you are about to fold olives, cheese cubes, and walnut shards into it and ask it to hold them all up for hours without tearing. Add the wrong thing at the wrong moment and the net rips, the olives sink to the bottom like they're trying to escape, and the crumb around the cheese comes out gummy and raw.

Add them right and you get the loaves people actually fight over: the olive loaf with the cheese pockets, the walnut-and-tart-fruit boule, the fougasse with the green olive eyes. This guide is about the difference between those two outcomes. It's built mostly from what the loaves on this site actually do (every recipe with mix-ins is quoted in the tables below), checked against what other bakers publish, and honest about the places where the advice you'll find online disagrees with itself.

## Part 1: What an Inclusion Actually Does to Dough

Most inclusion problems come down to four things, and they're worth keeping separate because each has a different fix.

**Weight.** Heavy pieces pull down on the gas cells around them. Too many, or pieces that are too big, and the loaf doesn't rise as high. A cheese cube the size of a die is a small anchor tied to a balloon.

**Water.** Some inclusions bring water in (olives, roasted peppers, caramelized onions, thawed fruit) and some take it out (dried fruit, chia, oats, nuts). Either way the hydration you planned on isn't the hydration you get. Wet ones loosen the dough and weaken the surrounding gluten, and thirsty ones quietly dry it out.

**Edges.** Sharp, hard pieces such as coarse salt, nut shards, and woody rosemary needles can slice through gluten sheets mechanically, especially during hard folds. This is why delicate handling matters most right after the inclusions go in.

**Chemistry.** Salt and acid from brine, sugar in dried fruit and chocolate, and the natural defenses of raw alliums all affect the dough's biology. The salt in olives counts as salt (see [Part 5](#part-5-adjusting-the-dough-around-them)), and raw garlic is actively unfriendly to fermentation (see [Part 3](#part-3-prep--before-anything-goes-in)).

Hold on to this list. Everything below is some combination of managing these four.

## Part 2: How Much — The "20% Rule" Meets the Recipes Here

You'll see a number quoted everywhere: keep inclusions to about 20% of the flour weight. It's a reasonable starting point, and it has company. *Tasting Table* suggests 20–25% of dough weight for nuts, seeds, and dried fruit, and just 2–6% for delicate things like herbs. Stretch & Folds notes that many experts cap inclusions at 20% of flour weight, then immediately says this is too general to be useful: 20% is far too much pesto or garlic, and her own cinnamon-raisin loaves exceed it.

The recipes on this site back her up. Here is every mix-in loaf and how it handles the load (inclusion weight as a percentage of total flour weight):

| Recipe | Inclusions | % of flour | Hydration | Salt |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Seed Spitters](/recipes/seed-spitters/) | Oats, chia, flax, sesame | 20% | 75% | 2.0% |
| [Dirty Martini](/recipes/dirty-martini/) | Olives, orange zest, thyme | 20% | 66% + 20 g brine | 2.0% |
| [Boo-gasse](/recipes/boo-gasse/) | Castelvetrano olives | 28% | 60% | 1.4% |
| [Craisin Crunch](/recipes/craisin-crunch/) | Toasted walnuts, craisins | 30% | 75% | 2.0% |
| [Korean Swiss Cheese](/recipes/korean-swiss-cheese/) | Gruyère, scallions, gochujang swirl | 30% mixed in, 48% with swirl | 70% | 1.6% |
| [Double Dutch](/recipes/double-dutch/) | Kalamata olives, aged Gouda | 48% | 68% | 1.6% |
| [Banana-rama](/recipes/banana-rama/) | Roasted banana peppers, provolone, caramelized onions | 48% | 72% | 1.8% |

So the working ceiling here is not 20%. Nearly half of the flour weight in olives and cheese is entirely doable, provided the dough compensates. Look at what moves as the inclusions go up: hydration goes *down* (68% for Double Dutch and 72% for Banana-rama, which the recipe notes is six points below its plain-country-loaf cousin at 78%), salt goes down when the inclusions are brined, the pieces get cut small, and everything gets dried first. The number isn't the rule. The rule is that the dough can only carry so much water and weight, and every inclusion draws from that budget.

**A useful working version:** start around 20% for anything heavy or wet, go up toward 30% if the pieces are small, dry, and the dough is strong, and treat 40%+ as a "this is the whole point of the loaf" decision that needs a lower-hydration base to match. Strongly flavored items (raw garlic, onion, herbs, anything pickled) need much less. A few percent goes a long way.

## Part 3: Prep — Before Anything Goes In

Prep matters more than technique. A well-prepped inclusion will go into a mediocre dough fine, and a wet, warm, oversized one will wreck a perfect dough. Four moves cover almost everything: **dry it, cool it, cut it small, cook the raw stuff.**

### Wet and briny (olives, roasted peppers, pickles)

Rinse if you want to cut the salt, then **pat thoroughly dry**. This site's recipes say it in different words every time ("pat thoroughly dry after chopping — excess brine loosens the dough"), because it's the single most common fix. A paper towel is the cheapest tool in the kitchen for this. Cut the pieces to roughly ¼ to ⅜ inch: [Boo-gasse](/recipes/boo-gasse/) specifies it and notes that "chunks any bigger fight you during shaping."

**Mind the color.** Dark olives bleed purple into pale dough. That's why Boo-gasse puts mild, bright-green Castelvetrano olives *in* the dough and keeps the dark Kalamatas on the *surface* as decoration only.

### Cooked aromatics (caramelized onions, roasted garlic and peppers)

Cook them all the way, **cool them completely**, and blot them. [Banana-rama](/recipes/banana-rama/) is blunt about why: warm onions folded into dough can slow the starter, and wet onions gum up gluten development at the fold and leave a slack dough that's hard to shape. They also bring water your hydration number never counted.

### Raw alliums (garlic, onion)

Skip raw garlic in the dough. In one informal kitchen test, That Sourdough Gal fermented four identical mini loaves: a control, one with roasted garlic, one with garlic powder, and one with raw minced garlic. The control finished bulk in just under 7 hours; the roasted garlic loaf finished about 5% later, the garlic powder loaf about 37% later, and the **raw garlic loaf took 172.8% longer, over 18 hours**. Her explanation is allicin, the antibacterial and antifungal compound raw garlic makes when chopped or crushed, and cooking destroys it. She's upfront that it's "no lab," so treat the numbers as a vivid demonstration and not a measurement. The practical advice is the same either way: roast, sauté, or caramelize first, and the flavor improves as a bonus (she found raw garlic harsh and bitter in the finished loaf, roasted garlic sweet).

### Cheese

Keep it **cold** right up until it goes in, and cut it into small, distinct cubes (about 1 cm). Warm cheese smears through the dough instead of staying in pockets, which [Double Dutch](/recipes/double-dutch/) calls out as costing "both texture and those nice melty pockets in the crumb." Firm, aged cheeses hold their shape; this site's loaves use aged Gouda, Gruyère, and sharp provolone for exactly that reason. If you'd rather have the cheese flavor blended into the dough than in pockets, grating gets you there (Stretch & Folds offers both options), but you give up the pockets.

### Nuts

**Toast them** (the flavor opens up and the surface dries out) and chop them roughly. [Craisin Crunch](/recipes/craisin-crunch/) notes that walnuts, like dried fruit, pull water from the dough over time, and that folding them in too early crushes them.

### Dried fruit

Dried fruit is thirsty. There are two schools of thought, and both work:

1. **Soak first.** A quick soak in warm water, tea, juice, or booze for about 10–15 minutes (or 30+ at room temperature) plumps the fruit so it doesn't steal water from your dough. This is the commonly recommended approach for raisins.
2. **Don't soak, but compensate.** Add the fruit dry and accept that it will pull water from the crumb during fermentation. [Craisin Crunch](/recipes/craisin-crunch/) takes this route and builds its 75% hydration with that in mind, then asks you to rest the loaf for at least two hours after baking because the "moisture pulled in by the walnuts and craisins" needs that long to redistribute.

Pick one and stay consistent between bakes, or you won't know why a loaf behaved differently.

### Seeds and grains

Chia and flax drink water; Stretch & Folds notes that add-ins like these pull moisture out of the bread, so plan to give the dough more liquid. [Seed Spitters](/recipes/seed-spitters/) handles it by soaking the oats in 40 g of warm water for 15 minutes first and folding the chia and flax in dry, and it bakes at 75% hydration to leave room for them.

### Herbs

Mince fine, and keep it light: 2–6% is *Tasting Table*'s range for herbs, and the hardy ones (rosemary) need the finest chop of all, since coarse needles act like tiny blades. Delicate herbs like thyme bruise easily, so [Dirty Martini](/recipes/dirty-martini/) folds them in by hand and keeps them out of the mixer entirely.

## Part 4: When and How They Go In

Here's where the internet disagrees with itself, so it helps to know the options and what's behind each.

**With the salt, for tiny dry things.** Stretch & Folds mixes small, dry add-ins (sesame, poppy, chia, sunflower seeds) into the dough around 30 minutes after the autolyse, when the salt goes in. They're small enough not to hurt the gluten.

**During the second (or second-and-third) stretch-and-fold.** This is the most common advice and the pattern this site's recipes follow. *Tasting Table* recommends the second or third set of folds, because by then the gluten network is strong enough to hold the pieces and there are still a couple of folds left to distribute them evenly without overworking the dough. That's about an hour into bulk if you're folding every 30 minutes, which is exactly what [Craisin Crunch](/recipes/craisin-crunch/) says: fold the walnuts and craisins in at the second fold, "once the dough has some structure to hold onto them."

**By lamination, for wet, heavy, or large pieces.** (Not the croissant kind, though the name is shared. This one is just stretching the dough into a thin sheet, scattering, and folding.) Wet the counter lightly, stretch the dough into a large thin rectangle, scatter the inclusions over it, and fold or roll it back up. You get many thin layers of dough and filling instead of a lumpy handful pushed into the middle. You Knead Sourdough layers the inclusions gradually: about 70% across the stretched sheet, another ~20% at the first fold, the rest at the end, with a gentle hand throughout. After lamination, **stop doing aggressive stretch-and-folds**. Switch to gentle coil folds if the dough needs support, because hard folds will tear the layers you just built.

**At shaping, for swirls and fillings.** Some things don't get folded in at all. [Korean Swiss Cheese](/recipes/korean-swiss-cheese/) spreads the gochujang across the dough at shaping and rolls it up, and [Pain au Chocolat](/recipes/pain-au-chocolat/) (true laminated dough, the croissant kind) rolls the chocolate batons inside each pastry. The filling stays out of the fermenting dough entirely, which is why a swirl can be wetter and heavier than any mixed-in inclusion.

**What if the dough is already full of gas?** Resist adding inclusions after bulk is done. *Tasting Table* warns that folding them in after fermentation can deflate the dough and hurt the rise. Lamination before shaping is the exception, since it's gentle, but that's another reason to keep it gentle.

### By hand or by machine?

If you use a mixer, the rule on this site is: **sturdy things may take a brief, low-speed spin; everything else goes in by hand.** Olives and zest are fine (Dirty Martini, Double Dutch). Cheese is not, because machine friction warms and smears it. Thyme, caramelized onions, soaked oats, and seeds are all hand-fold-only. When in doubt, hand-fold; it costs five minutes.

## Part 5: Adjusting the Dough Around Them

An inclusion-heavy dough isn't your regular dough plus a handful of things. The base needs to change.

**Hydration goes down.** If the inclusions are wet or briny, lower the base hydration by a few points. Banana-rama's base runs at 72% against 78% for a plain country loaf, and Double Dutch's recipe explains why: "olives and cheese both bring their own moisture and fat to the party — a wetter base dough gets sloppy fast once they're folded in." If the inclusions are thirsty instead (dried fruit, chia, oats), the dough needs more water, soaked in or added. See [pH, Flour, Hydration, and Temperature](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) for what hydration does to structure.

**Salt goes down for briny additions.** Olives and olive brine are salt. Double Dutch trims salt to 1.6% and Boo-gasse to 1.4%, instead of the usual 2%, and both recipes tell you to taste your olives before you commit, because brands vary a lot. [Salt](/guides/salt-timing-and-gluten) explains what salt does to gluten and fermentation, which is why it matters here.

**Read the dough, not the clock.** Inclusions dampen the rise. Craisin Crunch targets a bulk rise of roughly 40–60% rather than the closer-to-100% you'd see in a plain loaf at the same temperature, partly because the ancient grains and the inclusions both slow things. Aim for the dough to feel airy and jiggly and to hold together when you fold it. The rise percentage is a guide, and strength is the real signal.

**Be gentle at shaping.** Cheese cubes and big olive pieces create weak spots that can tear if you're aggressive. Double Dutch says to be "a little gentler than usual." Some pieces falling out during shaping is normal; tuck them back in.

**Watch the surface.** Anything sitting on the crust (sesame coating, olives pushed into a fougasse, exposed fruit) browns or burns faster than the dough around it. Seed Spitters warns that its sesame crust "toasts and darkens quickly" and to watch the final minutes.

**Cool completely before slicing.** Inclusions hold moisture and heat, so cutting early gives you a gummy crumb and smeared cheese. Craisin Crunch asks for a full two hours. That's the single easiest fix for "the middle was raw."

## Part 6: Troubleshooting Inclusions

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Everything sank to the bottom of the loaf | Dough was too slack when the pieces went in, or they weren't spread evenly across several folds | Wait until the dough has real structure (around the second fold), then fold more gently but more often; keep pieces small and dry |
| Dough went slack and soupy after folding in | Wet inclusions added their water on top of full hydration | Pat dry, cut hydration a few points next time, and add the inclusions in smaller batches |
| Dough tore during folds or shaping | Inclusions went in too early, pieces were too big, or the handling was too rough | Add them after gluten has built, cut smaller, switch to coil folds afterward, and shape gently |
| Gummy, raw-looking pockets around the inclusion | Excess surface moisture dissolved the nearby gluten, or the loaf was cut hot | Dry the inclusions thoroughly and cool the loaf fully (at least 2 hours for heavy loaves) |
| Cheese smeared through the dough instead of staying in pockets | Cheese was warm, handled by machine, or shredded | Keep it cold, cut it into cubes, and fold it in by hand |
| Dough turned purple or gray | Dark olives or other pigmented pieces bled into the dough | Use mild, light-colored olives inside the dough and keep dark ones as surface decoration only |
| Dough stiff, dry, or tight after adding fruit or seeds | Thirsty inclusions pulled water out of the dough | Soak the fruit or oats first, or increase the hydration a few points |
| Fermentation crawled to a stop | Raw garlic or onion, or too much salt from brine | Roast or sauté aromatics first, and trim salt to account for brined inclusions |
| Fruit or sesame burned on the crust | Surface inclusions brown faster than the loaf | Tuck pieces into the dough, tent loosely with foil in the last few minutes, or bake slightly cooler |
| Score tore raggedly on a loaf with nuts or seeds | The blade snagged on hard pieces right under the surface | Use kitchen scissors for heavily studded doughs (see [Troubleshooting](/guides/troubleshooting)) |

## Part 7: Try It Yourself

The fastest way to learn this is to change one thing at a time. Using an aliquot jar or a small test piece of dough, try:

1. **The soak test.** Split one dough in half and fold soaked dried fruit into one half and dry fruit into the other. Note the dough's feel at the fold, the rise, and the crumb. (Log it in the [Baking Log](/resources/baking-log).)
2. **The garlic test.** This one is surprisingly fun and fully repeatable: three tiny pieces of dough, each with a little roasted, raw, or no garlic. Time how long each takes to reach the same rise and see whether your results match the 5% and 170% slowdowns above.
3. **The temperature test.** Fold in cheese straight from the fridge into one half and room-temperature cheese into the other. Look at the crumb.
4. **The timing test.** Fold the same inclusions in at the first fold, the second fold, and at shaping on three small batches, and compare the tear and rise.

Change one variable at a time, write down what actually happened, and your instincts will sharpen faster than any guide can manage.
