Salt is the ingredient everyone measures and nobody thinks about. It's typically 2% of the flour weight — a couple of teaspoons in a loaf — and yet it touches all four of the levers in the four-levers guide: how fast the flour drinks its water, how strong the gluten network gets, how long that network survives fermentation, and how fast the fermentation itself runs. It also, incidentally, makes bread taste like bread.

This guide covers what salt actually does, the honest answer to the internet's favorite question (*does adding salt later really improve gluten development?*), the famous keep-salt-away-from-yeast rule and when it matters, and how the recipes on this site put all of it into practice.

---

## Part 1: The Four Things Salt Does

### 1. It slows the flour's drink

Salt competes with the flour's proteins (glutenin and gliadin) for water, so a salted dough hydrates more slowly and comes together later than an unsalted one. This is the entire reason a classic **autolyse** — that rest of just flour and water before anything else joins — traditionally excludes salt: with no salt competing for water, the proteins hydrate freely and begin bonding into gluten on their own, no kneading required.

### 2. It tightens the network

Once dissolved in the dough, sodium and chloride ions shield the charged sites on gluten proteins that would otherwise repel each other, letting the strands pack and bond more tightly. The dough becomes noticeably more elastic, less sticky, and less slack. Important nuance: this is a *strength* effect, not a *quantity* effect — salt doesn't create more gluten, it makes the gluten you have hold hands harder. It happens whenever the salt goes in.

### 3. It protects gluten over the long haul

Flour carries its own protease enzymes — protein scissors that slowly snip gluten apart, and that acid activates further as fermentation proceeds (four-levers guide, Part 1). Salt suppresses protease activity. That makes salt *protective* of your gluten network across a long fermentation — and it's the mechanism that puts a hard ceiling on the "add salt later" idea. Withhold salt through hours of bulk and you're not banking extra gluten development; you're leaving the network exposed to extra demolition.

### 4. It taps the brakes on fermentation — gently

Dissolved salt exerts osmotic pressure on yeast cells, and bakers have long used it as a fermentation-rate control. Honesty requires a caveat here, because this effect is routinely oversold: when Greg Blonder actually measured CO₂ output, yeast in solutions at bread-typical salt concentrations produced essentially the same gas as yeast in plain water. At the standard 2%, the braking effect on a bread-length ferment is real but mild; it grows with concentration (a 3% Neapolitan-style dough ferments noticeably slower than a saltless one). Treat salt as a fine-adjustment knob for fermentation, not a hand brake — temperature is the hand brake.

## Part 2: So When Should the Salt Go In?

The short answer: **a short delay captures nearly all of the benefit, and a long delay starts costing you.**

Mechanisms 1 and 2 argue for delay — let the proteins hydrate and start building unimpeded, then tighten the result. Mechanism 3 argues against delaying *long* — an unsalted dough is an unprotected dough. The crossover point lands right where a standard autolyse already sits: **20 minutes to a couple of hours** of flour-and-water rest, then salt joins. Pushing the delay further trades a diminishing hydration benefit against a growing enzyme-degradation risk. There is no secret extra gluten waiting at hour four.

And here's the part the more dramatic corners of the internet skip: **the finished-loaf difference is modest either way.** Blonder's side-by-side bakes — salt in from the start versus salt after gluten development — produced comparably shaped, comparably structured loaves; his cheerful conclusion was that you can add salt "whenever you prefer." Where timing genuinely, visibly matters is *before* the oven:

- **Mixing workability** — an unsalted autolysed dough hydrates faster and develops with less effort; salt-from-the-start doughs just need a few more minutes of mixing to arrive at the same place.
- **Fermentation pacing** — mild, but real, and cumulative over a long bulk.

So: salt timing is a real lever, a bounded one, and a forgiving one. Use the autolyse-then-salt pattern because it makes the dough nicer to work with — not because the loaf is doomed without it.

## Part 3: The Salt-and-Yeast Rule — Myth-Adjacent, Occasionally True

Plenty of yeasted-bread methods (Paul Hollywood's among them) insist that salt and yeast go into the bowl on opposite sides, never touching. There's a kernel of truth: a granule of **dry commercial yeast** sitting in direct contact with a salt crystal in a still, barely-moist environment experiences a sharp local osmotic gradient that can dehydrate cells at the contact point before mixing disperses everything. Cheap to avoid, so bakers avoid it.

For a **sourdough starter, the precaution is moot.** Your wild yeast and bacteria live inside a fully hydrated flour matrix — there is no bare-cell-against-bare-crystal moment to protect against. Once mixing starts and salt dissolves into the whole dough, every microbe experiences the same mild ~2% environment, which — per Part 1 — they handle just fine.

The one place it still applies on this site: a couple of the enriched base doughs call for optional granulated "insurance" yeast alongside the starter. Keeping *that* pinch out of direct contact with the salt pile before mixing begins is a reasonable, costless habit — even though it does nothing for the starter itself.

## Part 4: How the Recipes Here Handle It

The site's two dough families apply the same science two different ways, and it's a nice illustration of why there's no single "correct" salt timing:

**Lean loaves** (The Classics and their relatives) follow the bounded-delay pattern exactly: autolyse with flour and water only, work in the ripe starter, **salt goes in last**. The dough gets its free head start on hydration and gluten bonding, and the salt arrives well before proteases could make any mischief.

**Enriched and laminated doughs** (the brioche-family and croissant-family bakes) skip the separate autolyse entirely — flour, sugar, and salt are combined from the first moment, and gluten is built through extended mechanical mixing instead. With butter, eggs, and sugar already slowing everything down, a salt-timing refinement would add a step for an effect that's modest at the finish line (Part 2), so these doughs deliberately keep it simple. The mixer pays the difference.

## Part 5: How Much, and Which Salt

**How much: 1.8–2.2% of flour weight, and weigh it.** The 2% convention isn't arbitrary — below about 1.5% bread tastes flat and the dough runs noticeably faster and slacker; push past 3% and flavor turns salty while fermentation genuinely drags. This site's recipes state salt in grams and baker's percentage; trust the gram figure, because salt *volume* is wildly inconsistent (a teaspoon of fine sea salt weighs roughly twice what a teaspoon of flaky Diamond Crystal kosher does).

**Which salt: almost any, by weight.** Fine sea salt dissolves quickest and is the site default. Coarse or flaky salts work fine if given a moment to dissolve (adding them with a splash of the reserved water helps). Iodized table salt is functionally fine for fermentation at bread concentrations; some bakers detect a faint sharpness in flavor, so it's the last choice rather than a forbidden one. Save the beautiful crunchy finishing salts for the top of focaccia, where they can actually be noticed.

## Part 6: The Loaf That Teaches the Lesson

Forget the salt once — everyone eventually does — and you'll watch every mechanism in Part 1 run in reverse: the dough hydrates fast but stays slack and sticky, ferments ahead of schedule, over-relaxes, and bakes up pale (fast fermentation exhausts the sugars that would have browned the crust) with a strangely flat, cardboard-adjacent flavor.

**The rescue, if you catch it in early bulk:** dissolve the missing salt in a tablespoon or two of water, dimple it into the dough, and work it through with a round of folds — the dough will visibly tighten within minutes as mechanism 2 kicks in. Carry on with a watchful eye on the faster clock.

And if you don't catch it — you've accidentally baked a piece of history. Tuscany's traditional *pane sciocco* ("foolish bread") has been made saltless on purpose for centuries, a habit folklore attributes to a medieval salt tax and Tuscans attribute to the fact that it's eaten under salty prosciutto and ribollita, where a salted crumb would be redundant. Dante, exiled from Florence, wrote of learning "how salty is the taste of another man's bread" — he meant the loneliness, but he also meant the bread.

| Symptom | Salt story | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dough slack, sticky, fermenting fast; pale bland loaf | Salt forgotten | Dissolve and fold in during early bulk; shorten bulk |
| Dough tight, slow, reluctant to rise | Overdosed (3%+), or salt added to a stiff underhydrated mix | Check the math; balance with the full water amount; extend bulk |
| Salt crunch pockets in the crumb | Coarse salt added late and dry | Dissolve late additions in reserved water first |
| Bland loaf at a correct 2% | Under-fermented — flavor is fermentation's job, salt just frames it | Extend bulk; see the four-levers guide |
