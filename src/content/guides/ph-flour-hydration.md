Almost every question a sourdough baker asks — why is my crumb tight, why did this loaf spread instead of rising, why isn't it sour enough, why is the inside gummy — traces back to four adjustable variables: how acidic the dough is, what flour it's built from, how much water it's carrying, and how warm it is while it ferments. The first three are set at the recipe level; the fourth is set (and reset) every hour of the bake, which makes it easy to underestimate and arguably the most powerful of the lot.

The useful way to think about them is as four different jobs:

- **Flour** sets your structure budget — how much gluten you have to spend.
- **Hydration** decides how far you can push that structure toward an open crumb before it gives out.
- **pH** is the clock face — it tells you how much of your fermentation window you've used, with flavor and gluten integrity moving in opposite directions the whole time.
- **Temperature** is the throttle — it sets how fast that clock actually runs, and quietly tilts the flavor balance while it's at it.

Change one and you've quietly changed the other three. That's the part most guides skip, so there's a dedicated section on it further down.

(There's arguably a fifth lever hiding in plain sight — salt, which touches all four of these at once. It gets its own guide: [Salt: The Quiet Fifth Lever](/guides/salt-timing-and-gluten).)

## Part 1: pH — The Clock

### What's actually happening

A freshly mixed dough sits somewhere around pH 6.0–6.5, essentially neutral. As the lactic acid bacteria (LAB) in your starter work through the flour's available sugars, they produce lactic and acetic acid, and that number falls — through the 5s, into the 4s, and down toward 3.5 in a fully mature or overproofed dough.

That single falling number drives a surprising number of things at once.

### Yeast slows down, bacteria speed up

Wild yeast (*Saccharomyces* and relatives) is happiest around **pH 5.0–6.0**. As dough drops below roughly **4.5**, yeast activity gets measurably inhibited — the organic acids interfere with it directly. LAB, meanwhile, are perfectly content down in the 3s.

The practical consequence: **a dough that's gotten very acidic rises more slowly**, because the organism producing your CO₂ is being suppressed by the byproducts of the organism producing your flavor. This is exactly why commercial and semi-commercial bakers sometimes spike a very acidic dough with a pinch of instant yeast (around 0.2% of flour weight) — not because the sourdough is weak, but because the acid environment has outrun the wild yeast. (Worth noting: the site's enriched and laminated bases all carry an optional insurance instant yeast for related reasons — heavily enriched and laminated doughs ferment sluggishly for their own separate reasons, but the logic of "give the yeast some help when conditions are stacked against it" is the same.)

### Gluten gets more extensible, then falls apart

Acid activates proteases — both the flour's own enzymes and bacterial ones — and those enzymes break down gluten proteins. Early on this is helpful: mild acidification tightens and strengthens the network. Past roughly pH 4.0, and especially with extended time there, it turns destructive. Lab work on acidified doughs found the protein network visibly more open and disrupted, with dramatically different mechanical behavior than an unacidified control.

The characteristic signature of an over-acidified dough is **extensible but not elastic** — it stretches easily and doesn't spring back. Practically, that's a dough that goes slack, spreads sideways on the peel instead of holding a dome, and gives you a flat loaf with a pale, unstructured crumb. This is what "overproofed" actually means at the molecular level, and it's why a very long cold retard needs real gluten strength banked up front to survive the extra hours of acid exposure.

### Flavor: lactic vs. acetic

The two acids taste different, and which one dominates is something you can steer:

| Acid | Tastes like | Favored by |
|---|---|---|
| **Lactic** | Mild, rounded, yogurt-like | Warmer temperatures, wetter (higher-hydration) starters, frequent feedings |
| **Acetic** | Sharp, vinegary, aggressive | Cooler temperatures, stiffer (lower-hydration) starters, longer intervals between feedings |

Rough flavor calibration by dough pH: around **4.5–5.0** reads as mild and bready, **3.7–4.2** is the balanced, recognizably-sourdough tang most people are after, and below about **3.7** starts tasting genuinely sharp — usually alongside the structural problems described above, since the two arrive together.

### Crust color and shelf life

Acidity **slows Maillard browning**, so a very sour dough browns a little less readily at the same time and temperature (it also produces less acrylamide as a side effect, which is a small nutritional plus). And acidification meaningfully **extends shelf life** — organic acids inhibit mold and delay starch retrogradation, which is a large part of why a sourdough loaf stays pleasant on day three when a straight-yeast loaf has gone stale and dusty.

### Target pH by bread style

Different breads want different acidity, and you control it through feeding ratio and fermentation time on the starter side:

| Bread style | Target starter pH | How to get there |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild sandwich bread** | 4.5–5.0 | Frequent feedings (12-hour intervals), high ratios (1:5:5) |
| **Tangy artisan loaf** | 3.8–4.2 | Longer fermentation (~24 hours), lower ratios (1:2:2) |
| **Rye bread** | 3.8–4.2 | Same as above — but here it's about enzyme control, not just flavor (see Part 2) |
| **Pizza dough** | 4.5–5.0 | Young starter, used shortly after feeding |

A starter that's drifted **below 3.5** will smell strongly of vinegar or acetone and struggle to rise. The fix is dilution, not rescue: discard most of it and feed at a high ratio (1:10:10) every 8–12 hours until it climbs back into range. See [Maintaining a Starter](/guides/starter/maintaining-a-starter).

## Part 2: Flour — The Structure Budget

Flour brings three things to the argument: protein (gluten potential), ash and bran (minerals, fiber, buffering), and enzymes. (This part covers the mechanisms; for the flour-by-flour shopping and blending reference — spelt, einkorn, durum, blend ratios, whole-grain timing — see [Flour Types and Blending](/guides/flour-types-and-blending).)

### Protein content

| Flour | Typical protein | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| **All-purpose** | 10–12% (brand-dependent) | Softer network, fine for moderate hydration, limited at the wet end |
| **Bread flour** | 12–14% | Strong network, tolerates high hydration and long ferments, best gas retention |
| **Whole wheat** | 13–15% (but see below) | Protein number is misleading — bran dilutes and physically cuts the gluten |
| **Rye** | Low functional gluten | Cannot form an elastic network at all |

Whole wheat is the trap here. On paper it out-proteins bread flour, but the bran's sharp particles physically abrade gluten strands as the dough is worked, and the bran itself takes up space in the matrix. A 100% whole wheat dough behaves like a much weaker flour than its protein number suggests.

Rye is the extreme case: it has almost no gliadin/glutenin, so it can't build an elastic network at all. Rye breads get their structure from starch gelatinization and pentosan gums instead, which is why they're dense and why a 100% rye loaf is a fundamentally different craft from a wheat loaf rather than a variation on it.

### Ash, bran, and buffering

Whole-grain flours carry bran and germ, and that has two consequences that matter more than most bakers realize:

**They feed the culture harder.** More minerals and nutrients mean faster microbial activity — a whole-grain dough ferments meaningfully quicker than a white one at the same temperature. This is why the site's recipe spec drops DDT to 75°F for whole-grain-heavy doughs rather than the usual 75–78°F: you're compensating for a faster clock with a cooler start.

**They buffer the acid.** This one is genuinely counterintuitive. Whole-grain flour resists pH change — so a whole-grain dough can hold a *lot* more total acid (higher titratable acidity) while showing the same pH reading as a white dough. Two consequences: a whole-grain loaf at pH 4.0 tastes milder than a white loaf at pH 4.0, and pH alone is a less reliable proofing gauge on whole-grain doughs than it is on white ones.

Bran also absorbs water — a lot of it — which is why whole-grain doughs want **10–20% more water** than the same recipe in white flour, and why a proper autolyse matters more here than anywhere else: the fiber needs time to actually take up that water rather than pulling it out of the gluten later.

### Enzymes, and rye's narrow window

High-extraction and rye flours carry far more active amylase (starch-breaking) and pentosanase (gum-breaking) than white flour. Both are **pH-sensitive**, and they sit at opposite ends of the range:

- **Amylase** peaks around **pH 5.5–4.5**. If a rye dough is insufficiently acidic, amylase keeps digesting starch right into the oven — producing gummy crumb and the collapsed-top "flying roof" failure.
- **Pentosanase** peaks around **pH 4.0–3.5**. If the dough is *too* acidic, this enzyme breaks down the very pentosans rye depends on for structure, and the loaf loses volume.

So rye bakers aim at a narrow **pH 4.0–4.5** — threading between two different enzymatic failure modes. Wheat bakers have a much wider comfortable range, because bread and AP flours have low enough enzyme activity that acidity mostly affects gluten proteases rather than starch.

### Bran particle size

A smaller detail worth knowing: **finely milled bran absorbs more water and encourages more starch retrogradation** (firmer crumb after a day or two), while coarser bran absorbs less and stales slightly softer. If a whole-wheat dough feels tight, the fix is more water plus a longer rest — or blending in some white flour or vital wheat gluten for structural insurance.

## Part 3: Hydration — How Far You Can Push

Hydration is water as a percentage of flour weight. It's the lever with the most obvious effect on how the dough *feels* and the most direct effect on crumb.

| Hydration | Handling | Crumb | Who it's for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **60–65%** | Stiff, easy to shape, holds form reliably | Tight, even, close grain; modest oven spring | Beginners; sandwich loaves; anything needing clean slices |
| **70–75%** | Moderately soft, forgiving | Medium-open, good balance | The workhorse range — most of the site's recipes live here |
| **80–85%** | Slack, sticky, needs stretch-and-folds rather than kneading | Open, irregular holes, moist and custardy | The classic artisan look — needs strong flour and confidence |
| **90%+** | Nearly batter-like; only autolyse and folding, no kneading | Most open possible, if it holds | Experienced hands, strong flour, and tolerance for failure |

### Why more water opens the crumb

Water does three things at once. It makes gluten more **extensible** (stretchy) though somewhat less elastic, which lets gas cells expand rather than resist. It produces **larger gas cells** during fermentation. And it produces **more steam** in the oven, which drives oven spring directly. Stack those and you get the big irregular holes people chase.

The cost is margin. A wet dough has less structural reserve, so it hits the "too acidic to hold shape" point sooner and punishes overproofing much more harshly. Above about 80%, dough is too dilute to build strength through mixing — you have to develop it through time and folds instead, which means the fermentation window and the strength-building window are the same window.

### Baking adjustments

Wet doughs need **longer bakes** — there's simply more water to drive off before the crumb sets — and they tend to produce **thicker, chewier crusts** as a result. The usual approach is a hot covered start (Dutch oven at 450–475°F, per the site's standard) for 10–15 minutes to trap steam and hold the shape, then uncovered and often at a slightly reduced temperature so the crust doesn't burn while the interior finishes. Low-hydration doughs can be baked faster and will brown sooner.

## Part 4: Temperature — The Throttle

The first three levers describe what the dough *is*. Temperature decides how fast all of it *happens* — every enzymatic and microbial process on this page runs faster warm and slower cold. That's why it deserves equal billing rather than a footnote: you can set pH, flour, and hydration perfectly and still ruin the loaf by fermenting at the wrong temperature, because the schedule those first three implied no longer exists.

### Desired dough temperature (DDT)

Serious recipes don't say "let it rise somewhere warm" — they target a dough temperature, because the dough's own temperature (not the room's) is what the microbes experience. This site's standard is **75–78°F, dropping to 75°F for whole-grain-heavy doughs** (whole grain ferments faster at the same temperature — see Part 2 — so the cooler target compensates).

The practical control is **water temperature**, since it's the one ingredient whose temperature you can change instantly. Flour sits at room temperature, your kitchen is whatever it is, and mixing adds a little friction heat — so on a cold morning you mix with warm water, and on a hot afternoon with cold. Measure the dough right after mixing; if it missed the target, adjust where it ferments (a cooler counter, a turned-off oven with the light on) rather than shrugging.

### Temperature steers flavor, not just speed

The lactic/acetic table in Part 1 has temperature written all over it: **warmer fermentation favors mild, yogurty lactic acid; cooler fermentation favors sharp, vinegary acetic acid**. A dough that spends its life at 78°F and one that spends a night at 40°F can land at similar pH readings with noticeably different flavor characters. This is most of what a **cold retard** is for — at refrigerator temperatures (38–40°F) yeast activity nearly stops while acid production creeps along, buying flavor development without proportional gas production. The toll is time under acid: a long retard needs gluten strength banked up front (Part 1) to survive the extra hours of proteolysis.

### Ending bulk: pair temperature with percent rise

None of the first three levers tells you *when* to end bulk fermentation — temperature does. Tom Cucuzza's two-factor method pairs dough temperature with a target percent rise rather than a clock:

| Dough temperature | Target % rise | Planning window |
|---|---|---|
| 80°F / 27°C | 30%+ | 3.5–5.5 hours |
| 75°F / 24°C | 50% | 5–7 hours |
| 70°F / 21°C | 75% | 8–12 hours |
| 65°F / 18°C | 100% | 12–16 hours |

*(Assumes a bread-flour dough and an 8–16 hour cold retard at 38–40°F.)*

The reason this method works despite everything else on this page is that **percent rise absorbs hydration, starter percentage, and salt variability automatically** — those all shift fermentation speed, but they shift the rise you observe right along with it. Flour is the exception worth adjusting for: high-protein flours show the rise more visibly (target a bit higher), while low-gluten flours like einkorn and spelt show less (target lower). Full detail in Tom Cucuzza's percent-rise system, cited in Sources below.

### The ceiling

Above roughly **90°F, gluten begins breaking down outright regardless of pH** — at that point you've left the region where any of these levers behaves predictably. Warm is a throttle; hot is a wall.

## Part 5: How the Four Interact

This is where it gets interesting, because the effects are not additive.

**Hydration dilutes acid, so it slows the pH clock.** A 90% dough and a 60% dough fermented for the same time at the same temperature won't land at the same pH — the wetter one might finish bulk around 4.2 while the stiffer one hits 3.8. The wet dough also has more oven spring in reserve to compensate for whatever gluten weakening did occur. So "high hydration overproofs faster" is true in terms of *structural consequences* but not in terms of *acid accumulation* — the wet dough is less acidic but has less structure to lose.

**Whole grain buffers acid, so pH understates the sourness.** As covered above, a whole-grain dough at pH 4.0 is carrying far more total acid than a white dough at the same reading. If you're using a pH meter as a proofing gauge, it needs separate calibration per flour blend — a number that means "ready" on white flour means something different on a 40% whole wheat.

**Strong flour buys you permission for the other two.** A low-pH dough at high hydration is a disaster in AP flour and a triumph in good bread flour — the acid softens the network either way, but only the strong flour has enough left to hold the shape. If you want to run wet *and* long, the flour is where you pay for it. Conversely, a whole-grain dough at high hydration is fighting on two fronts: extremely slack, buffered so its pH reading misleads you, and fermenting fast from all those extra minerals. That combination is why 85% is a sensible practical ceiling for whole-wheat-heavy doughs at home.

**Temperature multiplies all of the above.** Every interaction on this page happens faster warm and slower cold — which means temperature errors don't add to the other levers, they *scale* them. A whole-grain dough (fast clock) mixed warm (faster still) can blow through its fermentation window before a recipe written for white flour at 75°F says you're halfway done. This is also why temperature is the first thing to check when a familiar recipe suddenly misbehaves: the flour, water, and starter probably didn't change between March and August, but the kitchen did.

## Part 6: Troubleshooting by Lever

| Symptom | Likely lever | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dough spreads flat, won't hold a dome | pH too low (overproofed) and/or hydration too high for the flour | Shorten bulk; step up to bread flour; drop hydration 5% |
| Loaf is dense with a tight crumb | Hydration too low, or underproofed | Add water; extend bulk; check dough temp against the rise table |
| Crumb is gummy | Under-baked, or enzyme activity (especially rye/whole grain at too-high pH) | Bake longer; for rye, use a more sour levain to bring pH toward 4.0 |
| Bread isn't sour enough | pH hasn't dropped far enough | Extend bulk; cooler ferment for acetic sharpness; stiffer starter; add whole grain |
| Bread is too sour | pH dropped too far | Shorten bulk; feed starter more frequently at higher ratios; warmer ferment favors milder lactic |
| Dough is unmanageably sticky | Hydration too high for the flour's strength | Reduce water; longer autolyse; more folds; blend in 20–30% white flour if whole-grain-heavy |
| Rye loaf collapsed on top | Insufficient acidity — amylase ran wild | Target pH 4.0–4.5 with a properly sour rye levain |
| Whole-wheat dough feels tight and dry | Bran hasn't finished absorbing | More water (10–20% over the white-flour equivalent); longer autolyse |
| Same recipe behaves differently every bake | Temperature drifting with the seasons | Measure dough temp after mixing; hit DDT with water temperature; judge bulk by % rise, not the clock |
| Bulk "finished" hours early or late | Dough temp missed the target | Check actual dough temp against the Part 4 table — the planning window moves 2–3 hours per 5°F |

## Part 7: Try It Yourself

Four experiments that make these effects visible in a weekend, all of which need nothing beyond what's already in the kitchen:

**1. The hydration ladder.** One flour (bread flour), three doughs at 60%, 75%, and 90% hydration. Identical starter, salt, time, and temperature. Note how each one handles, then photograph the crumb of each slice side by side. You'll see the gas cell size scale directly with the water.

**2. The flour swap.** Two doughs at 70% hydration, one 100% AP and one 100% whole wheat, everything else identical. Watch how differently they hydrate during mixing, test the windowpane on each, and compare final volume. The whole-wheat dough will want more water and more time, and will still come out denser — that's the bran's effect, made visible.

**3. The acid spike.** Two identical doughs at 75%; add a small amount of vinegar or lemon juice to one to drop its pH about half a unit. Same temperature, same time. The acidified dough should rise more slowly (yeast inhibition) and brown slightly less in the oven (suppressed Maillard). This one demonstrates two separate effects in a single bake.

**4. The temperature fork.** Mix one dough, split it in half, and ferment one half around 78°F and the other around 68°F — same target percent rise for both, per the Part 4 table, which means very different clock times. Bake both and taste side by side: the warm half should read milder and more lactic, the cool half sharper and more acetic, from doughs that were identical an hour after mixing. Nothing demonstrates the throttle more directly.

In all four: change exactly one variable, keep a written record, and use the same starter for every dough. Small-batch testing is the only way to account for the fact that flour lots, ambient humidity, and starter vigor all drift. The [Baking Log](/resources/baking-log) template is built for exactly this kind of record-keeping.
