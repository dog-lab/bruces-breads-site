## Step-by-Step Tutorial Script

### 1. Feed your levain, the day before

Feed your stiff starter (see the [Starter Guide: Starter Types & Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours/)) so it peaks roughly 8–12 hours before you plan to mix. A stiff starter takes longer to ripen than a loose one, so plan for an overnight feed if mixing in the morning, or a morning feed for an evening mix. Look for the dome to stop rising and just begin to flatten and crack slightly — not the loose, bubbly surface of a liquid starter. If you're tracking pH, ripe stiff starter for this recipe typically reads somewhere around **pH 4.0–4.5**.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_03_starter_at_peak.jpg" alt="A jar of stiff sourdough starter at peak ripeness" /></figure>

### 2. Mixing

Brioche needs its gluten fully developed *before* the butter goes in — this is a stand mixer or spiral mixer job, not a hand-kneading one. Hand-developing full structure in a dough this soft, and then working in 250g of butter by hand afterward, is possible but a genuine arm workout.

**Manual (hand-mixing)**

- Tear the ripe stiff starter into small, thumb-sized pieces and drop them into the cold milk and cold eggs in a large bowl. Mash and work the pieces in with a fork or your fingers for about 5 minutes, until mostly softened and blended.
- Add the flour and sugar. Mix by hand until no dry flour remains, then add the salt and continue working the dough — folding, pressing, and turning it — for a solid 10–15 minutes. It will feel rough and unyielding at first; keep going.
- Add the softened butter in small handfuls, one at a time, kneading each addition in fully before adding the next. The dough will look greasy and like it's falling apart partway through — that's normal. Keep working it until it comes back together, smooth and elastic, and passes a rough windowpane test (stretch a small piece thin enough to see light through without tearing).
- There's no separate autolyse step for this dough — the egg, milk, and sugar change how the flour hydrates enough that a plain autolyse doesn't serve its usual purpose. The extended mixing above does that structural work instead.

**Ooni Halo Pro Spiral Mixer**

- Attach the Spiral Dough Hook and Breaker Bar. Tear the stiff starter into small pieces and add it to the bowl along with the cold milk and cold eggs. Let it sit about 5 minutes to soften before mixing.
- Mix on low speed for 2–3 minutes to blend the starter in, then add the flour and sugar. Continue on low speed until no dry flour remains, then increase to medium and mix until the dough is smooth, elastic, and pulling cleanly from the sides of the bowl — typically 8–12 minutes. Check for a rough windowpane before moving on.
- Add the salt once the starter and flour are fully incorporated, not before.
- With the mixer running on low-medium, add the softened butter in 4–5 additions, letting each fully incorporate before adding the next. The dough will look like it's breaking apart partway through — keep mixing. Finish once it's smooth, glossy, pulling away from the bowl again, and passes a proper windowpane test.

**Desired dough temperature:** target **74–76°F (23–24°C)** right after mixing. Starting with cold milk and cold eggs makes this easy to hit. If the dough feels warm or greasy partway through mixing, a 10–15 minute rest in the fridge helps more than pushing through.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_04_mixing_shaggy.jpg" alt="Torn starter softening into cold milk and eggs, before the flour goes in" /></figure>

### 3. Bulk fermentation — cold retard recommended

An overnight cold retard (12–16 hours in the fridge) is the easiest way to handle this soft, butter-rich dough: it keeps fermentation at a manageable pace, firms the butter back up for easier shaping, and builds flavor. A room-temperature-only bulk works too — expect 4–6+ hours at a moderate 70–73°F kitchen, running faster warm and slower cool — but the dough will be noticeably harder to shape while still warm.

Judge readiness by feel: the dough should look noticeably puffy, jiggle when the bowl is nudged, and show a slow, partial spring-back on a gentle poke test. If tracking pH, dough coming out of bulk typically reads somewhere around **pH 4.2–4.7** — mild, since both the enrichment and the stiff starter buffer against a sharp drop.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_05_bulk_puffy.jpg" alt="A bowl of puffy, fermented brioche dough ready for the fridge" /></figure>

### 4. Shaping — the brioche à tête

This is the step that makes Well Bread what it is. Shape cold, straight from the fridge — cold dough is dramatically easier to handle than this dough is at room temperature.

- Turn the chilled dough onto a lightly floured bench and divide it into the two pieces from the table above: **1,130g for the body, 280g for the head.**
- **Shape the body** into a tight, smooth ball the same way you'd shape any boule (see the site's [Proofing and Shaping guide](/guides/proofing-and-shaping) for the full technique): fold the edges in to build a rough envelope, then drag the dough in a rotating motion across an unfloured patch of bench until the surface is smooth and taut.
- **Butter your brioche mold generously** — really work it into every flute, since this is the most common place an otherwise-perfect loaf gets stuck.
- Place the body ball into the mold, seam-side down, smooth side up.
- **Flour your finger** (or the handle of a wooden spoon) and poke it straight down through the center of the body, all the way to the bottom of the mold. This creates the socket the head will sit in — and reaching all the way to the bottom matters, because it's what anchors the head in place through proofing and baking.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_06_body_socketed.jpg" alt="The body ball, shaped and socketed, sitting in the buttered mold" /></figure>

- **Shape the head:** round the 280g piece into a ball first, then cup one side between your palms and gently roll to draw out a tapered point — like a small pear, or a comma. This point is what goes into the socket.
- Insert the pointed end straight down into the socket, pressing gently until the point reaches the bottom of the mold. The round top of the head should sit proud above the body's collar — that's the classic silhouette.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_07_assembled_tete.jpg" alt="The assembled, unbaked brioche à tête, head seated on the body" /></figure>

### 5. Final proof

Proof at room temperature until visibly puffy, with a slow poke-test spring-back on the body — enriched dough proofs slower than a lean loaf at the same temperature. Expect roughly 2–4 hours at a moderate 70–73°F kitchen, running toward the shorter end warm and needing more patience cool, especially if the dough is still cold from the fridge at the start. Watch that the head stays seated and upright as the body rises around it — a well-anchored head shouldn't shift, but keep an eye on it in the last stretch of proofing.

### 6. Egg wash and bake

Brush the whole proofed loaf with an egg wash (one egg beaten with a splash of milk or water), working carefully around the neck where the head meets the body — brush gently there rather than letting egg wash pool in the crevice. Pooled egg wash can act like glue and mute the visual separation between head and body that makes this shape distinctive.

Bake at **350°F (177°C)** — notably cooler than the site's usual 450–475°F Dutch oven range for lean loaves, since this is an uncovered bake with high sugar and fat content that browns fast. Expect the full bake to run **45–60 minutes**, longer than a standard loaf given the mold's depth and this loaf's larger overall size. Tent loosely with foil once the color is where you want it — the head in particular browns faster than the body, since it's smaller and more exposed, so watch it closely in the second half of the bake. Bake to an internal temperature of **190–195°F (88–90°C)**, checked at the center of the body, rather than relying on time or color alone.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_08_egg_washed_ready.jpg" alt="The egg-washed, proofed à tête, ready for the oven" /></figure>

If tracking pH out of curiosity, dough going into the oven typically sits around **pH 4.1–4.6** — close to where it finished bulk.

### 7. Cool

Cool in the mold for 15–20 minutes, then turn out onto a wire rack and cool completely before slicing — at least an hour. The fluted sides should release cleanly if the mold was buttered generously; if it resists, run a thin knife around the edge before turning it out.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_09_crumb_final.jpg" alt="A sliced brioche à tête showing its tender, golden crumb" /></figure>

## Alternatives and Improvements

- **Miniature brioche à tête:** the classic bakery-case version of this shape is small, individually portioned — divide the full batch into about 14–16 pieces at roughly an 80/20 body-to-head split each (around 75g body / 20g head per piece), and bake in individual fluted brioche tins. Shorter bake time, more forgiving to shape, and a natural next step toward a future brioche rolls/buns recipe.
- **Pearl sugar crust:** press Swedish pearl sugar or coarse sanding sugar onto the egg wash before baking for a bit of crunch and sparkle — a simple finishing touch that plays well with this loaf's richness.
- **A savory version:** drop the sugar to 20g, add a few grinds of black pepper, and fold in a handful of grated Gruyère at the end of mixing for a savory brioche suited to a cheese board or a holiday charcuterie spread.
- **Chocolate chips:** fold in 100–150g of chopped dark chocolate or chocolate chips at the very end of mixing for a simple, kid-friendly variation without a full recipe's worth of changes.
- **A tangzhong-softened crumb:** borrow the cooked-roux technique from the Sandwich Loaf base — cooking a small portion of the flour and milk into a paste before mixing — for an even softer, longer-lasting crumb. Changes the mixing sequence, so treat it as an experiment rather than a drop-in swap.

## Make It Yours

Well Bread is deliberately pitched at a moderate-rich point on the brioche spectrum, not the maximalist end — which leaves real room to push it in either direction once you understand why it behaves the way it does.

**Butter (currently 50% of flour weight).** This is the lever that does the most to shape brioche's character. Real brioche-style formulas run anywhere from a lean-for-brioche ~25% up to a genuinely decadent ~80%, occasionally higher. Push it up and the crumb turns richer, more tender, and more fragile — a very high-butter dough gets harder to shape and holds its structure less confidently at room temperature, since you're asking gluten to carry more fat before it breaks down. Pull it back toward 25–30% and you get a firmer, more bread-like crumb that shapes more forgivingly and stays fresh a little differently — closer to a rich dinner roll than a pastry-case showpiece. Below roughly 25%, you're arguably not making brioche anymore so much as a very good enriched loaf.

**Egg (currently 50% of flour weight).** Eggs bring structure, color, and richness without butter's fragility risk, so this is a comparatively forgiving lever to move. Push it up (toward 60–70%) and the crumb deepens in color and takes on a slightly custardy richness; pull it back (toward 30–35%) and the crumb lightens, the color mutes, and the dough gets a little easier to work by hand. Butter and egg interact — a very high-butter, very high-egg dough is the richest and most technically demanding version of this bake; dialing back one while keeping the other high is a reasonable way to moderate richness without losing what makes brioche brioche.

**Sugar (currently 10% of flour weight).** A small lever next to butter and egg, but not a trivial one — brioche's sugar isn't primarily there for sweetness, it's there to feed a more tender crumb and help the crust brown. Push it toward 15% for a noticeably sweeter, faster-browning loaf (watch the bake closely if you do — a sweeter dough wants the foil tent sooner). Pull it back toward 5% for a more savory-leaning brioche, which pairs well with the Gruyère variation under Alternatives and Improvements above.

**Proof timing and the cold retard.** The overnight cold retard isn't just a convenience — it's a real flavor and workability lever in its own right. A longer, cooler retard (18–24 hours instead of the recommended 12–16) builds more acidity and complexity from the stiff starter, at the cost of a firmer, cooler dough that asks for more patience during shaping. A shorter retard, or skipping it for a same-day room-temperature bulk, gets you a milder-flavored loaf faster, but the dough is noticeably harder to shape warm — expect it to test your patience with the topknot socket specifically.

Push any of these levers hard enough and you'll eventually need to adjust something else to compensate — a very high butter percentage usually wants a slightly cooler DDT and a longer cold retard to stay workable, for instance. That's normal, and it's exactly the kind of trade-off worth learning to feel out rather than avoid.

## Ingredient History Story

### The à tête: brioche's most recognizable shape

Brioche has never been just one shape. French bakers have long turned this same rich dough into a simple loaf-pan Nanterre (a row of dough balls settled side by side into a rectangular pan), a crown-shaped Vendéenne, and any number of regional variations — but the à tête is the showpiece among them. "Tête" is French for head, and the name describes exactly what you're building: a rounded body anchored in a fluted mold, crowned with a smaller topknot seated in a socket at its center, rising free of the body around it. It's the shape most people picture the instant someone says the word "brioche," even if they've never watched one get built.

That two-piece construction — not a decorative flourish, but the actual thing that makes the shape distinctive — is closely associated with Parisian bakeries, where fluted brioche molds became a fixture of the pâtisserie counter, setting the bakery-case version apart from the plainer loaf-pan brioche baked at home. Exactly when and how the à tête became codified as *the* classic silhouette is harder to pin down with real precision than a straightforward ingredient's origin story — culinary history tends to get fuzzier the closer it gets to questions like "who first thought to poke a hole in the dough and crown it" — but its status as the defining shape of French brioche isn't in question.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_13_history_bakery.jpg" alt="A traditional French bakery display with fluted brioche molds" /></figure>

### Why this dough earned an elaborate shape

The à tête's theatrics only make sense against what brioche actually is: a genuinely rich dough, built on a level of butter and egg that made it a luxury for most of its history. That richness traces to Normandy, the lush, dairy-rich region of northern France where centuries of pastureland and skilled dairying produced butter good enough to build a bread around. Enriched, butter-heavy breads like brioche became associated with festive luxury in French baking — the sort of thing that showed up for celebrations rather than an ordinary morning — precisely because butter and eggs were genuinely expensive ingredients for most of history. The word "brioche" itself traces to Norman French, and an ordinary dough that expensive was always going to end up wearing a shape worth showing off.

**An interesting story, worth setting straight:** brioche is often linked, wrongly, to the phrase "let them eat cake," supposedly uttered by Marie Antoinette about the French poor lacking bread. Historians have found that the phrase predates Marie Antoinette's arrival in France by decades and appears attributed to earlier royals in earlier writings — the attribution to her is now widely considered apocryphal. A good story and a true one aren't always the same loaf.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_11_history_normandy.jpg" alt="A traditional Normandy dairy farm with grazing cows" /></figure>

### What the dough itself is built from

None of that theater works without the raw ingredients behind it. The bread wheat in this dough's flour traces back to the Fertile Crescent, the arc of Southwest Asia where early farmers first domesticated wild grasses into a reliable, harvestable grain roughly ten thousand years ago, before cultivation spread into the Mediterranean and across Europe along ancient trade routes. And the eggs carry their own long migration — chickens were domesticated in South and Southeast Asia several thousand years ago and spread gradually westward along the same trade networks, becoming a near-universal enrichment ingredient across cultures, from brioche to challah to panettone.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_10_history_wheat.jpg" alt="A golden wheat field at sunset" /></figure>

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_12_history_eggs.jpg" alt="Free-range hens and a basket of fresh eggs" /></figure>

### A quick word on the mold

The tinned-steel fluted brioche mold has stayed largely unchanged in French bakeries for a very long time — a simple, functional shape that does two jobs at once: the flutes give the finished loaf its ridged, sunburst-like base, and the sloped sides make turning out a sticky, butter-rich dough far easier than a straight-sided pan would. It's a small piece of equipment that's done a lot of quiet work in French pâtisserie history.

### How this fits into that lineage

Every à tête that comes out of a home oven — this one included — is standing in for a whole tradition of turning an expensive, hard-to-make dough into something worth putting on display. The shape is the point as much as the flavor: a fluted body and a topknot say "occasion" before anyone's even had a slice.

## Bloopers Section

Brioche à tête punishes impatience and imprecision more than most shapes on this site — here's what that looks like in practice.

**The vanishing head.** Poked a shallow socket, pressed the head in without much conviction, and watched it slowly sink and disappear into the body over the course of proofing — by bake time, all that was left was a faint bump. The fix: poke that socket all the way to the bottom of the mold, and press the head's point in firmly enough that it genuinely anchors there.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_14_blooper_sunken_head.jpg" alt="A brioche with its topknot sunk flat into the body instead of standing proud" /></figure>

**The toppled head.** Centered the head reasonably well, but let final proof run long — by the time it went in the oven, the dough had softened enough that the head had already started leaning. Oven spring finished the job, and it rolled off to one side mid-bake, leaving a lopsided loaf with a stray golden ball welded to the side of the pan. The fix: don't let final proof run past a slow, partial spring-back, and check that the head is still centered right before it goes in the oven.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_15_blooper_toppled_head.jpg" alt="A lopsided brioche with its topknot fallen to one side" /></figure>

**The butter-broke-and-never-came-back dough.** Added the butter too fast, and the dough turned into a greasy, separated mess that looked like it was never going to recover. Baked anyway, into a dense, oily loaf that tasted more like butter-flavored bread pudding than brioche. The fix: cooler butter, smaller increments, real patience — and a short chill in the fridge if it looks broken partway through.

<figure><img src="/images/well-bread/wb_16_blooper_broken_dough.jpg" alt="A greasy, broken mass of brioche dough in a mixer bowl" /></figure>

Every one of these is a normal part of learning a shape this technical — nobody gets the socket depth or the head's angle exactly right the first time, and even a slightly lopsided à tête is still a very good loaf of bread.
