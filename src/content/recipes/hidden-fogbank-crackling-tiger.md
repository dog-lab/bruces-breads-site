## Step-by-Step Tutorial Script

### 1. Make the tangzhong (tonight, or up to a day ahead)

Whisk the tangzhong flour (50g) and whole milk (250g) together in a small saucepan until smooth. Cook over medium-low heat, stirring constantly, until it thickens into a thick, pudding-like paste that leaves visible trails when the whisk is dragged through it -- roughly 149°F/65°C if checking with a thermometer. Remove from heat immediately; overcooking makes it gluey rather than smooth.

Go by the temperature, not the clock. This is a real starch-gelatinization threshold around 149°F/65°C -- at too gentle a heat you can stir for a long while without the paste ever actually thickening, since stirring time alone doesn't get you there. If it isn't visibly thickening after a few minutes, nudge the heat up slightly rather than just stirring longer and waiting.

Transfer to a bowl, press plastic wrap directly onto the surface to stop a skin forming, and cool completely to room temperature before using. It can be made ahead and refrigerated up to a day; bring it back to room temperature before mixing.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_03_tangzhong.jpg" alt="Milk tangzhong paste in a saucepan, thick enough to leave trails from the whisk" /></figure>

### 2. Feed your starter

Feed your starter (standard 100% hydration) so it peaks 4-8 hours before you plan to mix -- timing depends on your kitchen's warmth. Look for it to double in size with a domed top just starting to flatten at the edges.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_04_starter_at_peak.jpg" alt="A jar of active sourdough starter at peak ripeness" /></figure>

### 3. Mixing (choose your method)

Salt always goes in after the starter, regardless of method. Butter goes in last, after gluten is developed, regardless of method.

**Manual (hand-mixing)**

- Combine the cooled tangzhong with the remaining main-dough flour (450g), remaining milk (50g), egg, starter, sugar, and salt in a large bowl. Mix until a shaggy, sticky dough forms, then knead by hand -- the pincer or slap-and-fold method -- until smooth, elastic, and passing a rough windowpane test. Expect this to take a while and feel tackier than a plain lean dough, since the egg adds its own stickiness on top of the tangzhong's.
- Once gluten is developed, work in the softened butter a third at a time, kneading each addition fully in before adding the next. The dough will look like it's falling apart at first -- keep kneading. It comes back together smoother and glossier than before.

**Ooni Halo Pro Spiral Mixer**

- Attach the Spiral Dough Hook and Breaker Bar. Add the cooled tangzhong, remaining milk, egg, and remaining flour to the bowl.
- Mix on low speed for 2-3 minutes until a shaggy dough forms, then add the starter and continue on low until incorporated.
- Add the sugar and salt, then increase to medium speed and develop the dough until smooth, elastic, and pulling cleanly from the sides of the bowl -- typically 8-10 minutes. Check for a windowpane before stopping.
- With gluten developed, add the softened butter in 2-3 additions on low-to-medium speed, mixing each fully in before adding the next. Continue until the dough is smooth and elastic again, a few minutes more.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_05_mixed.jpg" alt="Dough just after butter is fully incorporated -- smooth, glossy, elastic" /></figure>

**Desired dough temperature:** target **74-76°F (23-24°C)**, a couple degrees cooler than the site's standard 75-78°F -- same reasoning as Breadwinner: it keeps the butter from softening and greasing out mid-mix rather than working in cleanly.

### 4. Bulk fermentation -- and why this dough gets its own proofing gauge

**Always bank a room-temperature bulk rise before any cold retard.** Let the dough rise at room temperature until it's grown by roughly **30%** before deciding which path to finish bulk on: continuing at room temperature until it's **50-75% risen overall**, puffy, with a slow poke-test spring-back (roughly 4-6 hours total, longer in a cooler kitchen, shorter in a warmer one); or, once past that ~30% mark, moving to the fridge for a **12-16 hour cold retard**, which also makes this sticky, egg-and-butter dough noticeably easier to shape later.

<div class="callout"><strong>Set up an aliquot jar before you mix -- it's the real readiness gauge for this dough, not a pH meter.</strong> Pull off a small, representative sample of dough right after mixing (a golf-ball-sized piece is plenty) and press it into a small, straight-sided jar or clear deli container. Mark the starting level with a rubber band or a strip of tape, then set the jar next to the main dough so it ferments on the same schedule. Because the sample sits in a container with consistent, vertical sides, its rise reads as a true, unambiguous volume percentage -- no guessing at a lumpy bulk container's actual expansion. For this dough specifically, fermented near <strong>74-76°F</strong>, treat a full <strong>100% rise</strong> in the aliquot jar (the dough level exactly doubles the starting mark) as the real "ready" signal, whether that lands you in the room-temperature-only window or partway through a cold retard.</div>

Here's the part that trips people up, and the reason this callout exists at all: **butter and sugar together slow acid production noticeably more than they slow gas production**, because the fat coats the lactic acid bacteria right along with everything else in the dough, while the yeast -- less affected -- keeps right on producing CO₂. The upshot is that a pH meter can read this dough as underdone well after the aliquot jar says otherwise. On this recipe's own test bakes, the jar hit a full 100% rise while a pH reading taken at the same moment still sat in the **high 4s (around pH 4.7)** -- noticeably higher than the roughly 4.6 ceiling that works as a decent proxy on a more lightly enriched dough like Breadwinner. Trusting the number would have meant leaving these rolls in bulk for hours they didn't need. Trusting the jar -- and the way the dough actually looked and felt -- got a fully risen, well-structured roll with a real crunch and a crumb that wasn't gummy, tasted even before it had cooled all the way. If you've got a meter, keep using it as a secondary check and a useful record for your baking log; just don't let a stubborn number override what the jar and your own hands are telling you. (The [pH, Flour, Hydration, and Temperature guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) and [Troubleshooting](/guides/troubleshooting) both cover this in more general terms, for enriched doughs beyond just this one.)

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_06_bulk_aliquot.jpg" alt="A small aliquot jar of dough next to the main bulk container, marked at the starting level and risen to double" /></figure>

### 5. Divide and shape

Turn the dough out, divide into 6 portions -- the math says ~175g each, but expect closer to 150-155g once the bowl and your hands have taken their share of this sticky dough -- or 8 portions (~130g each) for smaller rolls. Shape each as a mini batard, following the same technique as the site's [Proofing and Shaping guide](/guides/proofing-and-shaping): pre-shape into a rough round, bench-rest a few minutes, then final-shape by flattening gently, folding the long edges in toward the center, and rolling from one end with your palms while tapering both tips, landing around 6 inches long -- the closest match to a classic Ike's-style oblong sandwich roll.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_07_shaped.jpg" alt="Six shaped dough oblongs with visible surface tension, seam-down on a parchment-lined sheet" /></figure>

### 6. Final proof

Seam-down on a parchment-lined sheet, spaced well apart, until visibly puffy -- roughly 2-3 hours at room temperature, longer if the dough came straight from the fridge. Make the Dutch Crunch topping during the last 20-30 minutes of this proof, so it's ready right when the rolls are.

**Dutch Crunch topping:** whisk 100g white rice flour (not glutinous/sweet rice flour), 110g warm water (about 100°F), 4g instant yeast, 10g granulated sugar, 10g neutral oil, and 2g fine sea salt into a thick, pourable, glue-like paste. Let it sit 10-15 minutes -- it should look faintly bubbly and active by the time you use it, not flat.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_08_proofed.jpg" alt="Rolls proofed and visibly puffy on the sheet, a bowl of bubbly rice paste alongside" /></figure>

### 7. Top and bake

Preheat to 425°F. **How you apply the rice paste decides the crack pattern, not luck.** A heavier, even coat brushed on or spread with the back of a spoon dries and cracks into broad, blocky plates as the roll expands underneath -- reads as giraffe spots. A thinner, streakier drizzle leaves gaps and uneven thickness that fracture into the narrower, more irregular striping that actually reads as tiger. Either is correct; it's a choice, not a mistake (more on this in Make It Yours below).

Bake 22-25 minutes, until deeply golden and thoroughly cracked across the top -- treat that as the outer edge of the window, not a target. Check for doneness with an instant-read thermometer rather than trusting time alone: you're aiming for the same **195-200°F** internal target as the base dough, and on this recipe's own test bakes these rolls have landed done anywhere from **18 to 20 minutes**, whether divided into 6 or 8. Start checking at 18 rather than waiting out the full 22-25 -- rolls portioned by weight on a scale tend to run a bit smaller than the math on paper suggests (see the yield note above), and an overbaked roll dries out fast.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_09_scored_topped.jpg" alt="Rolls freshly coated with rice paste, ready to go into the oven" /></figure>

### 8. Cool

Onto a wire rack straight from the oven -- these are rolls, not a panned loaf, so there's no pan-steam issue to manage. Unlike a lot of bread, this one is genuinely better fully cooled than hot: tasted straight from the oven it can come across flat, even a little unpleasant, but the same roll tastes noticeably better once it's come all the way down to room temperature. It holds up well past that first day, too -- frozen and reheated for about 60 seconds in the microwave, it comes back tasting nearly as good as it did fresh.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_10_crumb.jpg" alt="A roll sliced open, showing a soft, tender crumb under the cracked tiger-striped crust" /></figure>

## Alternatives and Improvements

- **Add back a pastry-flour split, if you want to chase an even more tender crumb:** this recipe's own test kitchen ran a clean A/B -- straight bread flour (this version) against a 70/30 bread-to-pastry-flour split on the main dough -- and found the pastry flour wasn't doing meaningful work worth the extra ingredient. Treat it as optional rather than essential if you want to try it yourself; swap in 135g pastry flour for 135g of the main-dough bread flour and expect a slightly softer crumb, if any real difference at all.
- **Sesame seed topping, the Bay Area's own earlier answer:** before the rice-paste version took over locally sometime around the 1970s, some early Bay Area bakers used a sesame-seed crust instead, likely to distinguish their bread from the Oregon bakeries that got here first. Brush the shaped rolls with an egg wash and press on sesame seeds instead of the rice paste for a historically-grounded, if less dramatic-looking, variation -- and update the allergen disclosure to include sesame if you go this route.
- **Choose your crack pattern on purpose:** a heavier, even rice-paste coat gives broad giraffe-spot plates; a thinner, streakier one gives narrower tiger stripes. Both are correct -- pick based on how you want the finished roll to look, not by accident.
- **Same-day room-temperature finish vs. overnight cold retard:** the tutorial gives both paths. Room-temperature-only is faster and simpler if your schedule allows a full afternoon; the cold retard spreads the work across two days and makes the sticky, enriched dough noticeably easier to shape once it's chilled.
- **Size for 8 rolls instead of 6** for a more standard sandwich-roll size rather than Ike's generous oblong -- see the yield note above for the adjusted bake time.

## Make It Yours

**Butter percentage (currently 12%):** push it up toward 18-20% for a noticeably richer, more tender crumb that starts eating more like a soft milk roll; pull it down toward 6-8% for a leaner, more classically "sandwich bread" texture. This sits in the same range as Breadwinner for the same reason -- rich enough to taste like something, restrained enough to still slice clean and hold a filling.

**Sugar percentage (currently 8%):** pull back toward 4-5% for a more neutral, savory-leaning roll that stands up better to strong sandwich fillings; push it up toward 12% and it starts eating more like a dinner roll.

**Rice-paste thickness and application (the crack-pattern lever):** this is the one lever unique to this recipe. A heavier, even coat reads as broad giraffe spots; a thinner, streakier drizzle reads as narrower tiger stripes. Push the paste thinner still and you risk patches that don't crack at all -- there's a floor below which the paste just dries flat. Push it too thick and it can lift away from the crust in slabs instead of cracking into it. Somewhere in the middle is where most home bakers land on their first try; a second batch is where you actually start choosing the look on purpose.

**Bulk fermentation timing and the aliquot-jar target (currently 100% rise):** this is the lever this recipe is really about. A shorter bulk gets you a milder, less complex flavor and a safer margin against overproofing; pushing bulk past 100% rise in the jar risks the same slack, spreading dough that overproofing causes in any enriched loaf -- the difference here is just that pH won't warn you nearly as early as it would on a leaner dough. Trust the jar's percentage over the clock and, within reason, over the meter.

## Ingredient History Story

This roll's story is really two stories that happen to end up on the same crust.

**Tijgerbrood** -- tiger bread -- started in the Netherlands sometime in the early 20th century, named for the mottled, stripy pattern the rice-paste topping leaves as it dries and cracks over the rising loaf underneath. The name traveled better than almost anything else about the recipe: in 2012, the UK grocery chain Sainsbury's actually renamed their version "giraffe bread" after a three-year-old customer wrote in, entirely reasonably, that the pattern looked more like a giraffe's spots than a tiger's stripes -- which turns out to be a more scientifically defensible position than anyone selling the bread probably realized at the time (more on that below).

The bread crossed the Atlantic decades before it crossed the country. American newspaper ads for it turn up out of Eugene, Oregon as early as the 1930s, with the distinctive rice-flour crust specifically called out by 1941. It took a while longer to become a San Francisco signature -- some early Bay Area bakers actually used a sesame-seed topping instead at first, possibly to differentiate their version from Oregon's -- but by the 1970s, the rice-paste crust had won out locally and Dutch Crunch settled into the role it still plays today: the default roll under half the sandwiches sold in this city, a bread more identified with San Francisco now than with the country it's actually named for.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_11_history_netherlands.jpg" alt="A traditional Dutch bakery display of tijgerbrood loaves with their cracked, mottled crust" /></figure>

And then there's the tiger itself, which -- it turns out -- has been getting an unfair rap. Real tiger cats exist, and they're spotted, not striped: the genus *Leopardus* includes several small wild cats that carry "tiger cat" as an actual common name -- the oncilla (*Leopardus tigrinus*), the southern tiger cat (*Leopardus guttulus*), and, as of a genetic study published just days before this recipe went up, a newly confirmed species called the eastern tiger cat (*Leopardus emiliae*), distinguished from its cousins by DNA rather than anything visible to the eye. Every one of them wears the same kind of dark, irregular rosettes this bread's own crust cracks into. So Sainsbury's three-year-old customer had a point about the specific loaf in front of her, but the Dutch bakers who named the whole category "tiger bread" a century earlier weren't wrong either -- they just had the right animal family in mind, if not always the right pattern on any given loaf. Spots or stripes, it's still a tiger's coat.

Which brings this roll to its name. San Francisco keeps a fog machine running most summer afternoons -- locals have taken to calling it Karl -- thick enough to hide the bridge some days and burn off by noon on others, the same fog this dough's shaping and topping happened somewhere inside of, historically speaking. Hidden fogbank, crackling tiger: a nod to the city this bread adopted, and to the animal whose actual coat -- spots and all -- turns out to have been the right namesake the whole time.

## Bloopers Section

**The dough that went nowhere.** An early attempt at this dough went straight from mixing into a cold retard with zero room-temperature bulk fermentation first -- and sat overnight showing no rise at all. Cold slows fermentation; it doesn't start it from a dead stop. The fix, now baked into Step 4 above: always bank that initial room-temperature rise before the fridge ever enters the picture.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_13_blooper_norise.jpg" alt="Two large, flat, unrisen dough balls in a container the morning after a cold retard" /></figure>

**The great butter escape.** A long, warm bench session after a cold retard -- eight-plus hours between fridge and oven -- left visible streaks of butter working their way out of the dough during shaping. It still baked up fine (rolls are forgiving that way), but the fix for next time is tighter timing: less lag between pulling the dough from the fridge and getting it shaped.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_14_blooper_butter.jpg" alt="A shaped dough oblong with visible greasy streaks where butter has separated out" /></figure>

**The bald spot.** A too-thin, too-hasty pass with the rice paste left one corner of a roll with barely any coating at all -- and that corner came out of the oven smooth and pale while the rest of the roll cracked beautifully. The fix is really just attention: an even pass with the brush or spoon matters more than the total amount used.

<figure><img src="/images/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger/hidden-fogbank-crackling-tiger_15_blooper_baldspot.jpg" alt="A baked roll with one smooth, uncracked patch where the rice paste didn't fully cover the surface" /></figure>

**The premature verdict.** A roll pulled straight from the oven and tasted while it was still hot -- and the texture was almost awful, dense and gummy, nothing like what months of testing kept promising. Not really a surprise in hindsight: an enriched, egg-and-butter crumb like this one just isn't finished setting up the moment it leaves the oven, and tasting it hot was always going to catch it mid-transformation rather than at its best. Tasted again once it had actually cooled, it was a completely different roll. The fix isn't really a fix, just patience: let this dough finish becoming what it's going to be before deciding what it is.

None of this is a reason to stop -- this dough fought back a couple of times before it gave up something worth eating, and that's a pretty normal shape for any recipe worth keeping around.
