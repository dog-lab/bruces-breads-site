## Step-by-Step Tutorial Script

### 1. Feed the starter

Feed a 70/30 starter 6-8 hours ahead so it is ripe and domed, or use 120 g of unfed discard plus 1 g of instant yeast (see the discard note above).

### 2. Prep the olives

Pit the Castelvetrano olives, **rinse them, pat them dry on towels**, and cut them into pieces **1/4 to 3/8 inch**. Smaller pieces make shaping far easier, and rinsing is what keeps the finished bread from tasting too salty. Do the same with the Kalamata olives, but leave those in halves or whole slices for decorating. Keep the two kinds separate.

### 3. Mix

Whisk the water and starter together, add the flour, and stir until no dry flour remains. Rest 30 minutes, then add the salt and olive oil and squeeze or fold until incorporated. Target **75-78°F (24-26°C)**.

### 4. Stretch-and-folds, with the olives

Do a set of stretch-and-folds at 30 minutes. At the **second** set (about 60 minutes), spread the dough out, scatter the Castelvetrano pieces over it, and fold them in. Do one more set 30 minutes later. Bulk ferment for about **3-4 hours** total at room temperature, until about 50% larger, airy, and jiggly.

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_04_olives_folded_in.jpg" alt="Folding chopped Castelvetrano olives into the dough" /></figure>

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_05_bulk_bubbly.jpg" alt="Olive dough after bulk fermentation, airy and bubbly" /></figure>

### 5. Divide

Turn out onto a lightly oiled bench and divide into **six pieces of about 160 g**. Pre-shape gently into rounds and rest 20 minutes, covered.

### 6. Shape: the cookie cutter method

The fastest way to a recognizable shape is a big cutter, not fiddling by hand.

1. Press or roll a piece of dough to about **1/2 inch** thick on a sheet of parchment, a little bigger than your cutter.
2. Press the cutter through the dough and lift away the scraps, which you can fold into the next piece or bake as a snack.
3. Use a knife or pizza cutter to cut slits **inside** the shape: they open as it proofs and bakes, and they are what make it a fougasse. Keep slits away from the very edge so the outline holds.
4. Stretch the slits open with your fingers a little more than you think you need.
5. Decorate with Kalamata: eyes and mouths for ghosts and pumpkins, spider legs, bat details. Press each piece in firmly so it stays put.

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_06_cookie_cutter_shaping.jpg" alt="A large ghost cookie cutter pressed into a sheet of olive dough" /></figure>

By hand, a skull is the easiest shape, then ghost. A spider (round body, eight legs cut out as slits and stretched) is the next challenge. Printable stencils are in the template sheet if you want a pattern to cut around.

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_03_patterns.jpg" alt="Four fougasse shape patterns: skull, bat, ghost, and spiderweb" /></figure>

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_07_decorated_unbaked.jpg" alt="Ghost and bat shapes with slits stretched open and Kalamata eyes, ready to proof" /></figure>

### 7. Proof and preheat

Place the shapes on parchment, brush lightly with olive oil, and proof **30-45 minutes**, until puffy. Preheat a baking steel or stone with the oven at **475°F (246°C)** for at least 45 minutes.

### 8. Bake

Slide the parchment onto the steel and bake **14-18 minutes**, until deep golden with darker edges. Brush with olive oil straight out of the oven. Hold the flaky salt: the olives provide plenty.

### 9. Serve with a dip

Fougasse is meant to be torn and dipped. For a grown-up Halloween table:

- **Labneh:** strain whole-milk (full-fat) Greek yogurt a little, stir in salt, and drizzle with olive oil. Use full-fat: a test with 2% Fage came out dry and flaky.
- **Muhammara (untested):** blend roasted red peppers, walnuts, a spoonful of pomegranate molasses, olive oil, and a bit of chili. Taste and adjust.
- **Hummus** also works, and so does a simple bowl of good olive oil.

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_08_dip_torn.jpg" alt="Tearing a baked fougasse skull and dipping it in labneh" /></figure>

## Alternatives and Improvements

- **Discard version:** tested and fine. Use 120 g of discard and 1 g of instant yeast in place of the ripe starter.
- **Hand shaping:** if you don't have cutters, shape each piece into a rough triangle, oval, or ghost by hand and rely on Kalamata decoration to make it readable.
- **Other add-ins (untested):** rosemary, lemon zest, or roasted garlic pair well with olives.
- **Skip the Kalamata:** use more Castelvetrano for a cleaner green look, but you lose the eyes and mouths.

## Make It Yours

**Olive level (currently about 25% of total flour).** Online recipes vary, but this bread tolerates a lot of olives. Go a bit higher for a more intense bake, lower for an easier shape.

**Salt (currently 1.4%).** Because olive brine adds salt, this stays lower than a normal loaf. Taste your olives before you commit.

**Hydration (about 65%).** Higher hydration gives a more open, lacy crumb and is harder to shape. Lower is easier to cut but denser.

**Size.** Six shapes is a lot of bread. Divide into eight smaller ones if you are feeding a party.

## Ingredient History Story

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_09_history_roman_hearth.jpg" alt="A Roman bakery with a domed hearth oven and flat loaves" /></figure>

Fougasse is a bread with a long Mediterranean pedigree. Its ancestor is the Roman *panis focacius*, a flatbread baked on the hearth (*focus* in Latin), and the same word gave Italy its focaccia. In Provence the bread took on its signature look: cut with slits, usually in a leaf or ladder pattern, which opens up as it bakes. A popular bit of folklore holds that bakers used these small flatbreads to test the oven temperature before loading the loaves. That is a tidy story, and plausible, though it is folklore more than documented history.

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_11_history_provence_bakery.jpg" alt="A Provençal boulangerie window with leaf-shaped fougasse" /></figure>

Olives are older still: cultivated around the Mediterranean for thousands of years, and the reason fougasse became the savory bread of southern France. Castelvetrano olives come from Sicily; Kalamata from southern Greece. 

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_10_history_olive_grove.jpg" alt="An olive grove and lavender in Provence" /></figure>

Here they share a loaf: one green, mild, and buttery, the other dark, winey, and perfect for spooky eyes.

## Bloopers Section

**Big olive chunks.** The first batch had large olive pieces that fought every attempt at shaping, tearing the dough and refusing to lie flat. The fix: cut them to 1/4 to 3/8 inch.

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_12_blooper_big_chunks.jpg" alt="Lumpy dough with big olive chunks tearing the surface (illustration of the blooper)" /></figure>

**A little too salty.** Even with less salt in the dough, the finished bread came out salty. The fix: rinse the olives, trim the dough salt to 6 g, and go easy on any finishing salt.

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_13_blooper_salty.jpg" alt="A fougasse crusted with coarse salt beside a glass of water (illustration of the blooper)" /></figure>

**Shapes in name only.** Hand-cut ghosts and bats were hard to tell apart without Kalamata details. The fix: extra-large Halloween cookie cutters, which made the shapes quick and recognizable.

<figure><img src="/images/boo-gasse/boo-gasse_14_blooper_unrecognizable.jpg" alt="Hand-shaped blobs on a tray that no one could identify (illustration of the blooper)" /></figure>
