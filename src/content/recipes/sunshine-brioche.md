## Step-by-Step Tutorial Script

### 1. Feed your levain, the day before

Feed your stiff starter (see the [Starter Guide: Starter Types & Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours/)) so it peaks roughly 8–12 hours before you plan to mix — a stiff starter takes longer to ripen than a loose one, so plan for an overnight feed if you're mixing in the morning, or a morning feed for an evening mix. You're looking for the dome to stop rising and just begin to flatten and crack slightly, not the loose bubbly surface of a liquid starter. If you have a pH meter, ripe stiff starter for this recipe typically reads somewhere around **pH 4.0–4.5** — milder than the site's standard starter, which is expected and part of the point.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_03_starter_at_peak.jpg" alt="A jar of stiff sourdough starter at peak ripeness" /></figure>

### 2. Zest and juice your oranges

Wash and scrub two medium oranges well. Zest both with a light touch, stopping at the orange layer — you're aiming for about 10g total, roughly 4 tightly packed teaspoons. Rub the zest into your 50g of granulated sugar with your fingers until the sugar smells strongly of orange. Then juice both oranges, strain out the pulp and seeds, and weigh out 150g of juice (top up with a splash of milk if you're short). Keep both the zested sugar and the juice cold and set aside until mixing.

### 3. Mixing

Brioche needs its gluten fully developed *before* the butter goes in — this is a stand mixer or spiral mixer job, not a hand-kneading one. Hand-developing full structure in a dough this soft, and then working in 250g of butter by hand afterward, is possible but a genuine arm workout, and it's the single easiest way to end up with a dense loaf if the gluten isn't strong enough going in.

**Manual (hand-mixing)**

- Tear the ripe stiff starter into small, thumb-sized pieces and drop them into the cold orange juice and cold eggs in a large bowl. Mash and work the pieces in with a fork or your fingers for about 5 minutes, until mostly softened and blended.
- Add the flour and the orange-zested sugar. Mix by hand until no dry flour remains, then add the salt and continue working the dough — folding, pressing, and turning it — for a solid 10–15 minutes. It will feel rough and unyielding at first; keep going.
- Add the softened butter in small handfuls, one at a time, kneading each addition in fully before adding the next. This is the demanding part — the dough will look greasy and like it's falling apart partway through. That's normal. Keep working it until it comes back together, smooth and elastic, and passes a rough windowpane test (stretch a small piece thin enough to see light through without tearing).
- There's no separate autolyse step for this dough — the egg, juice, and sugar change how the flour hydrates enough that a plain autolyse doesn't serve its usual purpose. The extended mixing above does that structural work instead.

**Ooni Halo Pro Spiral Mixer**

- Attach the Spiral Dough Hook and Breaker Bar. Tear the stiff starter into small pieces and add it to the bowl along with the cold orange juice and cold eggs. Let it sit about 5 minutes to soften before mixing.
- Mix on low speed for 2–3 minutes to blend the starter in, then add the flour and orange-zested sugar. Continue on low speed until no dry flour remains, then increase to medium and mix until the dough is smooth, elastic, and pulling cleanly from the sides of the bowl — typically 8–12 minutes. Check for a rough windowpane before moving on.
- Add the salt once the starter and flour are fully incorporated, not before.
- With the mixer running on low-medium, add the softened butter in 4–5 additions, letting each one fully incorporate before adding the next. The dough will look like it's breaking apart partway through — keep mixing. Finish once it's smooth, glossy, pulling away from the bowl again, and passes a proper windowpane test.

**Desired dough temperature:** target **74–76°F (23–24°C)** right after mixing, same as the base. Starting with cold juice and cold eggs (as above) makes this easy to hit without extra effort. If the dough feels warm or greasy partway through mixing, a 10–15 minute rest in the fridge helps more than pushing through.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_04_mixing_shaggy.jpg" alt="Torn starter softening into cold orange juice and eggs, before the flour goes in" /></figure>

### 4. Bulk fermentation — cold retard recommended

An overnight cold retard (12–16 hours in the fridge) is the easiest way to handle this soft, butter-rich dough: it keeps fermentation at a manageable sourdough pace, firms the butter back up for easier shaping, and builds flavor along the way. A room-temperature-only bulk works too — expect 4–6+ hours at a moderate 70–73°F kitchen, running faster in a warmer kitchen and slower in a cooler one — but the dough will be noticeably harder to shape while still warm.

Either way, judge readiness by feel rather than a fixed number: the dough should look noticeably puffy, jiggle when the bowl is nudged, and show a slow, partial spring-back on a gentle poke test. If you're tracking pH, dough coming out of bulk on this recipe typically reads somewhere around **pH 4.3–4.8** — on the mild end, since the enrichment and the stiff starter both buffer against a sharp drop.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_05_bulk_puffy.jpg" alt="A bowl of puffy, fermented brioche dough ready for the fridge" /></figure>

### 5. Shaping — the sunburst boule

Shape cold, straight from the fridge — cold dough is dramatically easier to handle than this dough is at room temperature. Turn it out onto a lightly floured bench, and shape it the same way you'd shape any boule (see the site's [Proofing and Shaping guide](/guides/proofing-and-shaping) if you want the full technique): fold the edges in toward the center to build a rough envelope, then drag the dough across an unfloured patch of bench in a rotating motion until the surface is smooth and taut. Enriched dough is softer and stickier than a lean loaf, so don't chase the same aggressive tension — a gently taut surface is enough here.

Place the shaped boule seam-side down on a parchment-lined baking sheet (rather than seam-side up in a banneton, since this loaf gets scored top-down before baking).

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_06_shaped_boule.jpg" alt="A smooth, shaped brioche boule on a floured bench" /></figure>

### 6. Final proof

Proof at room temperature until visibly puffy, with a slow poke-test spring-back — enriched dough proofs slower than a lean loaf at the same temperature. Expect roughly 2–4 hours at a moderate 70–73°F kitchen, running toward the shorter end in a warm kitchen and needing more patience in a cool one, especially if the dough is still cold from the fridge at the start.

### 7. Egg wash, score, and bake

Brush the proofed loaf generously with an egg wash (one egg beaten with a splash of milk or water). Then, working from the center outward, score the sunburst pattern: a series of shallow, evenly spaced cuts radiating from the middle of the loaf to the edge, like rays. Keep the cuts shallow — this is a decorative pattern more than a functional one, since enriched dough doesn't bloom at a score the way a lean loaf does, but a confident, even hand still makes a real difference in how it bakes up.

Bake at **350°F (177°C)** — notably cooler than the site's usual 450–475°F Dutch oven range for lean loaves, since this is an uncovered bake with a high sugar and fat content that browns fast. Start checking around 35 minutes, and expect the full bake to run **35–50 minutes** depending on your oven and the loaf's exact size. Tent loosely with foil if the top is browning faster than the inside is cooking through — with the egg wash and natural sugars from the juice, this loaf browns eagerly. Bake to an internal temperature of **190–195°F (88–90°C)**, checked at the center of the loaf, rather than relying on time or color alone.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_07_scored_sunburst.jpg" alt="Egg-washed, sunburst-scored loaf ready for the oven" /></figure>

If tracking pH out of curiosity, dough going into the oven typically sits around **pH 4.2–4.7** — close to where it finished bulk, since the cool oven-ready dough hasn't had much more time to sour.

### 8. Cool, then glaze

Cool in the pan — or, since this is a freestanding boule, directly on a wire rack — for at least 45 minutes to an hour before glazing, and don't rush this step. Once the loaf is fully cool, not just cool-to-the-touch on the outside, whisk together the Sunshine Glaze (65g powdered sugar + 2–3 tbsp fresh orange juice, whisked until pourable but not watery) and drizzle it over the top, letting it run down the sides and pool a little at the base. Slice once the glaze has set, about 15–20 minutes.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_08_crumb_final.jpg" alt="A sliced brioche loaf with a tender, orange-flecked crumb and a citrus glaze drizzle" /></figure>

## Alternatives and Improvements

- **Orange liqueur depth:** swap 2–3 tablespoons of the orange juice for Grand Marnier or Cointreau for a more grown-up, aromatic edge — a natural next step once you've made this recipe as written and want to push it further.
- **Blood oranges in season:** swap in blood oranges when they're available (typically winter into early spring) for a dramatically pink-streaked crumb and a slightly more tart, berry-adjacent flavor. Juice yield runs a little lower per orange, so plan on 3 blood oranges rather than 2 to hit the same 150g.
- **A marmalade swirl:** for a more assertive citrus hit and a marbled crumb, spread a thin layer of good orange marmalade over the shaped dough before the final tension-building step, rolling it in rather than mixing it through — a bigger departure from "minimal addition," but a genuinely good one.
- **Partial honey swap:** replace half the granulated sugar (25g) with honey for a rounder sweetness that plays well against the citrus. Honey brings a little extra liquid, so the dough may feel slightly softer — that's expected, not a mistake.
- **Rolls instead of a boule:** divide the dough into 60–70g portions (about 20 rolls) instead of shaping one large loaf, brushing each roll individually with the Sunshine Glaze once baked and cooled — a nicer format for a brunch table than slicing a single loaf.

## Make It Yours

Sunshine Brioche sits on the same moderate-rich brioche base as [Well Bread](/recipes/well-bread/), with citrus doing a lot of the personality work on top — which means there are two different kinds of levers here: the citrus/sweetness balance unique to this recipe, and the same underlying brioche levers (butter, egg, proof timing) that apply to the whole Brioche Base family.

**Orange juice and zest (currently 150g juice, 10g zest).** This is the lever most worth playing with. More zest (up to 15g or so) pushes the aromatic citrus punch harder without adding meaningful liquid or sugar, since zest is concentrated oil rather than juice. More juice nudges the dough's hydration and mild acidity up together — useful for a slightly softer crumb, but push it too far and you'll need a touch more flour to keep the dough workable. Pull back on both and orange becomes a background note rather than the headline — closer to Well Bread with a hint of citrus than a true Sunshine Brioche.

**Sugar and the glaze (currently 50g dough sugar, plus a 65g powdered-sugar glaze).** The dough's own sugar is a small lever on its own — see Well Bread's Make It Yours for how it trades off against browning speed and savory-leaning variations. The glaze is the bigger sweetness dial here, and the most forgiving one to adjust: thin it for a lighter sheen and less overall sweetness, thicken it for a heavier, more dessert-like finish, or skip it entirely (the nutrition figures above already account for that swap) for a loaf that leans breakfast rather than dessert.

**Butter and egg (currently 50% and 50% of flour weight, same as the base).** These behave exactly as they do in the base recipe — see Well Bread's Make It Yours for the full range and trade-offs. The orange doesn't change how butter and egg behave here; they're still the levers that move this dough between "rich enriched loaf" and "genuinely decadent pastry-case brioche."

**Proof timing and the cold retard.** Same lever as Well Bread, with one citrus-specific wrinkle: the mild acidity from the orange juice gives this dough's stiff starter a little more company on the "mild vs. sharp" side of fermentation. A longer, cooler retard builds more natural tang from both the starter and the juice together; a shorter one keeps the citrus brighter and the sourdough character milder.

Push these levers together rather than one at a time while you're first getting a feel for them — a heavier hand with the zest pairs naturally with a lighter glaze, for instance, since you're trading one kind of citrus intensity for another rather than stacking both to their maximum at once.

## Ingredient History Story

### The sunburst score: brioche dressed for an occasion

Not every score on a loaf is functional. On a lean sourdough, scoring exists mainly to control where the dough splits as it springs in the oven — a practical release valve as much as a decoration. But European baking has a long, separate tradition of purely decorative scoring: radiating rays, wheat-sheaf motifs, rosettes, and other patterns cut into festive breads specifically to signal that a loaf was meant for an occasion rather than an ordinary table. A sunburst — a ring of evenly spaced cuts radiating out from the center — is one of the oldest and most recognizable of these decorative patterns, precisely because it reads clearly from across a table or a bakery counter without needing any special technique to appreciate.

Brioche already carries an occasion-worthy history on its own — see [Well Bread's history section](/recipes/well-bread/) for the fuller telling of how a genuinely expensive, butter-and-egg-rich dough became French baking's go-to for celebrations rather than a Tuesday breakfast. Giving this brioche a sunburst score rather than a plain, unscored top continues that same "worth dressing up" instinct, just carried out with a blade instead of a fluted mold — a different tool doing the same essential job of turning a rich dough into something visibly worth the occasion.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_16_history_sunburst_scoring.jpg" alt="A baker's hand scoring a decorative sunburst pattern into a brioche dough with a bread lame" /></figure>

### Brioche's rich, festive roots

That richness traces to Normandy, the lush, dairy-rich region of northern France where centuries of pastureland and skilled dairying produced butter good enough to build a bread around. Enriched, butter-heavy breads like brioche became associated with festive luxury in French baking — the sort of thing that showed up for celebrations rather than an ordinary morning — precisely because butter and eggs were genuinely expensive ingredients for most of history. The word "brioche" itself traces to Norman French, and the bread's identity is still tangled up with that region's dairy tradition today.

**An interesting story, worth setting straight:** brioche is often linked, wrongly, to the phrase "let them eat cake," supposedly uttered by Marie Antoinette about the French poor lacking bread. Historians have found that the phrase predates Marie Antoinette's arrival in France by decades and appears attributed to earlier royals in earlier writings — the attribution to her is now widely considered apocryphal, a myth that stuck long after the facts caught up with it. It's a good reminder that a good story and a true one aren't always the same loaf.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_10_history_normandy_butter.jpg" alt="A traditional Normandy dairy farm with grazing cows and fresh butter" /></figure>

### What the dough itself is built from

Bread wheat's story starts in the Fertile Crescent roughly ten thousand years ago, spreading through the Mediterranean and across Europe along ancient trade routes long before anyone was scoring a loaf into rays. Eggs carry a similarly long migration — chickens were domesticated in South and Southeast Asia several thousand years ago and spread gradually westward along the same trade networks, becoming a near-universal enrichment ingredient across cultures, from brioche to challah to panettone.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_09_history_wheat_field.jpg" alt="A golden wheat field at sunset" /></figure>

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_11_history_eggs_hens.jpg" alt="Free-range hens and a basket of fresh eggs on a sunlit farm" /></figure>

### Orange: the sunshine ingredient's long trade route

Sweet oranges trace back to origins in Southeast Asia, in the region spanning southern China and northeastern India, where citrus cultivation stretches back many centuries. From there, oranges moved slowly westward along historic trade routes, carried through India and Persia and into the hands of Arab traders and cultivators, who introduced citrus to the Mediterranean world. Centuries later, Spanish and Portuguese explorers carried orange trees across the Atlantic during the Age of Exploration, and citrus took root with particular success in what's now Florida, and later California — both regions that would go on to become defining names in the world's orange industry. Brazil, too, would eventually become the world's largest producer of orange juice, closing the loop on a fruit that quietly crossed nearly every ocean on its way into this dough.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_12_history_orange_grove.jpg" alt="A sunlit orange grove heavy with ripe fruit" /></figure>

### A quick word on the zester

The microplane-style zester most bakers reach for today has a surprisingly recent kitchen history — it's a direct adaptation of a woodworking rasp, repurposed for food use and popularized in home kitchens only in the past few decades. Before that, cooks relied on coarser box graters or knives to remove citrus peel, tools that made it far harder to avoid the bitter white pith just beneath the surface. The fine, even zest this recipe calls for is, in a small way, a genuinely modern convenience.

### How this fits into that lineage

Every sunburst-scored loaf that comes out of a home oven is doing the same basic thing as Well Bread's fluted, topknotted à tête: taking a genuinely rich, historically expensive dough and giving it a shape that says "occasion" before anyone's had a slice — just with a blade instead of a mold, and a ring of citrus-bright dough standing in for the tradition's original showpiece hardware.

## Bloopers Section

Brioche punishes impatience more than most doughs on this site — here's what that looks like in practice.

**The butter-broke-and-never-came-back loaf.** Added the butter too fast, or too warm, and the dough turned into a greasy, separated mess that looked (and felt) like it was never going to come together again. Baked anyway, out of stubbornness, into a dense, oily brick that tasted more like butter-flavored bread pudding than brioche. The fix: cooler butter, added in smaller increments, with real patience between each one — and if it does look broken, a short chill in the fridge does more good than another five minutes of frantic mixing.

**The bitter loaf.** Got a little too enthusiastic with the microplane and zested straight through into the white pith. The finished loaf had an odd, faintly bitter aftertaste that took a slice or two to place. The fix: lighter pressure, more passes if needed, and stop the moment the peel underneath turns pale.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_14_blooper_pith_zest.jpg" alt="Orange zest with visible bitter white pith mixed in" /></figure>

**The glaze puddle.** Couldn't wait. Glazed the loaf while it was still faintly warm, and watched the glaze thin out and run into a translucent, sticky puddle at the base of the board instead of setting into a glossy sheen on top. Still tasted fine, looked like a crime scene. The fix: let it cool completely — genuinely completely, not "cool enough" — before glazing.

<figure><img src="/images/sunshine-brioche/sb_15_blooper_melted_glaze.jpg" alt="A brioche loaf with melted, runny glaze pooling at the base" /></figure>

Every one of these is a completely normal part of learning a rich, temperamental dough like brioche — nobody gets the butter incorporation right the first time, and a slightly bitter or slightly melted loaf is still a loaf worth eating while you figure it out.
