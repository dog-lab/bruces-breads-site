## Step-by-Step Tutorial Script

### 1. Make the tangzhong (day-of, or up to a day ahead)

Whisk the tangzhong flour (50g) and whole milk (250g) together in a small saucepan until smooth. Cook over medium-low heat, stirring constantly, until it thickens into a thick, pudding-like paste that leaves visible trails when the whisk is dragged through it -- roughly 149°F/65°C if checking with a thermometer. Remove from heat immediately; overcooking makes it gluey rather than smooth.

Transfer to a bowl, press plastic wrap directly onto the surface to stop a skin forming, and cool completely to room temperature before using -- mixing it in warm risks both overheating the dough on contact and dulling the starter's activity. It can be made ahead and refrigerated up to a day; bring it back to room temperature before mixing.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_03_tangzhong.jpg" alt="Milk tangzhong paste in a saucepan, thick enough to leave trails from the whisk" /></figure>

### 2. Feed your starter

Feed your starter (standard 100% hydration) so it peaks 4-8 hours before you plan to mix -- timing depends on your kitchen's warmth. Look for it to double in size with a domed top just starting to flatten at the edges.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_04_starter_at_peak.jpg" alt="A jar of active sourdough starter at peak ripeness" /></figure>

### 3. Mixing (choose your method)

Salt always goes in after the starter, regardless of method. Butter goes in last, after gluten is developed, regardless of method.

**Manual (hand-mixing)**

- Combine the cooled tangzhong with the remaining main-dough flour (450g), remaining milk (50g), egg, starter, sugar, and salt in a large bowl. Mix until a shaggy, sticky dough forms.
- Knead by hand until smooth, elastic, and passing a rough windowpane test -- expect this to take longer and feel tackier than a plain lean dough, and longer still than this loaf's plain water variant, since the egg adds its own stickiness on top of the tangzhong's.
- Once gluten is developed, work in the softened butter a third at a time, kneading each addition fully in before adding the next. The dough will look like it's falling apart at first -- keep kneading. It comes back together smoother and glossier than before.

**Ooni Halo Pro Spiral Mixer**

- Attach the Spiral Dough Hook and Breaker Bar. Add the cooled tangzhong, remaining milk, egg, and remaining flour to the bowl.
- Mix on low speed for 2-3 minutes until a shaggy dough forms, then add the starter and continue on low until incorporated.
- Add the sugar and salt, then increase to medium speed and develop the dough until smooth, elastic, and pulling cleanly from the sides of the bowl -- typically 8-10 minutes. Check for a windowpane before stopping.
- With gluten developed, add the softened butter in 2-3 additions on low-to-medium speed, mixing each fully in before adding the next. Continue until the dough is smooth and elastic again, a few minutes more.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_05_butter_incorporation.jpg" alt="Dough partway through butter incorporation -- looks broken, then comes back together glossy" /></figure>

**Desired dough temperature:** target **74-76°F (23-24°C)** -- a couple degrees cooler than the site's standard 75-78°F, same reasoning as Well Bread: it keeps the butter from softening and greasing out mid-mix rather than working in cleanly.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_06_mixed.jpg" alt="Dough just after butter is fully incorporated -- smooth, glossy, elastic" /></figure>

### 4. Bulk fermentation

**Always start with a room-temperature bulk rise before any cold retard -- never go straight from mixing to the fridge.** Let the dough rise at room temperature until it's grown by roughly **30%** -- a provisional figure, not yet locked in by repeat testing -- before deciding which path to finish bulk on:

- **Room temperature only:** continue until the dough is roughly **50-75% risen overall**, puffy, with a slow poke-test spring-back. Expect **4-6 hours total** from the start of bulk.
- **Cold retard:** once past that initial ~30% rise, move to the fridge for **12-16 hours**. This also makes this stickier, egg-and-butter-enriched dough noticeably easier to shape.

This loaf, and its plain water-based sibling, both ferment at a more moderate pace than Well Bread, since neither carries anywhere near that much fat -- budget accordingly rather than expecting Well Bread's timing.

<div class="callout"><strong>pH check (optional):</strong> check pH rather than relying on visual rise alone -- aim for the dough to read somewhere around <strong>pH 4.6, or a touch lower,</strong> by the time it goes into the oven. A reading meaningfully above that (low 5s) means fermentation hasn't run far enough, even if the visual rise looks close -- this recipe's own validation history (see Bloopers below) is a real example of exactly that trap.</div>

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_07_bulk.jpg" alt="Dough mid-bulk-fermentation, visibly risen and puffy" /></figure>

### 5. Shaping

Turn the dough out onto a lightly floured bench. Press it into a rough rectangle roughly as wide as your loaf pan, then roll it up tightly into a log, pinching the seam closed. Place seam-down in a greased 9x5in pan.

For a domed, bakery-style top, let it proof uncovered. For a flatter, square-shouldered Pullman top, use a pan with a sliding lid instead.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_08_shaped.jpg" alt="Dough shaped into a tight log, seam-down in the loaf pan" /></figure>

### 6. Final proof

Proof until the dough crowns 1-2in above the rim of the pan (or nearly fills a covered Pullman pan) -- for sandwich bread, pan-fill height is a more reliable doneness cue than jiggle or poke-test alone, since the shape is constrained by the pan. Expect roughly **2-3 hours** depending on kitchen warmth.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_09_proofed.jpg" alt="Dough proofed to a dome well above the rim of the pan" /></figure>

### 7. Bake

An egg wash is very much in the spirit of this loaf -- brush on a light egg wash (a whole egg beaten with a splash of water or milk) for shine and deep color, or a milk wash for a softer, more matte crust. Bake at **350°F (177°C)**, tenting loosely with foil if the top browns faster than the inside finishes -- this variant's egg and extra sugar mean it browns a bit faster on its own than the plain water variant, so watch it a little closer. Target an internal temperature of **195-200°F (91-93°C)**.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_10_baked.jpg" alt="Finished loaf, deeply golden and glossy from the egg wash, fresh from the oven" /></figure>

### 8. Cool

Turn the loaf out of the pan immediately after baking -- unlike a Dutch oven loaf, a hot pan traps steam against the crust and softens the sides into a soggy patch if left in. Cool completely on a wire rack before slicing, at least an hour; a soft sandwich loaf sliced warm tears rather than cutting clean.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_11_crumb.jpg" alt="The cooled loaf sliced, showing a soft, tender, faintly golden crumb" /></figure>

## Alternatives and Improvements

**Swap to the water variant -- this loaf's plain Classics-section sibling:** the same tangzhong technique, without any dairy or egg, sits in The Classics instead of Everyday Bakes.

| Ingredient | Breadwinner (this recipe) | Water variant |
|---|---|---|
| Tangzhong liquid | Whole milk, 250g | Water, 250g |
| Main-dough liquid | Whole milk, 50g | Water, 75g |
| Egg | 50g (about 1 large) | omit entirely |
| Fat | Butter, 60g (12%), worked in after gluten development | Neutral oil (avocado, canola, or similar), 40g (8%), added with the initial mix -- no separate butter-incorporation stage needed |
| Sugar | 40g (8%) | 30g (6%) |

The water variant's dough also tolerates the standard **75-78°F** DDT rather than this recipe's cooler 74-76°F, since there's no butter at risk of greasing out -- and mixing is a full step simpler without a separate butter-working stage. It bakes and cools identically otherwise. Worth knowing: in this recipe's own validation testing, both variants tasted good once properly fermented, but the milk-and-butter version had noticeably more richness and depth -- a real, if modest, difference worth trying both to judge for yourself.

- **Fold in mix-ins after bulk, before the final shaping roll:** a cinnamon-sugar swirl rolled into the log before it goes into the pan is a natural match for this variant's richness -- cut into it like a babka once baked. Toasted seeds pressed onto an egg-washed crust work too.
- **Try a whole-grain swap on the main-dough flour only** (leave the tangzhong portion as bread flour, since that's where the gelatinization does its work) -- up to about 20% whole wheat adds flavor without fighting the tangzhong's softening effect too hard.
- **Size it for two smaller pans instead of one 9x5in** if you want more crust-to-crumb ratio, or you're baking primarily for sandwich-sized rather than full-size loaves.
- **Drop the egg, keep the milk and butter** for a slightly less rich, more classically "milk bread" crumb -- mixing gets a little easier without the egg's extra stickiness, and the dough will be marginally less golden inside.

## Make It Yours

**Butter percentage (currently 12%):** this sits well below Well Bread's 35% on purpose -- a sandwich loaf still needs to slice clean and hold a filling. Push butter up toward 18-20% for a noticeably richer, more tender crumb that starts eating more like a soft milk roll; pull it down toward 6-8% and you'll land close to the water variant's territory without giving up dairy entirely.

**Sugar percentage (currently 8%):** pull it back toward 4-5% for a more neutral, savory-leaning loaf that works better under strong-flavored sandwich fillings; push it up toward 12% and you'll get a noticeably sweeter crumb that eats more like a dinner roll or even a starting point for cinnamon rolls -- at that point, look at Well Bread instead, since you're leaving sandwich-loaf territory.

**Milk vs. water (see Alternatives above for the full swap):** this is the single biggest lever on this recipe -- it's the difference between the Everyday Bakes and Classics sections entirely, not just a flavor tweak. Everything else about the process stays nearly identical.

**Bulk fermentation timing (the room-temp-then-retard sequence):** a longer room-temperature head start before the cold retard builds more flavor and a lower finished pH, at the cost of a narrower window before the dough overshoots; a shorter head start is more forgiving of a busy schedule but risks the under-fermentation this recipe's own test-kitchen history (below) already ran into once. The ~30% room-temperature threshold is a real, tested floor -- going meaningfully below it is asking for the same failure this recipe already had.

## Ingredient History Story

### Milk comes to Hokkaido

This loaf's richness starts with a place, not an ingredient: Hokkaido, Japan's northernmost island, wasn't always known for dairy. That changed largely because of one American. In 1873, Edwin Dun arrived in Japan hired by Albert Capron -- whose father, Horace Capron, was the Meiji government's chief foreign adviser on developing Hokkaido. Between 1876 and 1883, working out of Sapporo, Dun built a dairy farm and butter and cheese production facilities essentially from scratch, importing livestock and equipment as working models and personally teaching as many as seventy Japanese students the fundamentals of animal husbandry and veterinary medicine. It was unglamorous, foundational work -- and it's the reason Hokkaido became, and remains, Japan's dairy heartland.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_12_history_hokkaido.jpg" alt="A Hokkaido dairy pasture landscape, evoking the island's transformation into Japan's dairy region" /></figure>

### Milk meets bread, seven decades later

Bread wasn't part of everyday Japanese cooking before the 20th century -- rice held that role. That changed after World War II, when American wheat shipments arrived as postwar food relief, addressing rice shortages and giving Japanese bakers a reason to take bread seriously. What emerged was *shokupan* -- literally "eating bread" -- a soft, pillowy white loaf that became Japan's standard sandwich and breakfast bread. By the time bread had fully arrived, Hokkaido's dairy industry, already three generations deep thanks to Dun's groundwork, was perfectly positioned to supply the milk that would go into its richest, most prized version: Hokkaido milk bread, built on the same *yudane*/tangzhong technique as its plainer cousins, but leaning into dairy rather than working around it.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_13_history_milkbread.jpg" alt="A traditional Japanese bakery display of soft, golden milk bread loaves" /></figure>

### From regional specialty to global sensation

For decades, Hokkaido milk bread stayed mostly an Asian phenomenon -- beloved at home, largely unknown elsewhere. That changed surprisingly recently: bakeries in Los Angeles, London, and other cities outside Asia only began selling it in earnest during a genuine boom in 2022 and 2023, with premium loaves commanding real prices and regularly selling out. Nearly a century and a half after Edwin Dun's imported cattle first grazed Sapporo's pastures, milk from that same region -- by way of a technique named in a 2007 Taiwanese cookbook, Yvonne Chen's *65°C Bread Doctor* -- became one of the most talked-about loaves in the world.

## Bloopers Section

**The brick loaf.** Dense, tight, barely risen despite what felt like a full afternoon of waiting -- this recipe's own validation bake ran into exactly this, on this very variant, before the fix was found: a half-batch mixed with mid-mix fridge breaks came out of mixing cooler than intended, then went straight to the fridge with no room-temperature bulk rise first. It read pH 5.1, well above the roughly 4.6-or-lower target, and baked up small. The fix: always bank the room-temperature bulk rise before any cold retard, and check pH instead of trusting the clock or the visual rise alone.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_14_blooper_dense.jpg" alt="Small, dense, under-risen loaf next to a properly risen one for comparison" /></figure>

**The greasy dough.** Butter added too soft -- nearly melted, not just softened -- turned the whole dough slick and oily-looking instead of smooth. It baked fine in the end, but the crumb was noticeably closer-textured than usual. The fix: butter should be cool-room-temperature, pressable but not shiny; if the kitchen's warm, a short chill in the fridge before adding it helps more than trying to power through with a too-soft stick.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_15_blooper_greasy.jpg" alt="A dough with a slick, oily sheen from butter added too warm" /></figure>

**The soggy-sided loaf.** Left to cool in the pan because it smelled too good to deal with right away -- the bottom and sides steamed themselves soft instead of setting up. The fix: turn it out onto a rack the moment it comes out of the oven, however tempting it is to leave it be.

<figure><img src="/images/breadwinner/breadwinner_16_blooper_soggy.jpg" alt="Loaf with visibly soft, pale sides from cooling in the pan" /></figure>

Every one of these happened on the way to this recipe actually working -- including, honestly, the dense-loaf blooper on this exact recipe, twice, before the room-temperature-bulk fix stuck. That's just what dialing in a new technique looks like.
