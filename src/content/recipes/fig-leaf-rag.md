## Step-by-Step Tutorial Script

**Timeline at a glance (kitchen around 70-73F / 21-23C):** levain build the night before, mix in the morning, about 4-5 hours of bulk with folds, shape, cold proof 10-16 hours, then bake.

### 1. Build the levain (evening, about 8-10 hours ahead)

Mix 10 g of starter, 45 g of bread flour, and 45 g of water in a jar. Let it ferment at room temperature until it has domed and roughly doubled, is bubbly throughout, and smells pleasantly tangy. In a warmer kitchen this can take closer to 6-7 hours; in a cooler one, 10-12. If you have a pH meter, a ripe levain reads roughly **3.8-4.3**. Practice varies, so treat the dome-and-bubbles look as the main signal.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_03_levain_peak.jpg" alt="A jar of ripe levain, domed and bubbly, with a rubber band marking its starting level" /></figure>

### 2. Prep the fruit and the black garlic (about 30 minutes before you mix)

**Figs:** snip off the hard stems and quarter the figs. Cover them with 120 g of hot water and let them steep for about 20 minutes. Drain through a sieve into a measuring cup. The strained liquid (usually 70-90 g) counts toward the 310 g of dough water, so top it up with plain water to reach 310 g. Pat the figs dry and set them aside. Quartering matters: whole figs leave big craters in the crumb and tear the dough.

**Black garlic:** peel the cloves, weigh out 35 g, and mash them with a fork into a paste. Stir the paste into the dough water until it forms a smooth, dark slurry.

**Walnuts (if using):** toast at 350F / 175C for 6-8 minutes until fragrant, cool completely, and break into rough pieces.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_04_fig_soak.jpg" alt="Quartered dried black mission figs steeping in a bowl of hot water next to a bowl of mashed black garlic" /></figure>

### 3. Mix (choose your method)

**Desired dough temperature (DDT):** aim for about **76F / 24-25C** at the end of mixing. With a 72F kitchen and 72F flour, water around 82F (28C) gets a hand-mixed dough there. If you mix with the spiral mixer, friction adds heat, so use much cooler water (roughly 45-60F / 7-15C). A thermometer is optional. Without one, the dough should feel neither cool nor warm right after mixing.

Salt always goes in after the levain is mixed in, never before.

**Manual (hand-mixing)**

- Whisk the bread flour, whole wheat, rye, and vital wheat gluten together in the bowl. Add the dough water (with the black garlic slurry and fig liquor), and mix until no dry flour remains. Autolyse for at least 30 minutes; you can stretch this to 90-120 minutes if your schedule allows.
- Add the ripe levain and work it in with the pincer method until fully incorporated. Add the salt and keep folding for 3-5 minutes total, until no dry bits remain. Manual mixing only reaches light-to-medium gluten development, which is fine because the folds below finish the job.

**Ooni Halo Core Spiral Mixer**

- This roughly 1 kg dough is a comfortable fit for the Halo Core. Autolyse is built into this method, so there is no separate autolyse step ahead of it.
- Attach the Spiral Dough Hook and Breaker Bar. Add the water mixture (fig liquor and black garlic slurry included), then the whisked dry flours (with the vital wheat gluten in them). Mix at a low speed for 2-3 minutes until no dry flour remains, then rest the dough in the bowl with the splash guard closed for at least 30 minutes.
- Add the levain and mix at a low speed to build structure, then add the salt once the levain is in. Finish at a low-to-medium speed and stop at medium gluten development. Do not mix to full development.
- Figs are a sturdy inclusion, so add them with the pepper for a short, final low-speed mix. Walnuts are delicate and always go in by hand later.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_05_mixing.jpg" alt="Dark, speckled dough in a spiral mixer bowl with fig pieces and cracked pepper visible" /></figure>

### 4. Bulk fermentation with folds (about 4-5 hours at 70-73F / 21-23C)

Keep the dough covered at room temperature.

**Hand-mixed dough:** do a gentle set of stretch-and-folds at 30 minutes (plain). At 1 hour, spread the dough out, scatter the figs and pepper over it, and fold it up to lock them in. At 1.5 hours, repeat with the walnuts, folding by hand. Give a last gentle fold at 2 hours, then leave it alone.

**Machine-mixed dough:** the figs are already in. Do folds at 30, 60, and 90 minutes, folding the walnuts in by hand at the 60-minute fold, then leave it alone.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_06_fold_figs.jpg" alt="Hands folding quartered figs into a dark, speckled dough during a stretch-and-fold" /></figure>

**How to tell it's ready:** with the dough at about 76F, aim for roughly a **40-60% increase in volume**. This number is tied to dough temperature, not fixed. In a warmer kitchen (78F+ dough or room), stop closer to 40% and expect bulk to take 3-4 hours, because the fig sugars speed fermentation. In a cooler kitchen (65-68F), let it go closer to 60%, which may take 5-6 hours. Look for a domed, slightly jiggly surface, bubbles along the edges, and a poke that springs back slowly. If you use a pH meter, the dough at the end of bulk reads roughly **4.5-4.9** (readings drift with sugary inclusions, so lean on the visual cues).

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_07_bulk_done.jpg" alt="Bulk-fermented dough in a clear container, domed and bubbly, with fig pieces visible through the side" /></figure>

### 5. Shape and cold proof

Turn the dough out onto an unfloured surface, pre-shape into a round, and rest 20-30 minutes. Shape into a tight boule, tucking in any figs sitting on the surface (they scorch). Place seam-side up in a banneton dusted with rice flour. Cover and refrigerate at about 38-40F (3-4C) for **10-16 hours**. Dough pH just before baking is typically around **4.1-4.5**, though it varies with flour and inclusions.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_08_shaped.jpg" alt="A tight, dark boule seam-side up in a rice-floured banneton" /></figure>

### 6. Bake

Preheat the Dutch oven (lid on) in the oven at **460F / 238C** for 45-60 minutes. Ovens cycle and overshoot their setpoint, and Dutch oven lid knobs have different temperature ratings, so check your pot's rated maximum before going higher than this. Turn the cold dough out onto parchment, score with a single deep cross or a curved slash, and lower it into the pot.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_09_scored.jpg" alt="A scored boule on parchment with a curved slash, ready for the Dutch oven" /></figure>

Bake with the lid on for **20 minutes**, reducing the oven to 450F / 232C. Remove the lid and bake **20-25 minutes** more, until the crust is a deep mahogany. Because figs and black garlic add sugar, this loaf browns fast. If the top is darkening too quickly, tent it loosely with foil. The internal temperature should read about 205-210F (96-99C).

### 7. Cool

Cool on a rack for at least 2 hours before slicing. The crumb needs time to set, and the black garlic and fig flavors round out. It is even better the next day.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_10_crumb.jpg" alt="A sliced loaf showing a dark, jammy crumb studded with fig pieces and walnuts" /></figure>

## Alternatives and Improvements

- **Add white miso (untested):** stir 12 g of white miso into the dough water with the black garlic, and keep the salt at 9 g instead of 10 g. Miso deepens the umami and plays nicely with the black garlic. Contains soy.
- **Earl Grey fig soak (untested):** steep a teaspoon of loose-leaf Earl Grey in the 120 g of hot water for the figs, strain, and use the tea-infused liquor as above. The bergamot should lift the figs. Keep it light; strong tea can read as bitter.
- **Fig and Gorgonzola (untested):** fold 80 g of crumbled Gorgonzola in with the walnuts (or in place of them). A classic fig-and-blue-cheese pairing. Contains milk.
- **Orange lift:** a few drops of orange emulsion in the dough water brightens the figs. Start small and taste.
- **More rye:** swap another 25-50 g of the bread flour for dark rye for a denser, earthier loaf, and add a splash of water, because rye is thirsty.

## Make It Yours

Fig Leaf Rag is a sturdy dough with a lot of personality, and a few dials change its character more than the rest.

**Fig quantity (currently 20% of flour weight).** Try 15% for a more bread-forward loaf, or 25-30% for a fruit-loaf feel. Watch the dough structure as you go up, because more fruit means a weaker network and more sugar feeding the fermentation.

**Black garlic (currently 7%).** At 7% it gives a clear but not overwhelming sweetness. Pull back toward 4-5% if it reads too sweet next to the figs; go up toward 9-10% for a darker, more molasses-like crumb.

**Hydration (currently 72%).** This is the dial with the most room. If the dough feels stiff after mixing, add water 5-10 g at a time. If it feels slack and sticky with the fruit in, handle it with wet hands and resist adding flour. More water generally gives a more open crumb, at the cost of a stickier dough to shape.

**Vital wheat gluten (currently 1.2% of flour).** It is insurance against the fruit and nuts tearing the gluten network. At 2% the loaf holds its shape more firmly; with none, it still bakes, just with a slightly flatter profile and a tighter crumb.

**Inclusion ratio (currently 32% with walnuts, 20% without).** The walnuts are the first thing to cut if the dough is slack at shaping.

**Proof timing.** A longer, colder retard (up to 16 hours) deepens the flavor and makes the dough easier to score. A shorter one gives a milder loaf.

## Ingredient History Story

### Figs: one of the world's oldest crops

The fig (*Ficus carica*) has a good claim to being among the first plants people ever cultivated. Archaeologists working at an early village site in the Jordan Valley have found fig remains that date back roughly 11,000 years, possibly predating the domestication of wheat and barley. Figs are unusual because the best-known cultivated varieties set fruit without pollination, so a farmer could simply plant a cutting from a good tree and get the same tree again.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_11_history_fig.jpg" alt="A fig tree heavy with ripening fruit in a Mediterranean courtyard" /></figure>

The black mission fig took a trip west. As the story is usually told, Franciscan missionaries carried fig cuttings to the California missions starting in the late 1700s, and the variety took the name of the missions that spread it. Dried, the fruit concentrates into the sticky, jammy chew this loaf leans on.

### Black garlic: patience in a cloved bulb

Black garlic is not fermented, despite the name often used for it. Whole heads are held at a low, steady heat and high humidity for weeks, and the sugars and amino acids in the cloves slowly brown through the same kind of Maillard chemistry that colors bread crust. The cloves emerge black, soft, and sweet, with notes of balsamic, molasses, and tamarind.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_12_history_black_garlic.jpg" alt="A head of black garlic with peeled, glossy black cloves on a wooden board" /></figure>

The ingredient was popularized in Asia, especially in Korea and Japan, and reached Western kitchens through chefs in the 2000s. It is a young ingredient in the history of cooking, but it sits comfortably next to very old ones.

### Black pepper: the spice that moved empires

Black pepper (*Piper nigrum*) is native to the Malabar Coast of southwestern India, and it was being traded west long before the Roman Empire. It was so valuable in antiquity that, according to ancient accounts, the Visigoth king Alaric demanded 3,000 pounds of pepper as part of the ransom he asked of Rome in 408 CE.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_13_history_pepper.jpg" alt="Black pepper vines with green and red peppercorn clusters in a tropical setting" /></figure>

Centuries later, the quest to control the pepper trade helped drive Europe's age of exploration. Today it is on every table, and in this loaf a coarse crack of it keeps the figs from tasting like dessert.

### Rye: the weed that became a crop

Rye (*Secale cereale*) began as a weed in wheat and barley fields, and farmers in cooler, harsher regions of Central and Eastern Europe eventually found that it thrived where wheat struggled. It became the backbone of dark breads across northern Europe. At only 5% of the flour here, rye brings an earthy depth and a bit of extra moisture-holding power without taking over.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_14_history_rye.jpg" alt="A field of rye at golden hour, long bearded heads swaying in the wind" /></figure>

### Why "Fig Leaf Rag"?

The name is a pun on "Maple Leaf Rag," Scott Joplin's 1899 piano rag, one of the pieces that made ragtime a national craze. A rag lives on syncopation: a steady, structured left hand with a playful, off-the-beat melody over the top. This loaf works the same way. The country-loaf structure keeps the beat, and the figs, black garlic, and pepper dance on top. And a fig leaf, of course, is the oldest garment in the book.

## Bloopers Section

Two test bakes of this loaf turned up three honest mistakes, and the loaf survived all of them.

**The Missing Gluten.** On the first test bake, the vital wheat gluten stayed in the jar. The loaf came out fine and tasted good, but the gluten is there as insurance for a dough carrying this much fruit and nut, and a heavier hand with the figs could have tested it. The fix: weigh the gluten out first and whisk it into the flours before anything gets wet, so you can't forget it.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_15_blooper_missing_gluten.jpg" alt="A jar of vital wheat gluten still sitting unopened next to a bowl of dough (illustration of the blooper)" /></figure>

**The Whole-Fig Craters.** The figs also went in unquartered on the first bake. The loaf still came out fine, but big chunks of fig make for uneven bites and weaken the dough around them. The fix: stem and quarter every fig, and pat them dry.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_16_blooper_whole_figs.jpg" alt="A sliced loaf with large whole-fig chunks in the crumb (illustration of the blooper)" /></figure>

**The Extra Splash.** On the second test bake, 20 g more water than written went into the dough. The dough handled it without complaint and the loaf actually rose a bit better. It's a good reminder that hydration is a dial in this recipe, not a wall. The fix: weigh the water to the gram and, if you want to experiment, change it on purpose.

<figure><img src="/images/fig-leaf-rag/fig-leaf-rag_17_blooper_extra_water.jpg" alt="A measuring pitcher overflowing slightly into a bowl of dough on a scale (illustration of the blooper)" /></figure>

Every one of these is a normal part of learning a dough, and the loaf kept its good humor through all of them.
