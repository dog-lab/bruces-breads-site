## Step-by-Step Tutorial Script

### 1. Feed your starter

The night before (or the morning of, if your starter is fast), feed it at a 1:1:1 ratio -- equal parts starter, flour, and water by weight. In summer heat, a fed starter can peak in as little as 4-6 hours instead of the usual 8-12, so keep an eye on it rather than walking away for the whole afternoon. You want it bubbly, roughly doubled, and still slightly domed on top -- once it starts sinking in the middle, it's past peak and the acidity will start working against you. If you have a pH meter handy, a ripe starter typically reads somewhere in the **4.0-4.5** range, though the exact number shifts with your feeding ratio and flour -- treat the dome-and-jiggle test as your primary signal and pH as a nice-to-have second opinion.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_03_starter_peak.jpg" alt="Starter at peak, bubbly and doubled, dome slightly convex before flattening" /></figure>

### 2. Autolyse

Combine the bread flour, spelt, einkorn, and water (reserve nothing) in a bowl. Mix just until no dry flour remains -- it'll look shaggy and rough, not smooth. Cover and let it rest 30-45 minutes (up to 90-120 minutes if your schedule allows -- beyond that, you're not gaining much more). This gives the flour time to fully hydrate and lets enzymes start breaking things down before you add starter and salt, which means less manual mixing work later. Because einkorn and spelt hydrate a little slower than bread flour, don't shortchange this rest -- it's doing real work here.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_04_autolyse.jpg" alt="Dough after autolyse, before starter is mixed in, shaggy and rough" /></figure>

### 3. Mixing

Add your active starter to the autolysed dough first, work it in, and only add the salt once the starter is incorporated. From here, pick the method that matches your kitchen setup. Either way, you're aiming for a **desired dough temperature (DDT) of roughly 75-78°F (24-26°C)** right at the end of mixing -- adjust your water temperature going in if your kitchen runs hot or cold, since that target is what keeps the bulk fermentation timing below on track.

**Manual (hand-mixing)**

Squeeze and fold the starter into the dough by hand -- the "pincer" method works well here: cut through the dough with your fingers spread like a claw, over and over, until the starter is fully distributed. Then sprinkle in the salt and continue with slap-and-folds or firm folding in the bowl until no dry bits remain and the dough starts to smooth out slightly. This usually takes 3-5 minutes of active work. Don't expect full gluten development at this stage -- light-to-medium development is normal and expected, since the stretch-and-folds during bulk fermentation will finish the job. Spelt and einkorn are actually *better suited* to hand mixing than machine mixing, since their weaker gluten is easy to blow past by machine before you even notice.

**Ooni Halo Pro Spiral Mixer**

Attach the Spiral Dough Hook and Breaker Bar. Add the flours to the bowl first, then pour in the water. Mix at low speed (roughly 15-20% / approximately 90-100 RPM) for 2-3 minutes, until no dry flour remains. Then let the dough rest in the bowl with the splash guard closed for 30-45 minutes -- this rest *is* the autolyse for this method, so there's no separate autolyse step ahead of it. Add the starter and mix at low speed for 1-2 minutes to work it in, then add the salt and continue at low-to-medium speed until it's fully incorporated. Finish with a short final mix at roughly medium speed for 30-60 seconds, aiming for medium gluten development. Resist the urge to mix further: with spelt and einkorn in the mix, the spiral mixer develops gluten fast, and these grains have much less tolerance for over-mixing than bread flour alone. Stop while the dough still feels slightly tacky and short, not silky.

*No mixer on hand? Hand-mixing works perfectly well for this dough -- see the manual method above.*

### 4. Bulk fermentation & folds

Cover the dough and let it begin bulk fermentation. At a moderate room temperature (~70-73°F / 21-23°C), this stage typically runs 4.5-6 hours; in summer conditions (75-82°F / 24-28°C) with a dough mixed to the DDT above, expect it to move noticeably faster -- commonly 3.5-5 hours -- so check the dough by feel and appearance rather than the clock alone, especially since a warmer dough needs to come out of bulk at a *lower* percentage rise than a cooler one. Perform 4 sets of stretch-and-folds, spaced 30 minutes apart, over the first two hours.

Fold in the walnuts and craisins during the *second* fold, once the dough has some structure to hold onto them -- folding them in too early crushes the walnuts and lets the craisins tear through a weaker dough.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_05_fold.jpg" alt="Mid-fold, hands stretching and folding the dough, dough smoothness increasing" /></figure>

You're looking for the dough to grow by roughly 40-60% in volume, feel airy and jiggly when you nudge the bowl, and hold together with visible strength when you do a fold -- not the closer-to-100% rise you might see with an all-bread-flour dough at the same DDT, since the ancient grains and inclusions both slightly dampen the rise. By the end of bulk, dough pH is typically somewhere in the **4.5-5.0** range -- again, a secondary check behind the visual and tactile cues.

**Summer tip:** if your kitchen runs hot and the dough is moving faster than you can keep up with, move the bowl to the coolest spot in your kitchen (not the fridge yet) between folds, or shorten the gap between folds to 20 minutes instead of 30.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_06_proof_comparison.jpg" alt="Side-by-side comparison of properly proofed dough versus over-proofed, deflated dough" /></figure>

### 5. Shaping

Turn the dough out onto a lightly floured surface. Shape it into a tight boule (or batard, if splitting into two loaves), building surface tension by dragging the dough toward you against the counter in a few short pulls. You want a smooth, taut skin with no tears -- the inclusions may create small bumps under the surface, which is normal.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_07_shaped.jpg" alt="Shaped dough showing surface tension, just before the banneton" /></figure>

Place the shaped dough seam-side up into a well-floured banneton.

### 6. Proofing

You have two reasonable paths here:

- **Room temperature proof:** 1-2 hours at room temp, checking with the poke test (press a floured finger in -- it should spring back slowly, leaving a slight indent).
- **Cold retard (recommended, especially in summer):** 30-45 minutes at room temp to take the initial edge off, then straight into the fridge for 8-16 hours. This slows fermentation to a manageable pace, deepens flavor, and makes the dough much easier to score cleanly, since a warm, over-relaxed dough tends to deflate under the blade. By the time you're ready to bake, dough pH has usually dropped further, into roughly the **4.0-4.5** range.

### 7. Scoring & baking

Preheat your Dutch oven (empty, lid on) at 450-475°F (230-245°C) for 45-60 minutes -- check your specific Dutch oven and lid knob's rated maximum before going any higher, since most enameled cast iron caps out in this range and home ovens routinely overshoot their dial setting anyway. While it heats, turn your dough out onto parchment, dust off excess flour, and score with one confident, decisive cut about ½ inch deep.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_08_scored.jpg" alt="Scored dough, top-down view showing the score pattern" /></figure>

Carefully lower the dough (on its parchment) into the hot Dutch oven, cover, and bake at 450°F (230°C) for 20 minutes covered, then remove the lid and bake another 20-25 minutes uncovered, until deeply golden brown. Watch the craisins near the surface in the last 10 minutes -- their sugar content means they can catch and darken faster than the crust around them; tent loosely with foil if they're browning too quickly.

### 8. Cooling

This is the hardest step: let the loaf cool completely on a wire rack, at least 2 hours, before slicing. Cutting into a hot loaf lets steam escape too early and leaves you with a gummy crumb -- especially relevant here, since the moisture pulled by the walnuts and craisins needs that cooling time to fully redistribute.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_09_crumb.jpg" alt="Crumb shot, fully cooled and sliced, showing walnut and craisin distribution" /></figure>

## Alternatives and Improvements

- **Swap craisins for dried tart cherries.** Same lamination-friendly texture and similar moisture behavior, but a sharper, less relentlessly sweet tartness that plays differently against the walnuts -- worth trying if you find the craisin version leans too sweet for your taste.
- **Add orange zest to the dough.** A tablespoon or two mixed in with the starter at the mixing stage brightens the whole loaf and turns it into a fairly classic walnut-cranberry-orange combination without touching the hydration.
- **Try pecans instead of walnuts.** Pecans are softer, sweeter, and less assertively bitter than walnuts -- a gentler match for the craisins if you want the fruit to lead rather than share the spotlight.
- **Push the whole-grain percentage higher.** Bumping spelt or einkorn up and bread flour down (say, to 40% combined ancient grain) makes for a heartier, more rustic crumb, but expect to add a touch more water, since both grains are thirstier than bread flour, and expect a slightly faster bulk fermentation.
- **Build with a stiff levain instead of your standard starter.** A stiffer, lower-hydration levain ferments more slowly and produces a milder, less acidic tang, which lets the walnut and dried-fruit flavors come through with less competition from sourness -- a good project once you're comfortable maintaining a second starter culture alongside your regular one.

## Ingredient History Story

### Bread flour: the modern workhorse

Bread flour as we know it is a relatively recent invention, even though wheat itself has been cultivated for over 10,000 years. What makes bread flour distinct -- its high protein content, typically 12-14% -- is the product of both selective breeding of hard wheat varieties and industrial milling processes that emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Hard red winter and spring wheats, grown across the American and Canadian plains, became the backbone of commercial bread flour precisely because their protein structure formed strong, elastic gluten networks capable of trapping gas and holding the open, airy crumb that became the hallmark of Western sandwich and artisan bread alike.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_10_history_breadflour.jpg" alt="Wheat field across the American plains" /></figure>

Before that standardization, bakers worked with whatever local wheat they had, and bread was far more regionally variable -- denser, often flatter, shaped by whatever gluten strength the local grain happened to offer. The rise of bread flour as a distinct, purchasable category effectively gave home and professional bakers a reliable, predictable canvas, which is part of why so many modern sourdough recipes -- including the bulk of this loaf's structure -- lean on it as their foundation.

### Spelt: the Old World cousin

Spelt (*Triticum spelta*) is one of wheat's oldest cultivated relatives, with archaeological evidence of its cultivation stretching back roughly 5,000-6,000 years across Central Europe and parts of the Near East. Unlike modern bread wheat, spelt is a "hulled" grain -- its kernel stays wrapped in a tough husk even after threshing, which historically made it more labor-intensive to process but also more resilient to pests, disease, and poor soil, since that husk offered natural protection.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_11_history_spelt.jpg" alt="Spelt grain field in a Central European agricultural landscape" /></figure>

Spelt held on as a staple grain in parts of Germany, Switzerland, and Austria long after modern bread wheat became dominant elsewhere -- in fact, in regions like Baden-Württemberg, spelt (known locally as *Dinkel*) never really left, and it's still a defining grain in regional breads and beers today. Its gluten is genuinely different in character from modern wheat's: more soluble, more extensible, and less elastic, which is exactly why it behaves the way it does in this dough -- contributing a nutty, slightly sweet flavor and a softer chew, but needing the sturdier bread flour to carry the structural weight.

### Einkorn: the oldest living wheat

If spelt is wheat's old cousin, einkorn (*Triticum monococcum*) is its ancient ancestor. It's widely considered the oldest domesticated wheat species, with cultivation traced back roughly 10,000 years to the Fertile Crescent -- the region spanning parts of modern-day Turkey, Syria, and Iraq where agriculture itself is believed to have first taken hold. Genetic and archaeological evidence, including grains found at Neolithic sites, place einkorn at the very root of wheat's domestication story, before later hybridization events gave rise to the durum and bread wheats that dominate today.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_12_history_einkorn.jpg" alt="Close-up of einkorn wheat grain heads, ancient wheat variety" /></figure>

Einkorn never became a major commercial crop, largely because it's low-yielding compared to modern wheat and its kernels are notoriously difficult to thresh. But it persisted in small pockets of mountainous farming communities across Europe and the Near East, valued for its distinct, almost buttery flavor and its resistance to disease. Its gluten is famously fragile -- some bakers describe it as having "gluten quality" without "gluten quantity" -- which is precisely why this recipe treats it with a light touch, letting bread flour do the structural work while einkorn contributes flavor complexity and a piece of wheat's deepest history to every slice.

### Walnuts: millennia of cultivation

Walnuts have been cultivated for so long that their exact wild origin is debated, but the strongest evidence points to Central Asia -- the mountainous regions stretching from Kyrgyzstan into western China -- as the tree's ancestral home, with cultivation and trade spreading walnuts along Silk Road routes into Persia, the Mediterranean, and eventually the rest of Europe. The Romans in particular prized walnuts and are credited with spreading walnut cultivation widely across their empire; the tree's Latin genus name, *Juglans*, references Jupiter, reflecting the high regard the fruit held in Roman culture.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_13_history_walnut.jpg" alt="Walnut orchard, close-up of walnuts still on the tree" /></figure>

Walnuts arrived in North America with European settlers and eventually found ideal growing conditions in California's Central Valley, which today produces the vast majority of the world's commercial walnut supply. In bread, walnuts bring more than crunch -- their natural oils lend a subtle bitterness that balances sweeter inclusions, which is exactly the role they play alongside the craisins here.

### Cranberries (craisins): a North American native

Unlike the other ingredients in this loaf, cranberries are a genuinely North American native -- they were never imported from elsewhere, having grown wild in the bogs of the northeastern United States and eastern Canada long before European contact. Indigenous peoples of the region used cranberries not just as food but for dyes and in medicinal preparations, and early European settlers adopted the fruit quickly, associating it with harvest and autumn much as it remains today.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_14_history_cranberry.jpg" alt="Cranberry bog in the northeastern United States" /></figure>

Dried, sweetened cranberries -- craisins -- are a far more recent invention, developed commercially in the late 20th century as a way to make the notoriously tart fruit more broadly palatable outside of sauce or juice form. In this loaf, their concentrated tartness and chew play against the richness of the walnuts and the nutty depth of the ancient grains -- a distinctly New World ingredient folded into a dough built on some of the Old World's oldest grains.

### A world tour in one loaf

Trace the ingredients in this bread back to their roots and you end up with a surprisingly literal map: wheat's ancient birthplace in the Fertile Crescent, its old-world persistence in the mountain villages of Central Europe, its modern industrial form on the American plains, walnuts carried along the Silk Road and later planted in California soil, and cranberries that never left North America at all. Every slice is, in a very real sense, a few thousand years and several continents compressed into one loaf.

## Bloopers

Every baker who's made this loaf more than once has a story. Here are a few of the classics.

**The Pancake Loaf.** Left the dough to bulk ferment on the counter on a 90°F day and walked away for "just an hour." Came back to a dough that had blown well past peak and spread out flat the second it hit the banneton. The fix: in true summer heat, set a timer and check every 30 minutes -- this dough moves fast enough to outrun your afternoon.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_15_blooper_pancake.jpg" alt="A flat, pancake-like loaf on a baking sheet" /></figure>

**The Scorched Bottom.** Preheated the Dutch oven directly on the bottom rack for the full preheat, no parchment, and ended up with a bottom crust closer to charcoal than bread. The fix: parchment paper isn't optional here, and a middle oven rack keeps the bottom from getting too much direct heat.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_16_blooper_scorched.jpg" alt="A loaf with a scorched, overly dark bottom crust" /></figure>

**The Sunken Middle.** Went a little too generous with the walnuts and craisins -- nearly double the recipe amount -- and the extra weight and moisture were more than the gluten (already working overtime with the ancient grains) could support. The fix: this recipe's 15%/15% inclusion ratio isn't arbitrary -- it's chosen for what this particular flour blend can actually carry without the structure giving out.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_17_blooper_sunken.jpg" alt="A collapsed, dense wedge of bread with a tight, gummy interior" /></figure>

**The Great Starter Escape.** Fed the starter right before a hot afternoon, put the lid on snugly, and came back to a jar that had erupted over the counter. The fix: in summer, leave the lid loose or use a rubber band to mark the starting level so you can see how close it's getting before it stages a breakout.

<figure><img src="/images/craisin-crunch/craisin-crunch_18_blooper_starter_escape.jpg" alt="A mason jar with dried starter overflow crusted around the rim and down the side" /></figure>

Every one of these is a completely normal part of learning to read your dough -- not a sign you're doing it wrong. Bread has a way of teaching you exactly what it needs, one slightly-too-flat or slightly-too-dark loaf at a time.
