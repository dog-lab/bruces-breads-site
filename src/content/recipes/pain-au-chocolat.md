## Step-by-Step Tutorial Script

### 1. Feed your levain, the day before

Feed your stiff starter so it peaks roughly 8-12 hours before you plan to mix -- see the Starter Guide: Starter Types & Flours if you need a refresher on maintaining one. A stiff starter takes longer to ripen than a loose one, so plan an overnight feed for a morning mix, or a morning feed for an evening mix. Look for the dome to stop rising and just begin to flatten and crack slightly. If tracking pH, ripe stiff starter for this recipe typically reads somewhere around **pH 4.0-4.5**.

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_03_starter_peak.jpg" alt="A jar of stiff sourdough starter at peak ripeness" /></figure>

### 2. Mix the détrempe

Combine the cold milk with the torn, ripe stiff starter in a large bowl and work it in by hand for about 5 minutes until mostly softened. Add the bread flour, pastry flour, and sugar, and mix until no dry flour remains. Add the salt and the softened butter (25g -- this is the small amount that goes directly into the dough, not the butter block), and mix or knead just until the dough comes together into a smooth, cohesive mass -- you're aiming for a dough with enough structure to hold together through lamination, not full gluten development the way you would for an enriched loaf like Well Bread. Over-mixing the détrempe makes it fight back during rolling, so stop as soon as it's smooth rather than pushing for a windowpane.

**Desired dough temperature:** target **68-72°F (20-22°C)** at the end of mixing -- noticeably cooler than the site's usual enriched-dough range, since this dough needs to stay firm and cold-workable all the way through lamination. Use cold milk and, if your kitchen runs warm, consider chilling your flour beforehand.

Shape the détrempe into a flat rectangle, wrap tightly, and refrigerate for at least 1 hour (up to overnight) before laminating -- this rest relaxes the gluten and brings the whole mass to a consistent, workable temperature.

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_04_detrempe.jpg" alt="The détrempe, mixed and shaped into a flat rectangle before its chill" /></figure>

### 3. Prepare the butter block

While the détrempe chills, pound and roll the cold European-style butter between two sheets of parchment into a flat, even square or rectangle roughly two-thirds the size of your détrempe rectangle. Aim for a consistent thickness throughout -- a butter block with thick and thin spots laminates unevenly, and the thin spots are exactly where the layers will tear during rolling. Chill the butter block until firm but still pliable -- cold enough to hold its shape, not so cold it cracks when you try to fold it.

### 4. Encase and laminate

Roll the chilled détrempe out to about twice the size of the butter block, and encase the butter using an envelope fold (also called a "corner fold") -- Build Block Butter's default encasing method -- folding all four edges of the détrempe up and over the butter block like wrapping a package, sealing the seams well so no butter escapes during rolling. (A book fold -- rolling the dough long and folding both ends in to meet at a center crease over the butter -- works just as well for this initial encasing if you prefer it; which fold you use here is genuinely up to you.)

Roll the encased dough out into a long rectangle, then fold it in thirds like a letter (a single fold). Wrap and chill for 30-45 minutes. Repeat this roll-and-single-fold sequence two more times, chilling fully between each -- three single folds total is the site's default fold schedule for this dough (one double fold plus two single folds also works, if you prefer that sequence; either gets you to the same number of distinct butter layers).

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_05_lamination_fold.jpg" alt="Hands mid-fold, laminating the dough into thin, even layers" /></figure>

Between folds, watch for two things: the dough should still feel cold and firm when you unwrap it (if it's gone soft, give it another 15 minutes in the fridge before rolling), and you shouldn't see streaks of raw butter breaking through the dough's surface (a sign the butter got too warm relative to the dough -- a longer chill next time helps).

### 5. Roll to final thickness and cut

After the last fold and a final chill, roll the laminated dough out to its final thickness -- about **3-4mm**, per Build Block Butter's standard. Trim the ragged edges (compressed, unlaminated dough that won't puff properly) before cutting.

Cut the sheet into rectangles roughly **4 x 3 inches** each. Keep your cuts clean and straight -- a dragging or dull blade seals the cut edge shut and blocks that side of the pastry from puffing during baking, which shows up as a flat, dense edge next to otherwise flaky layers.

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_06_cut_rectangles.jpg" alt="A laminated dough sheet, trimmed and cut into rectangles ready for shaping" /></figure>

### 6. Shape and fill

This is the step that makes a pain au chocolat what it is, and it's a genuinely small diversion from working with Build Block Butter -- here's the exact shaping technique this recipe uses:

- Take one 4 x 3 inch rectangle and roll it out gently to about **4 x 5 inches**, just enough to give yourself a little extra length to work with -- don't press hard enough to compress the layers you just built.
- Lay one chocolate baton across the dough, a short distance in from one edge, and roll the dough over it once.
- Lay the second chocolate baton just past the fold, and continue rolling the dough the rest of the way around it, finishing with the seam underneath the pastry.
- Brush the outside with the **egg white wash**. This recipe calls for egg white specifically, rather than a whole egg or egg-and-milk wash -- it browns cleanly without the deeper, eggier color a yolk-heavy wash gives a lean-ish laminated dough, which keeps the finished color closer to a bakery-case pain au chocolat.

**Cinnamon-sugar variant:** after rolling the rectangle out to 4 x 5 inches (before adding either baton), brush that exposed surface with egg white wash first, then sprinkle it evenly with the cinnamon sugar. From there, roll in the batons exactly as above -- first baton, one roll, second baton, finish rolling -- so the cinnamon sugar ends up laminated into the spiral rather than just dusted on top. Finish with a second, plain egg white wash over the outside before baking, same as the standard version.

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_07_shaped.jpg" alt="A shaped, egg-washed pain au chocolat before its final proof" /></figure>

Place shaped pastries seam-side down on a parchment-lined baking sheet, spaced a couple of inches apart to allow for spread during proofing and oven spring.

### 7. Final proof

Proof at **75-80°F (24-27°C)** -- warmer than a lean loaf's proof, since this range keeps the butter workable-soft without melting it, per Build Block Butter's standard. Expect **1.5-3 hours**, watching for the pastry to look visibly puffy and jiggle slightly when the tray is nudged, with the individual layers starting to show some separation at the cut edges. Avoid proofing anywhere warm enough to soften the butter past workable -- if butter starts visibly leaking out at this stage, your proofing spot is too warm.

### 8. Bake

Preheat the oven to **400-425°F (205-220°C)**. Bake for the first 10 minutes at that temperature to drive a strong burst of oven spring and set the layers, then drop the oven to **375°F (190°C)** for the remainder of the bake. Total bake time runs **18-22 minutes** for pastries this size -- watch for deep golden-brown color and visibly defined, flaky layers along the cut edges. Tent loosely with foil if the tops are coloring faster than the inside can catch up.

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_08_baked_tray.jpg" alt="A tray of freshly baked pain au chocolat, deep golden and visibly flaky" /></figure>

### 9. Cool

Cool on the tray for 5 minutes, then transfer to a wire rack. These are genuinely best within an hour or two of baking, while the layers are still audibly crisp -- laminated pastry doesn't hold its texture past a day the way a crusty lean loaf does, so plan to share or freeze what you won't eat same-day (baked and cooled pastries freeze well; reheat frozen in a 350°F oven for about 10 minutes to recrisp).

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_09_crumb.jpg" alt="A pain au chocolat sliced open, showing the flaky layers and two chocolate batons in cross-section" /></figure>

## Alternatives and Improvements

- **A single baton instead of two:** for a lighter chocolate hit, use just one baton per pastry, centered, rather than the two-baton roll described above -- a genuine bakery variation, not a shortcut.
- **Dark, milk, or a mix:** the 55-60% cacao batons above are a starting point, not a rule -- a milk chocolate baton softens the bitterness for a sweeter tooth, and using one of each per pastry (one dark, one milk) is a real thing some bakeries do.
- **Almond pain au chocolat:** brush the inside with a thin layer of almond cream (frangipane) before adding the batons, and top the finished, cooled pastry with sliced almonds and a dusting of powdered sugar -- a heavier, more dessert-leaning riff that borrows from pain aux amandes.
- **Orange zest in the détrempe:** fold a teaspoon of finely grated orange zest into the détrempe during mixing for a citrus note that plays well against dark chocolate -- a small, low-risk way to make this recipe your own without touching the lamination itself.
- **A flakier, more open crumb:** if you want more visible, separated layers at the cost of a slightly less structured pastry, drop to two single folds instead of three -- fewer, thicker butter layers puff more dramatically but hold their shape a little less predictably.

## Make It Yours

This recipe inherits its core levers from Build Block Butter itself, plus a couple that are specific to how this particular pastry gets filled and finished.

**Chocolate ratio (currently 2 batons, ~20g per pastry).** This is the most direct lever here, and the most forgiving to adjust. More chocolate (a third baton, or larger batons) pushes this firmly toward a dessert pastry and adds real weight and moisture to the center, which can make the innermost layers a touch softer than the outer ones -- not a flaw, just a different texture profile. Less chocolate (a single, smaller baton) keeps more of the pastry's identity in the lamination itself rather than the filling, closer to how a plain croissant eats.

**Fold count (currently three single folds, Build Block Butter's default).** Fewer folds (two single folds instead of three) means fewer, thicker butter layers -- the finished pastry puffs more dramatically and has a more rustic, uneven layer structure. More folds (a fourth single fold) means thinner, more numerous layers and a tighter, more uniform crumb, but pushes your total butter-to-dough ratio thinner across more layers, which can read as slightly less rich per bite even though the total butter hasn't changed. Push past four folds on this dough's butter percentage and the layers can start to merge rather than staying distinct -- more isn't automatically better past a certain point.

**Egg white vs. whole egg wash.** This recipe defaults to egg white for a cleaner, paler bakery-style finish. Swapping in a whole egg (or egg plus a splash of milk) gives a deeper golden-brown color and a slightly more matte, less glossy sheen -- a legitimate stylistic choice, not a correction. If you prefer a shinier, richer-looking crust, that swap is a simple, low-risk way to get it.

**Proof timing.** A shorter proof (toward the 1.5-hour end) gives a firmer, more distinctly layered pastry with strong oven spring; a longer proof (toward 3 hours) softens the layers slightly and gives a more tender, less shatter-crisp bite. Watch the butter rather than the clock either way -- if it's visibly softening or leaking, the proofing spot is running warmer than this dough wants, regardless of how long it's actually been.

## Ingredient History Story

### A Viennese import that became unmistakably French

The story of pain au chocolat is really the story of the croissant's shape -- the crescent-moon roll widely (if imprecisely) traced to Vienna's *kipferl*, a curved bread that predates the French pastry by centuries. When Viennese-style baking took hold in Paris in the 19th century, French bakers adapted the technique and, critically, gave it the lamination -- the folded, buttered layering -- that defines the whole viennoiserie category today, "viennoiserie" itself literally meaning "Viennese-style" while describing what became a thoroughly French tradition. Pain au chocolat is that same laminated dough, cut into a rectangle and wrapped around chocolate instead of rolled into a crescent -- a variation that appears to have emerged alongside the croissant itself, once bakers realized the dough that made a good crescent roll also made an excellent chocolate-filled pastry.

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_10_history_bakery.jpg" alt="A traditional French bakery display of laminated pastries, croissants and rectangular chocolate pastries side by side" /></figure>

### An interesting story, worth knowing: what to call it

Ask for this pastry in Paris and you'll get "pain au chocolat." Ask in much of southwestern France -- Bordeaux, Toulouse, and the surrounding region -- and locals are just as likely to say "chocolatine." The divide is real, well-documented, and genuinely contested: it shows up in regional surveys, has been the subject of half-serious petitions and local pride campaigns, and even prompted a French lawmaker to (unsuccessfully) propose settling it by national vote. Linguists have traced "chocolatine" toward Gascon and Occitan-influenced regional French, while "pain au chocolat" reflects the more standardized Parisian naming that spread with the pastry nationally -- but neither side has definitively won, and the debate remains genuinely alive rather than settled history. This recipe uses "pain au chocolat" simply because that's the more widely recognized name outside France, not as a vote in the argument.

### The fold, not the flour, is what makes this pastry

Unlike most of the recipes on this site, this one's defining story lives almost entirely in technique rather than any single ingredient's geography -- the wheat and butter behind it share the same broad European agricultural history as brioche and the site's other enriched doughs (see Well Bread's history section for that fuller story). What's genuinely distinct about pain au chocolat and the viennoiserie family it belongs to is the lamination process itself: repeatedly folding a butter block into dough to build dozens of thin, alternating layers, so that as the pastry bakes, water trapped in each butter layer flashes to steam and physically pushes the layers apart. That's a mechanical, technique-driven kind of flakiness -- genuinely different from what makes a good crusty loaf or a tender brioche crumb, and worth understanding on its own terms rather than folding it into an ingredient's backstory.

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_11_history_lamination.jpg" alt="A cross-section of laminated dough showing dozens of thin, distinct butter layers" /></figure>

### A quick word on the rolling pin

The long, straight, handle-less French-style rolling pin favored for lamination work isn't just a stylistic choice -- its even, cylindrical shape (no tapered ends, no handles to create uneven pressure) is specifically suited to rolling out large, even sheets of dough without introducing the thick-and-thin variation a handled pin can leave behind. That evenness matters more here than almost anywhere else on this site, since an inconsistent roll translates directly into inconsistent lamination.

### How this fits into that lineage

Every pain au chocolat that comes out of a home oven is standing on the same technical foundation as a Parisian bakery-case croissant -- cold butter, folded dough, patient chilling -- just cut into a different shape and given something to wrap around. The chocolate is the twist; the lamination underneath it is the actual inheritance.

## Bloopers

Lamination punishes impatience more than almost anything else on this site -- here's what that looks like in practice.

**The butter-everywhere disaster.** Rushed a fold without letting the dough chill fully, and watched streaks of raw butter break straight through the dough on the very next roll. Kept going anyway, out of stubbornness, and ended up with a greasy sheet that laminated into muddy, uneven layers instead of distinct ones. The fix: respect the chill time between every single fold, no exceptions, even when the process is already taking most of an afternoon.

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_12_blooper_butter_leak.jpg" alt="A dough sheet with visible streaks of raw butter breaking through the surface" /></figure>

**The flat, dense brick.** Cut the rectangles with a dull paring knife that dragged instead of sliced, sealing every edge shut without realizing it. The pastries went into the oven looking promising and came out looking almost exactly the same shape they went in -- no puff, no visible layers, just a dense, chewy loss of an afternoon's lamination work. The fix: a genuinely sharp blade, and a quick check that the cut edges show distinct, separated layers before shaping.

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_13_blooper_flat.jpg" alt="A flat, unpuffed pastry straight out of the oven, showing no visible layer separation" /></figure>

**The chocolate lava tray.** Didn't seal the seam underneath firmly enough, and the pastry slowly unrolled itself during proofing, then finished the job in the oven -- chocolate pooled and scorched onto the parchment in a dark, bitter puddle around an otherwise decent-looking pastry. The fix: a firm press to seal the seam before setting it down, and a check partway through proofing that nothing's started to unfurl.

<figure><img src="/images/pain-au-chocolat/pain-au-chocolat_14_blooper_chocolate.jpg" alt="A baked pastry with scorched chocolate pooled on the parchment around it" /></figure>

Every one of these is a normal part of learning lamination -- nobody gets a laminated dough's temperature and timing exactly right on the first attempt, and even a flat or leaky batch still tastes like butter and chocolate, which is a fairly forgiving floor to fall back on.
