## Step-by-Step Tutorial Script

This recipe isn't really for a finished pastry. It's for a *component* -- a laminated dough you build, fold, and freeze ahead, so that whenever the craving hits, the hard part's already done and all that's left is shaping, proofing, and baking. Think of it less like a cake and more like keeping a well-stocked pantry: build a batch on a slow weekend, tuck it in the freezer, and you're two hours from fresh croissants any Tuesday you feel like it.

### 1. Feed Your Starter (Stiff)

Feed your starter at its stiff hydration (see the [Starter vs. Levain](/guides/starter/starter-vs-levain/) and [Starter Types and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours/) guides for how to build and maintain one) so it peaks around the time you plan to mix. Give it more time than a liquid starter needs at the same temperature -- expect closer to **8-12 hours** rather than the 4-8 hours a liquid starter wants. Watch for the dome flattening and cracking slightly, rather than staying loose and bubbly, as your ripeness cue.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_03_starter.jpg" alt="Stiff starter at peak, dome flattened and just starting to crack" /></figure>

<div class="callout"><strong>pH check (optional):</strong> a ripe stiff starter typically falls somewhere in the roughly 3.9-4.4 range -- a representative range, not a precise target. The dome-and-crack visual test is your primary signal.</div>

### 2. Mix the Detrempe

**Soften the stiff starter into the cold milk first.** Tear or break the starter into small, thumb-sized pieces and drop them into the cold milk in your mixing bowl. Let it sit about 5 minutes so the liquid softens the outside of each piece before you add anything else -- this keeps you from working unmixed lumps of stiff starter through the whole dough later.

Then combine with the flours, sugar, salt, the small dough-butter amount, and the insurance yeast if you're using it. Mix only to a smooth, evenly hydrated dough with **light-to-moderate** gluten development -- not the full development other doughs on this site call for. Lamination does the structural work through folding; an over-developed detrempe just fights back harder against rolling later.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_04_mixed.jpg" alt="Detrempe just after mixing -- smooth but only lightly developed" /></figure>

**Desired dough temperature:** cooler than most other doughs on this site -- target **68-72°F (20-22°C)**. Everything about lamination depends on keeping the butter block firm and distinct, and that starts with a cool detrempe.

Salt goes in with everything else here, all at once -- this dough doesn't split salt into a separate step.

### 3. Rest and Chill

Flatten the dough into a rough rectangle, wrap tightly, and refrigerate at least 1 hour (up to overnight). This relaxes the gluten for easier rolling and brings the dough down to match the cold, firm consistency the butter block needs.

### 4. Prepare the Butter Block

Target a fixed footprint rather than eyeballing thickness: **7in x 7in (18cm x 18cm), about ⅓in (0.8cm) thick** for the envelope (four-corner) encasing below, or a more rectangular **7in x 10in (18cm x 25cm), about ¼in (0.6cm) thick** for a letter-style (two-flap) encasing instead.

A clean way to hit that shape exactly: fold a sheet of parchment paper into the target rectangle, creasing all four sides so it holds its form, then place the cold butter inside that folded pocket and pound and roll it from there. The parchment acts as a mold -- the butter spreads to fill the target footprint instead of you measuring and correcting loose butter by eye.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_05_butterblock.jpg" alt="Butter being pounded into shape inside a folded-parchment mold" /></figure>

Once shaped, dust the block lightly with flour on both sides -- this soaks up surface moisture that would otherwise make it tacky against the detrempe. The butter itself needs to be cold but pliable: firm enough to hold its shape, soft enough to roll without cracking into shards. If it cracks, it's too cold; if it smears, it's too warm -- chill it again briefly if it's gotten too soft to handle.

### 5. Laminate

Roll the detrempe out to **14in x 14in (35cm x 35cm)** for the envelope (four-corner) method, or **14in x 10in (35cm x 25cm)** for a letter-style fold-over -- both give enough margin to fully enclose the butter block with a little overlap.

**Envelope encasing (also called a "corner fold") -- this recipe's default:** place the 7x7in butter block in the center of the dough square, rotated so its corners point toward the midpoints of the dough's edges (a diamond orientation), and fold each of the four dough flaps in toward the center to fully enclose it, pinching the seams to seal.

**Letter (two-flap) encasing -- an alternative:** place the 7x10in butter block over one half of the rolled dough rectangle, then fold the bare half over it like closing a letter, pinching the edges to seal.

**A naming note, since this trips people up:** "envelope," "letter," and "book" are really just names for fold *motions*, and different bakers apply them at different points in the process. Some use the book fold only as a later turn on dough that's already had its butter sealed in some other way (fold both ends of the rolled dough in to meet at a center crease, then close it like a book, for four layers instead of the envelope's three); others use that same motion to encase the raw butter block in the first place, exactly like the envelope method does. Both are correct -- which fold you reach for at which stage is genuinely a baker's choice, not a fixed rule. This recipe sticks with the envelope method start to finish, mostly for the sake of one motion to remember, but swapping in a book fold anywhere in the process -- for the initial encasing, a later turn, or both -- changes nothing about the result.

Roll the enclosed package out to an even thickness, then fold: **one double (book) fold plus two single (letter) folds, or three single folds** -- either gives a comparable number of butter layers, so pick whichever schedule feels more manageable in your kitchen. **Chill 30-45 minutes between every fold, no exceptions** -- this is where lamination gets lost if rushed.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_06_laminate.jpg" alt="Mid-fold, dough being turned for the next lamination pass" /></figure>

If the butter starts streaking through the dough instead of staying in a distinct layer -- visible yellow smears rather than a clean marbled edge at a cut corner -- it's warmed too much. Stop and give it a longer chill before continuing rather than pushing through.

### 6. Final Rest -- and This Is Where You Freeze It

After the last fold, wrap the block tightly and either chill at least 1 hour (ideally overnight) if you're baking soon, or **freeze it here** if you're building ahead -- this is the natural pause point for the whole "build now, bake later" idea behind this recipe. Frozen, well-wrapped laminated dough holds well for up to about a month; thaw overnight in the fridge before shaping.

### 7. Shape

Roll the laminated dough to a final thickness of about 3-4mm (⅛in). Cut into triangles for croissants, rectangles for pain au chocolat, or squares for danishes (press a well into the center for the filling so it doesn't puff shut during proofing). Work quickly and keep the dough cold -- a short chill mid-shaping is worth the pause if things start feeling soft or sticky.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_07_shaped.jpg" alt="Shaped croissants and pain au chocolat, ready to proof" /></figure>

### 8. Proof

The trickiest step in the whole process: this dough needs warmth to proof (roughly 75-80°F ambient is ideal) but not so much that the butter melts and the layers collapse into something greasy and dense instead of flaky. Proof until visibly puffy and jiggly, with the cut layers along the edges just beginning to fan apart -- usually **1.5-3 hours** depending on temperature and whether the insurance yeast is in play. In a warm kitchen, a cooler spot (or a turned-off oven with just the light on) is more reliable than the counter.

<div class="callout"><strong>pH check (optional):</strong> proofed laminated dough typically sits in roughly the 4.0-4.5 range -- this dough runs slightly less acidic than a lean loaf, thanks to the milder, more lactic stiff-starter ferment.</div>

### 9. Egg Wash and Bake

Brush with egg wash before baking. Bake hot to start -- **400-425°F (204-218°C)** for the first 10 minutes to build a strong burst of steam that pushes the layers apart -- then, if needed, drop to **375°F (191°C)** to finish baking through without over-browning. Total bake time for croissant-sized pieces runs roughly **18-22 minutes**.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_08_baked.jpg" alt="Finished pastries just out of the oven, deep golden and visibly layered" /></figure>

Doneness here isn't an internal-temperature check the way it is for a loaf -- these are thin and largely hollow. Look for deep golden-brown color on all sides, visibly separated and puffed layers, and a light, hollow sound when the bottom is tapped.

### 10. Cool

Cool on a rack for at least 15-20 minutes before eating so the interior finishes setting. Unlike a yeasted loaf, laminated pastry doesn't benefit from a long cool-down -- the shattery exterior is genuinely best eaten fresh, within a few hours of baking.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_09_crumb.jpg" alt="Cross-section of a finished croissant showing the honeycomb of laminated layers" /></figure>

## Alternatives and Improvements

- **Skip the insurance yeast entirely** once you've got a couple of successful bakes under your belt and trust your stiff starter's timing -- it's there for reliability while you're learning the technique, not a permanent requirement.
- **Try the letter (two-flap) encasing instead of the envelope (four-corner) default** -- it's a slightly faster fold to execute and produces a comparable layer count; worth trying both to see which suits your hands better.
- **Prefer a book fold for the initial encasing instead:** center the butter block on the rolled detrempe rather than placing it diagonally, fold both ends of the dough in to meet over it at a center crease, then fold once more like closing a book. Works just as well as the envelope method for sealing the butter in -- purely a matter of which motion feels more natural in your hands.
- **Push toward a higher bread-flour ratio (80/20 instead of 70/30)** if you want a chewier, more structured final pastry -- the tradeoff is a detrempe that rolls back more stubbornly between folds.
- **Add a touch more sugar (up to 15%)** for a slightly sweeter dough that leans closer to a Danish pastry's character, especially useful if you're planning to fill with fruit or pastry cream rather than chocolate.
- **This dough freezes at any lamination stage, not just after the final fold** -- if your schedule gets interrupted mid-fold, a well-wrapped partially-laminated block holds in the fridge for a day or two, or the freezer longer, without real harm to the finished layers.

## Make It Yours

Once you're comfortable with the basic process, these are the levers that actually shape how this dough behaves -- turn them deliberately, in either direction, rather than treating this formula as fixed.

**Detrempe hydration (currently 45% milk, ~45.5% effective):** pull it back toward 35-40% and you'll get a firmer, easier-to-roll dough that's more forgiving for a first attempt -- but pushed too far, it turns dry and crack-prone during lamination (this dough actually failed at 37% effective hydration before landing here -- see Revision History just below). Push it up past about 48-50% and the dough gets noticeably stickier to handle cold, though the payoff can be a slightly more tender crumb. The 45% mark is a genuinely tested sweet spot, not an arbitrary starting point.

**Revision history:** this formula started at 35% milk (~37% effective hydration) and failed outright on its first real test bake -- dry, crack-prone, and barely proofed. The 45% figure above (~45.5% effective) is the settled result of two rounds of test baking, not a first guess. If a future bake ever runs dry or cracks again, check flour freshness, kitchen humidity, and the butter block's fat content first -- those are the more likely culprits at this point, not the detrempe formula itself. The fuller story behind that first failed bake -- including the rescue brioche that came out of troubleshooting it -- is told in the blog post *Going Stiff: A Starter Experiment in Three Acts*.

**Butter block ratio (currently 50% of detrempe flour weight):** this is the lever with the least room to move. Below roughly 40%, the layers thin out and the pastry starts eating more like an enriched bread than a laminated one. Above roughly 60%, the block gets difficult to manage -- it's more likely to burst through the dough mid-roll, and you'll lose more butter to tearing than you gain in flakiness. Stay within shouting distance of 50% unless you're specifically chasing one of those two effects.

**Fold count and schedule:** more folds (up to a point) mean more, thinner butter layers and a finer, more delicate flake; fewer folds mean fewer, thicker layers and a coarser, more rustic texture -- neither is wrong, they're just different pastries. Past about 5-6 total single-fold equivalents, the layers get thin enough that they start merging back together rather than staying distinct, so there's a real ceiling here, not just diminishing returns.

**Proof temperature and time:** run it cooler and longer (closer to 70°F) for a more controlled, predictable rise with less risk of the butter melting out -- the tradeoff is tying up your kitchen for longer. Run it warmer (closer to 80°F) and you'll proof faster, but you're working a much narrower window before the butter starts to soften past the point of no return. There's real risk on the warm end of this range in a way there isn't for a plain loaf.

## Ingredient History Story

Laminated dough is older than any one pastry built from it, and its story is really a story about a technique traveling and being reinvented, not about a single ingredient's geography.

The earliest documented layered, butter-or-lard pastries come out of Spain in the early 1600s -- cookbooks by Domingo Hernandez de Maceras (1607) and Francisco Martinez Montino, chef to Philip II (1611), both describe fat worked into dough in layers, likely building on earlier Arab and Moorish culinary traditions in the region. The technique crossed into France not long after, where it was formalized rather than merely used: in 1651, Francois Pierre La Varenne published the first explicit description of *tourage* -- encasing a solid block of butter inside dough and repeatedly folding it -- in his influential cookbook *Le Cuisinier Francois*. French pastry chefs, most notably Antonin Careme in the 19th century, refined the method further into something close to the technique described above.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_10_history_lamination.jpg" alt="Seventeenth-century French kitchen scene evoking the codification of lamination technique" /></figure>

**An interesting story:** French culinary lore credits a struggling apprentice baker named Claude Gellee with accidentally inventing laminated pastry in 1612, folding butter into dough to hide the fact that he'd forgotten to add it earlier, while trying to bake bread for his sick father. It's a charming story -- and Gellee himself was a real person, though he went on to fame as a landscape painter rather than a baker -- but there's essentially no historical evidence behind the anecdote beyond its persistence as culinary folklore. Worth knowing, not worth repeating as fact.

The shape most people associate with this technique today -- the crescent -- has an entirely separate origin that didn't involve lamination at all. Vienna's *kipferl*, a crescent-shaped yeasted roll with a documented history stretching back centuries, was an unlaminated bread, closer in spirit to a soft dinner roll than to what comes out of this recipe. It came to Paris in 1839, when Austrian entrepreneur August Zang opened the Boulangerie Viennoise, introducing Parisians to Viennese baking -- the kipferl among it, plus the steam oven that would go on to become standard equipment in French bakeries generally. Zang's bakery was popular and widely imitated, but for decades afterward, Parisian "croissants" were still essentially kipferl: shaped like a crescent, made from a plain yeasted dough.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_11_history_vienna.jpg" alt="Vienna street scene evoking the Boulangerie Viennoise's mid-19th-century Parisian moment" /></figure>

It took until 1915 for those two threads -- France's centuries-old lamination technique and Vienna's crescent shape -- to actually merge. That year, French baker Sylvain Claudius Goy published what's considered the first true croissant recipe: Goy replaced the kipferl's plain, brioche-style dough with a fully laminated one, folding butter through it in layers the way La Varenne had described nearly three centuries earlier. The result -- airy, shattering, distinctly *not* what Zang had been selling -- is essentially the pastry this recipe is built to produce today.

That's really the throughline worth taking from all of it: this technique isn't a French thing or an Austrian thing so much as it's proof that a genuinely good technique tends to find its way to wherever it's needed, arriving decades or centuries after it was first figured out, worked into whatever shape happens to be sitting there waiting for it.

## Bloopers Section

**The butter breakout.** Instead of a clean, marbled cross-section, a cut corner shows solid streaks of yellow running through the dough -- the unmistakable sign the butter warmed up mid-roll and started smearing instead of staying in a distinct layer. The fix: chill immediately, longer than you think you need to, and remember that "it still looks okay" is not the same as "it's still cold enough."

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_12_blooper_smear.jpg" alt="Cut corner of dough showing streaky, smeared butter instead of clean layers" /></figure>

**The flat pancake croissant.** Proofed a little too warm, a little too long -- the shaped crescents spread sideways instead of puffing up, and came out of the oven looking more like laminated flatbread than croissants. The fix: a cooler proofing spot next time, and trust the visible layer-fanning cue over the clock.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_13_blooper_flat.jpg" alt="Flattened, over-proofed croissants that spread instead of rising" /></figure>

**The butter-block blowout.** Mid-roll, the dough splits open along one edge and a slab of butter shoves straight through it -- usually because the block was rolled a hair too big for its footprint, or too cold to bend without cracking. The fix: stick to the target measurements, and give a too-firm block a minute to soften before you fold it in.

<figure><img src="/images/build-block-butter/build-block-butter_14_blooper_burst.jpg" alt="Butter block bursting through a torn section of dough during rolling" /></figure>

None of this means the technique is beyond you -- lamination has one of the steepest early learning curves on this whole site, and every baker who's good at it now has a version of these same three photos somewhere in their own history.
