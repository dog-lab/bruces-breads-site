## Step-by-Step Tutorial Script

### 1. Feed your levain, the day before

Using the site's 1:5:2.5 stiff feed, break **21 g** of carryover into **53 g** of water, add **106 g** of bread flour, and mix by hand until no dry bits remain. That totals the 180 g the recipe needs. Let it ripen 8-12 hours, until it is domed and just beginning to crack. If you are tracking pH, ripe stiff starter typically reads about **pH 4.0-4.5**.

### 2. Mixing

Brioche needs its gluten fully built before the butter goes in, so this is a machine job.

**Ooni Halo Pro Spiral Mixer**

- Attach the Spiral Dough Hook and Breaker Bar. Tear the stiff starter into small pieces and add it with the cold milk, orange juice, whole eggs, and yolks. Let it sit about 5 minutes to soften.
- Whisk the instant yeast into the flour. Add the flour, sugar, orange zest, and emulsion. Mix on low for 2-3 minutes, then on medium for 8-12 minutes until smooth, elastic, and pulling from the sides. Check for a rough windowpane.
- Add the salt once everything is incorporated, and mix another minute or two.
- With the mixer on low-medium, add the softened butter in 4-5 additions, letting each disappear before the next. It will look broken and greasy partway through; keep going until it turns glossy and passes a full windowpane.
- Add any held-back liquid (a splash of orange juice works well) only if the dough looks stiff. Target **74-76°F (23-24°C)** off the mixer. If it feels warm or greasy, rest the bowl in the fridge 10-15 minutes.

**Manual (hand-mixing):** mash the starter into the cold liquids and eggs for about 5 minutes, add the flour, sugar, zest, and emulsion, and work it for 10-15 minutes. Add the salt, then knead in the butter in small handfuls. It is a real arm workout, so a machine earns its keep here.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_07_mixing_windowpane.jpg" alt="Orange-flecked brioche dough in the mixer, and stretched thin to show a windowpane" /></figure>

### 3. Bulk fermentation: room temperature first, then cold

Bank a room-temperature rise before the fridge. Let the dough sit at 74-76°F for about **2-3 hours**, until it has grown roughly 30-40% and looks puffy. Then cover it and retard for **12-16 hours**. The cold firms the butter for shaping and builds flavor. Dough leaving bulk typically reads about **pH 4.2-4.7**.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_08_bulk_puffy.jpg" alt="A bowl of puffy, risen brioche dough ready for the fridge" /></figure>

### 4. Make the filling puck

While the dough retards, whisk the cocoa, powdered sugar, cinnamon, and salt together to break up cocoa lumps. Add the tahini, butter, and emulsion and stir until you have a crumbly paste. Add the orange juice a teaspoon at a time until it comes together into something like thick, pliable fudge. Cocoa is thirsty, so go slowly: too wet and it oozes, too dry and it crumbles.

Press the paste between two sheets of parchment into a disk about **6.5 inches (16 cm)** across and **1 cm** thick. Freeze it at least 30 minutes, or refrigerate it overnight. You want it firm enough to lift off the parchment in one piece.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_09_filling_puck.jpg" alt="The pressed tahini-cocoa puck beside the rolled-out disk of dough" /></figure>

### 5. Shape the dome: the dumpling method

Work cold; this dough is far easier to handle straight from the fridge. This step takes a little visualizing, so here it is in four moves.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_04_shaping_puck.jpg" alt="Shaping the filled dome in four moves: set the puck, pleat the edge over it, pinch the seam, flip seam-side down" /></figure>

1. Turn the dough onto a lightly floured bench. Weigh out **130 g** for the bones and skull, wrap it, and put it back in the fridge. **Do not shape the decorations yet**; they wait until after the final proof. The remaining **about 1,086 g** is the body.
2. Pat or roll the body into a round disk about **11 inches** across, slightly thicker in the middle. Even thickness matters more than a perfect circle.
3. Peel the puck off its parchment and set it **dead center**.
4. Lift the edge at 12 o'clock up and over the puck toward the center and press it down. Do the same at 6, then 3, then 9, then fill in between. You will end up with **8-10 pleats**, each overlapping the one before it, like closing a dumpling or gathering a purse.
5. Pinch all the pleats together at the top into one tight seam, give it a slight twist, and tuck the twisted end flat. Any gap is where the filling will escape. If a pleat gapes, dampen a fingertip with water and press it shut. If the dough tears, patch it with a pinch of the reserved decoration dough.
6. Flip it **seam-side down** onto a parchment-lined sheet. Cup your hands around the base and rotate gently to round the dome, tucking any loose dough underneath. Skip the hard drag-on-the-bench technique; there is a cold chocolate disk inside that will tear the dough. The dome looks a little lumpy at first, and the proof smooths it out.

### 6. Final proof

Proof at room temperature, about **3-4 hours**, until visibly puffy with a slow, partial poke-test spring-back. This loaf is heavier than plain Well Bread because of the filling, and it starts cold, so be patient. Expect the dome to grow noticeably, which is exactly why the bones wait. Start preheating the oven when the dome looks about 80% proofed.

### 7. Bones and skull, after the proof

Bones cut and placed before the proof end up the wrong size, because the dome grows underneath them. Making them after the proof means they fit the loaf you actually have.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_05_bones.jpg" alt="Bones and skull: layout from above, making one bone, and the side view on the proofed dome" /></figure>

- Take the 130 g reserve out of the fridge about 20 minutes before the proof ends, so it is pliable but still cool. Weigh one **25 g** piece and four **26 g** pieces.
- Roll the small piece into a ball: the skull, or *bolita*.
- **Size the bones to the dome in front of you.** Lay a piece of kitchen string from the edge of where the skull will sit, over the dome, down to its base, and cut your bone that long. On a typical proofed dome that lands around **5-6 inches**, but your string is more reliable than any number. Roll each piece into a rope that length (if it springs back, give it a two-minute rest and roll again).
- Press a dent near each end of each rope with a fingertip, then round the lumps so each rope looks like a bone.
- Brush the dome lightly with the egg-white mix. Lay the four bones like spokes from the center outward, with the ends reaching the base of the dome (some bakers lay two crossing bones instead; either is traditional). Glue the skull onto the center with a dab of egg white. Use gentle pressure; the proofed dome is delicate.
- Brush the bones and dome once more, lightly, with the egg-white mix, avoiding the pleats where it might pool. Bake right away.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_10_proofed_decorated.jpg" alt="The proofed dome with bones and skull in place, egg-washed and ready to bake" /></figure>

### 8. Bake

Bake at **350°F (177°C)** for **50-60 minutes**. Tent loosely with foil after about **20 minutes**, because the bones and skull brown first and this dough has a lot of sugar and egg. If your oven runs a little hot, an oven thermometer will confirm it and you can lower the temperature to match. Check doneness through the side of the loaf, aiming at the bread about 1.5 inches from the center, and look for **190-195°F (88-90°C)** in the crumb. Do not read the temperature in the filling; it will be a different number.

If tracking pH out of curiosity, dough going into the oven typically sits around **pH 4.1-4.6**.

### 9. Finish while hot

The butter-and-sugar coat can go patchy, because melted butter is a lubricant, not a glue. What helps:

- **Move fast.** Brush the loaf within about five minutes of leaving the oven, while the crust is still hot. Use two thin coats of butter about a minute apart, not one puddle.
- **Use fine sugar.** Fine crystals cling much better than coarse ones. Rub the zest into the sugar, then give it a few pulses in a spice grinder or small food processor (or crush it with the back of a spoon).
- **Spoon, then press.** Set the loaf on a rimmed tray and spoon the orange sugar over it, then press the sugar gently into the butter with your palm, working up the sides. Tap off what does not stick and reuse it. Sprinkle the sesame on last and press it in.
- **If it is still patchy:** warm 1 tablespoon of orange juice with 1 tablespoon of sugar for about 20 seconds to make a thin syrup, brush it on after the butter, and sugar the loaf while it is tacky.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_11_sugar_finish.jpg" alt="Brushing the hot loaf with butter and sprinkling on orange sugar and sesame" /></figure>

### 10. Cool

Let the loaf rest at least **45-60 minutes** before slicing. Slice earlier and you get lava and a slightly gummy crumb. Slice it just after that and you get a fudgy, set center that holds a clean line. Both are good; it is also lovely lightly warm.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_06_crumb_final.jpg" alt="A sliced pan de muerto showing the fudgy tahini-chocolate center" /></figure>

## Alternatives and Improvements

- **Individual *pancitos* (untested):** divide the body into 8 pieces of about 135 g, seal a 25-30 g filling disk into each, and add a mini bone cross to each from the reserve. Expect a bake of roughly 22-28 minutes at 350°F.
- **A swirl instead of a core (untested):** roll the body into a rectangle, spread a thin layer of softened filling (loosened with a little more juice), roll it into a log, and coil it into a round. You get chocolate ribbons in every slice, at the cost of more leakage risk and a less tidy dome.
- **Anise:** add 1 tsp of toasted, lightly crushed anise seed to the dough for a more traditional flavor, if licorice is your friend.
- **Orange blossom water:** if you happen to have it, replace the orange emulsion with 1 tbsp of orange blossom water for the classic floral note. The zest and juice stay.
- **Natural cocoa:** swap in natural baking cocoa using the notes in the ingredient list above.
- **A pinch of chili:** a little cayenne in the filling nods to Mexican hot chocolate.

## Make It Yours

Bone Appétit is built to be a celebratory loaf, but its levers are yours to move once you know what each one does.

**Filling ratio (currently 100 g tahini to 45 g cocoa).** More tahini and less cocoa gives a halvah-like, nutty center. Flip the ratio and you get a darker, brownie-like heart. The paste also has to stay firm enough to form a puck, so adjust the juice in small steps.

**Sugar in the dough (currently about 17% of flour weight).** Push it toward 20% for a very traditional bakery-sweet loaf. Pull it back toward 12% if the filling and the sugar crust already feel like plenty. Either way, sweeter doughs brown sooner, so tent a little earlier.

**Butter (currently about 36%) and egg (currently about 41% counting the yolks).** Butter shapes tenderness and richness; egg adds color and structure without butter's fragility. The loaf already carries fat in its tahini filling, so pushing butter toward Well Bread's 50% would make it noticeably greasier. Pulling it toward 30% gives a firmer, more bread-like crumb.

**Orange intensity.** The emulsion, zest, and juice are three separate dials. Cut the emulsion in half for a quieter orange, or add a second teaspoon for a brighter one. The test bake with a splash of extra juice as bassinage landed as a gentle, clear orange that never overpowered the chocolate.

**Proof timing and the cold retard.** A longer, cooler retard (18-24 hours instead of 12-16) builds more flavor and gives a firmer dough that is easier to shape around the puck, at the cost of a slower final proof.

## Ingredient History Story

### The loaf and its symbols

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_12_history_ofrenda.jpg" alt="A Día de Muertos ofrenda with marigolds, candles, papel picado, and pan de muerto" /></figure>

Pan de muerto belongs to the Days of the Dead, November 1 and 2, when Mexican families build *ofrendas* (altars) to welcome back the souls of loved ones with marigolds, candles, photographs, and favorite foods. Bread is one of the classic offerings. The usual telling is a blend of traditions: pre-Hispanic communities made offerings to the dead, including amaranth breads and votive tamales, while the Spanish brought wheat, eggs, sugar, and citrus, along with a European custom of eating bread in honor of the dead on All Saints' and All Souls' Day. The decoration keeps the symbolism: the round loaf as the circle of life, the ball on top as a skull, the crossed strips as bones, and in some tellings the drooping ends as tears.

A word of caution about a popular story: you will sometimes read that pan de muerto descends from Aztec rituals involving human hearts baked into amaranth bread. Sources that look into it, including [La Monarca Bakery's history page](https://lamonarcabakery.com/blogs/education/history-of-pan-de-muerto), treat it as a myth with little credibility, and point out that it was a narrative used to justify conquest. It makes a spooky tale for Halloween, but poor history, so this recipe leaves it out.

### A map of three continents in one loaf

This particular loaf turns out to be a tidy map of the Mediterranean-meets-Mesoamerica story. 

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_13_history_cacao.jpg" alt="Ripe cacao pods growing on the tree, one split open" /></figure>

**Cocoa** is the most native ingredient in the bread. Cacao was cultivated and drunk in Mesoamerica for thousands of years, and archaeologists have found cacao residue on pottery in southern Mexico that dates back nearly 4,000 years, so the cocoa in the filling is the oldest local voice in the bowl. 

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_14_history_oranges.jpg" alt="An orange grove in Andalusia with blossoms and ripe fruit" /></figure>

**Oranges and orange blossom** came the other way: citrus arrived in Spain through the Arab-ruled Al-Andalus, and Spanish colonists carried it to New Spain, which is how *azahar* ended up scenting Mexican breads. 

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_15_history_sesame.jpg" alt="Sesame plants in a field, and a bowl of seeds with a jar of tahini" /></figure>

**Sesame** was domesticated in South Asia thousands of years ago, moved west through the Middle East (where it became tahini and halvah), and reached the Americas in the colonial era. And **wheat, eggs, sugar, and butter** are the European enrichment that made the dough brioche-like in the first place. Put tahini, cocoa, and orange in one loaf and you have quietly reunited a family that history scattered across three continents.

### How this fits into that lineage

It is the same stiff-starter brioche as Well Bread, just gone to a costume party. On Día de Muertos this bread is offered with respect and love, not as a spooky prop. Bake it for Halloween by all means, but if you bring it to a table on November 1 or 2, it is a lovely gesture to make it in memory of someone.

## Bloopers Section

Filled, decorated brioche punishes guessing, and here is what that looked like in practice.

**The bones that didn't fit.** Rolled the bones out to 8 inches before the final proof, then discovered they were far too long for the dome. Shortened them to a little over four inches to fit the unproofed dome, and then the dome rose during proof and the bones needed reshaping all over again. The fix: leave the decorations in the fridge until after the proof, then size them to the dome in front of you with a piece of string.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_16_blooper_long_bones.jpg" alt="A pan de muerto with comically long bones drooping off the dome (illustration of the blooper)" /></figure>

**The sugar that wouldn't stick.** Brushed the warm loaf with melted butter, sprinkled on the orange sugar, and watched most of it slide off in an uneven coat. Butter is a lubricant, not a glue. The fix: brush while the crust is still hot, use two thin coats, and use fine sugar pressed on with your palm.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_17_blooper_patchy_sugar.jpg" alt="A loaf with patchy sugar coating and loose sugar on the tray (illustration of the blooper)" /></figure>

**A shade too dark.** The tent went on a few minutes too late, and the bones and skull came out a touch darker than planned. Everything tasted great, but the oven may run a little hot. The fix: tent at about 20 minutes and check the oven with a thermometer.

<figure><img src="/images/bone-appetit/bone-appetit_18_blooper_dark_bones.jpg" alt="A loaf whose bones and skull baked darker than the dome (illustration of the blooper)" /></figure>

Every one of these is a normal part of learning a shape this fiddly, and the loaf still tasted excellent.
