Bruce the Bald Baker was staring into an empty flour canister, which is the kind of thing that makes a baker philosophical.

"I heard there were flour riots in Milledgeville," he said. "It has something to do with a Bacon Riot in Atlanta. Without flour we stop baking." He turned the canister upside down and shook it, just to be sure. Nothing. "I want you to go check them out, Sami. And, whatever you do, stay away from Central State Hospital."

"Why?" asked Sami.

Bruce put the canister down and, for once, didn't make a joke. Central State Hospital was once billed as the largest institution of its kind in the world, and many years ago a great many people there were treated in ways that were frightening and cruel. It is a sad chapter, and a real one, and not a place for a sandwich or a baking blog. "Just stay away," Bruce said.

Sami, who had never been so eager to follow an instruction in his whole crusty life, saluted with a pickle.

## Sami Does His Homework

Being a careful sandwich, Sami started with background checks, and Milledgeville turned out to have more stories than a library with a leaky roof.

There was **One-Legged Rufus Kelly**, an eighteen-year-old Confederate private who lost a leg at Jericho Ford, Virginia, and was still on crutches when Sherman's army came marching toward the little town of Gordon. Rufus climbed onto a horse, rode into town, and announced from a hotel porch, "I will defend the women and children of Gordon alone." (He had some help from a fellow named John Bragg, but the quote is too good to share.) A state historical marker still tells the story. Sami read it twice and decided he liked Rufus very much.

Then Sami found the ghosts.

At the **Old Governor's Mansion**, the legend goes, a beloved cook named Molly still bakes blueberry muffins in the mornings. Visitors swear they can smell them, though the ovens are cold. Once, back in October of 1994, the whole lower floor smelled so strongly of burnt potatoes that somebody called the fire department. The firefighters searched every room, found nothing wrong, and agreed that yes, it did smell like burnt potatoes. Sami respected the muffins and had questions about the potatoes.

Over at the **Tate House**, the ghost is a cranky old mayor named Sam Walker, remembered as "the meanest man in Milledgeville," whose heavy footsteps are still said to thump up and down the stairs when nobody is there.

Sami looked at the calendar. Halloween was right around the corner. He looked at the ghosts. He looked at the calendar again.

"Maybe I'll just read about it," he told the flour canister, and pulled one of Bruce's flour sack towels over his head like a very worried burrito. Then he peeked out one corner and carried on, because somebody had to find out why the flour was gone. The trail started in Atlanta.

## The Day Bacon Started It

Sami opened Bruce's laptop, found the Atlanta History Center's story of "The Atlanta Bacon Riot of 1863," and began to read. He read so hard that the words seemed to lean in closer, and then closer still, and then, with a little *whoomp* of old-paper air, Sami was inside the story.

It was March 18, 1863, in downtown Atlanta. The war was in its third year, and the South was running short of everything. Soldiers' wives were running short of even more, because their husbands were off fighting while the prices at home went through the roof. A group of ten or twenty women walked into a shop and asked a simple question: how much for bacon?

The shopkeeper said a dollar and ten cents a pound, which was a ridiculous price, and refused to lower it.

Their leader was a tall, determined woman, and she did not ask twice. Out came a big navy revolver, and the shopkeeper suddenly found it very easy to be reasonable. The women took what they needed. Before leaving, they explained that their families had been left with almost nothing but a bit of corn bread.

Sami, caught in the crossfire of a store aisle, did what any sensible sandwich does when a pistol comes out. He froze, mid-lettuce, with his eyebrows nearly in his crust.

"Nobody's after you, honey," murmured an older woman in line, patting his top slice kindly. "We just want our bacon."

Newspapers were rude about it. They called the women "viragos" and "amazons," which was supposed to be an insult, though Sami privately thought it sounded like a very good name for a band. (He also noticed, being honest as well as careful, that this was a Confederate state fighting a war to keep slavery going, and nothing about that war is cute. These particular women were hungry and furious at their own government, and that part of the story is what he'd come to see.)

Whatever the papers said, it worked. Within weeks, food riots broke out in Macon, Augusta, Columbus, and, in the spring of 1863, Milledgeville. The Governor himself, Joseph E. Brown, called the legislature into a special session nine days after the Atlanta riot and told them, "The great question in this revolution is now a question of bread."

## Then the Flour Hit the Fan

Which, naturally, was where Sami wanted to go next. The page turned, the air went *whoomp* again, and he landed in the middle of a muddy street in Milledgeville.

This was the capital of Georgia, remember, the town where the legislature sat and the Governor lived, which meant the people in charge were close enough to hear every word. Women with hungry children and empty cupboards, many of them soldiers' wives, crowded into the middle of town, past the Provisions and Dry Goods signs, to the storehouses where flour was stacked high and priced out of reach.

It was loud, and it was crowded, and a sandwich in a muddy street is a sandwich in real danger of being stepped on. Sami hopped from crate to crate, dodging boots and baskets and one very determined barrel, until a barrel of flour burst open on the boardwalk and a great white cloud rolled out over him.

When it cleared, he was dusted from crust to crust like a freshly floured baguette.

The record of what happened that day is thin, and Sami wrote that down honestly, because a careful sandwich doesn't make things up. What we do know is that the women made their point, and that one of them made it right to a lawmaker's face. As one legislator reported it, she told him that if the government would not feed their families, their husbands would not lay down their arms and come home quietly. They would come "gun in hand, and there would be more bloodshed than was at Bunker Hill."

That sentence landed. The men at the front were Georgia's soldiers, after all. And across the South, historians have found, riots and desertions tended to show up in the same places, as men slipped away from the army to get home to their hungry families.

Georgia noticed. In the single year of 1863 and 1864, the state spent more than half of its budget on relief: corn for soldiers' families, salt (essential for preserving meat), and cotton cards so women could make thread. It was a welfare effort bigger than anything America had seen before. All because hungry women had stood in muddy streets across the state and said: *bread, or blood.*

Sami thought about that for a long moment, brushing flour off his top slice.

"Huh," he said. "So *that's* why they say the whole state rose to the occasion."

## Home Again, Mostly

The page turned one last time. *Whoomp.* Sami was back on Bruce's counter, still dusted in flour, laptop humming softly beside him, a perfectly ordinary Sunday afternoon.

Bruce came in, took one look at the flour all over his sandwich, and then at the flour all over his counter. "I take it you found the riots?"

"I found the riots," said Sami. He told him everything: the bacon, the Bunker Hill quote, the corn and salt and cotton cards, the barrel. When he was done, Bruce nodded slowly.

"Well, that's good to know," he said. "And honestly, Sami, what's there to be afraid of? It all happened a long, long time ago."

Sami looked at him for a moment. Then he looked at the oven, which was off, cold, and had not been touched all day.

"Bruce," he said slowly. "Do you smell blueberry muffins?"

Bruce did.

Neither of them said anything for a while. Then Bruce, very calmly, took the pickle out of Sami's hand, locked the kitchen door, and went to find a bigger flour canister.
