Between all the buzz over Salt Rising Bread and a certain family of well-traveled Dutchmen turning up in the blog comments again, Bruce the Bald Baker decided he needed a break — and that Sami needed a job. "I need a recipe," Bruce said, tying Sami's little travel bandana himself. "San Francisco. Something real. And you're not going alone — the Dutch Bunch will keep you out of trouble and show you around."

The Dutch Bunch, if you haven't met them yet, are Bruce's relatives — loud in the good way, proud of exactly one country, and constitutionally incapable of passing up a chance to mention it. Sami had a feeling, even before the car doors shut, that "showing him around" and "showing him around San Francisco" were not going to turn out to be the same errand. Something told him this would not be the last anyone heard from the Dutch Bunch, either.

He turned out to be right almost immediately.

## The Bank That Started With a Dutchman

First stop, to Sami's genuine surprise, was a bank branch downtown — not the obvious opening move for a sandwich hunting sourdough history, and he said as much.

"Patience," said the Dutch Bunch, in the tone of people who had clearly been waiting for this exact question. Long before this bank was this bank, they explained, a Dutchman named James de Fremery sailed into San Francisco in 1849 — right in the thick of the Gold Rush — and set himself up as a commission merchant. He didn't stay a merchant for long. De Fremery went on to found the Savings Union Bank of San Francisco, served as the city's Dutch Consul, and built up enough of a fortune that a park and recreation center across the bay in Oakland still carry his name. His bank, generations and mergers later, is the same one whose stagecoach logo Sami had seen on every third building since they'd arrived.

"A Dutchman got here before the crust even set on this city," one of the Dutch Bunch said, with the particular satisfaction of someone who has just won an argument nobody else was having. Sami, professionally, wrote it down. A sandwich chasing California sourdough history had not expected the first real lead to be a banker.

## Tulip Day in Union Square

Next came Union Square, timed — the Dutch Bunch had clearly planned this — for Tulip Day. Every spring, some 80,000 tulips, grown in America from Dutch bulbs, fill the square for one Saturday, and anyone who shows up can walk away with a handful for free. Sami arrived to a square gone entirely orange, pink, and red, city block after city block of it, and a crowd of San Franciscans cheerfully hauling off armfuls of flowers like it was the most normal thing in the world.

"Every single one of these started as a bulb from home," said the Dutch Bunch, not bothering to hide how pleased they were about it. Sami didn't have arms built for carrying tulips, but he did have a very good vantage point from someone's tote bag, and from there Tulip Day looked less like a garden and more like the entire city had agreed, just for one Saturday, to be a little bit Dutch.

## The Windmills of Golden Gate Park

The last stop was the one the Dutch Bunch had clearly been saving. At the western edge of Golden Gate Park, two real windmills — actual working windmills, not decorations — stand where the park runs out into the dunes above Ocean Beach. The Dutch Windmill went up in 1903 for the least romantic reason imaginable: Golden Gate Park was built on sand, sand needs a lot of water, and pumping it in by windmill was cheaper than paying the water company. It moved 30,000 gallons an hour in its working years, until electric pumps made it obsolete in 1913 and it was left to rot for decades — roof gone, sails gone, nothing left but the stone base — until a San Franciscan named Eleanor Rossi Crabtree took up its cause in 1964 and spent years fighting to bring it back. It was fully restored by 1981 and is now protected as a city landmark. Its twin, the Murphy Windmill, stands nearby doing the same quiet work of just still being there.

At the Dutch Windmill's feet sits the Queen Wilhelmina Tulip Garden, blooming every February through April in the windmill's own shadow — which meant Sami, without planning it, had now seen this city's entire Dutch bloodline in one afternoon: the bank that built its fortune, the square that gives its flowers away, and the windmill that outlasted everybody who said it couldn't be saved.

Bruce the Bald Baker, when Sami called to check in, went quiet for a second at the mention of the windmills. "I used to bike past those," he said. "Every weekend, back when I lived here." He didn't say much else about it. He didn't need to.

It was somewhere between the windmill and the drive back into the city that Sami actually found what he'd been sent for — a real, honest San Francisco recipe, with a history all its own that he'd let the recipe tell when it's ready. He tucked it away carefully. Assignment technically complete, even if the Dutch Bunch had taken the scenic route to get him there.

---

The drive home ran straight through the South, and somewhere around the second or third state line, Sami noticed something: every rest stop, every exit, every stretch of highway seemed to have a Cracker Barrel on it. He mentioned it to Bruce once they were back, half as a joke.

That turned out to be an actual story. Cracker Barrel had swapped its old logo — Uncle Herschel, leaning on his barrel, a fixture since forever — for a plain, modern, text-only version in 2024. Customers noticed slowly and then all at once; by August 2025 the backlash was loud enough that the company reversed course entirely, and by the 26th, Uncle Herschel was back on the signs for good. Even the President had weighed in on it, somewhere along the way.

"So maybe," Sami said, only half kidding, "we call the new recipe Crackle Barrel."

"No," said Bruce, already shaking his head. "We lean into San Francisco. And tigers." He said it like it settled something, the way he says most things that settle something. "We're calling it Hidden Fogbank, Crackling Tiger."

"Why?" Sami asked.

"Read the recipe when it comes out," Bruce said. "You'll see."
