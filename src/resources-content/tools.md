*Related reading from the guide collection: [pH, Flour, Hydration, and Temperature](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) — where desired dough temperature and the aliquot-jar-versus-pH-meter story live — [Proofing and Shaping](/guides/proofing-and-shaping), [Water](/guides/water) (for steam setups), [Flour Types and Blending](/guides/flour-types-and-blending), [Salt](/guides/salt-timing-and-gluten), and [Troubleshooting](/guides/troubleshooting).*

You can bake a fine loaf with a bowl, a scale, and a pot. Millions of people did it for centuries without a single piece of anything on this page. What tools actually buy you is *repeatability*: the ability to tell whether a good loaf came from the recipe or from luck, and whether a bad one came from the dough or from your oven, your kitchen, or your mixer's mood. Most of the gear below earns its counter space by measuring something you'd otherwise guess at, or by taking a miserable chore (scraping a bowl for the fortieth time, say) and making it merely irritating.

This page is organized the way a bake unfolds, from measuring to mixing to bulk fermentation to shaping, baking, and slicing, so you can find a tool at the moment you'd reach for it. Everything here is something I actually use, with notes on where it let me down as well as where it shines. The few things I'm still shopping for are labeled as candidates, not recommendations.

One caution about links: I checked each product page in mid-October 2026, and kitchen gear is discontinued, redesigned, and sold out faster than flour goes stale. Where something is on closeout or out of stock, I say so. Where I can't find an exact match anymore, I describe what to look for instead. That's also why this page lives under Resources rather than Guides: the list will keep changing, and so will its sources, especially the Amazon links.

## Part 1: Measuring

Measuring tools come first because they're the only ones that make every other part of this guide *checkable*. Weigh your ingredients, take your temperatures, and you can compare today's bake to last week's. Skip them and every bake is a fresh mystery.

### Scales

Weigh everything. Flour packs differently from one scoop to the next, and a "cup" of it can differ by a fifth or more depending on how you scoop it; a gram is a gram. I use two scales:

- [My Weigh KD-8000](https://hbitech.com/product/kd-8000/) — an 8 kg (about 17½ lb) capacity with 1 g readability, a tare function, and a removable stainless steel platform that washes clean, which matters more than you'd think once dough is involved. It runs on batteries or an adapter.
- [OXO 11 lb Stainless Steel Food Scale with Pull-Out Display](https://www.oxo.com/11-lb-stainless-steel-food-scale-w-pull-out-display.html) — the pull-out display is the clever bit: it slides out from under a big bowl so you can actually read the number.

**Why resolution matters more than capacity.** A scale that reads to the nearest gram is perfect for flour and water, but it gets sloppy at the small end. Salt at 2% of a 500 g flour load is 10 g, so a 1 g scale can be off by half a gram on either side, which is a 5% swing in the salt (see the [Salt guide](/guides/salt-timing-and-gluten) for what that does to fermentation and gluten). If you do small test bakes, or measure yeast, a pinch of spice, or a few grams of baking powder, a 0.1 g scale is a cheap upgrade worth making. A precision baking-scale review at Absurdly Optimized makes the same point: at 0.01 g resolution, you can finally measure baking powder correctly.

### Thermometers

If you add one measuring tool beyond the scale, make it a fast, accurate probe thermometer. Temperature drives fermentation more than almost anything else a home baker controls (the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) covers why), and it's the one thing you can't judge by looking. These are the ones I use:

- [Thermapen ONE](https://www.thermoworks.com/products/thermapen-one) — a fast instant-read probe: about one second to a reading, accurate to ±0.5°F across most of its useful range, per ThermoWorks. It's the kind of probe you want for checking dough right out of the mixer. As of this writing ThermoWorks is closing out the Thermapen ONE ("it's time to say goodbye," says their own page), and a newer Thermapen NANO is listed alongside it, so if you're shopping, look at the current lineup first.
- [ChefAlarm](https://www.thermoworks.com/products/chefalarm) — a probe on a cable with adjustable high and low temperature alarms and a built-in countdown/count-up timer that runs up to 99 hours and 59 minutes. It ships with a NIST-traceable calibration certificate. I use its timer as my main kitchen timer.
- [Square DOT](https://www.thermoworks.com/products/square-dot) — a two-channel alarm thermometer with Pro-Series probes, handy when you want to watch two things at once (dough and room, or oven and loaf). It also shows a rolling 15-minute average, which smooths out an oven's cycling.
- [Infrared thermometer (IR-GUN-S)](https://www.thermoworks.com/products/ir-gun-s) — adjustable emissivity and 12:1 optics, which means a one-inch spot at twelve inches. I use mine for exactly one job: monitoring dough temperature while the Ooni is mixing. Just for grins I sometimes check it against the Thermapen, and the two are always very, very close. There's a catch worth knowing about, though, in the next paragraph.
- A countertop/refrigerator thermometer ([the one I use](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0C4XLLGZ7)) — because a cold retard is only as predictable as your fridge, and refrigerators drift from shelf to shelf and with how often the door opens.

**The IR gun's big limitation.** An infrared thermometer reads *surface* temperature only. ThermoWorks says so plainly on their own site: they "only measure surface temperatures and NOT the internal temperature of food." That makes an IR gun a natural fit for the flour bin, the counter, a mixing bowl, a baking stone, or a preheating Dutch oven. It also likely explains why it works so well for my mixer-side checks: dough that's constantly being turned keeps its surface and its middle close together, so the surface reading tracks the real temperature. On dough sitting still in a container, the surface and the center can drift apart, and for your final DDT check the probe is the one to trust.

**Why you're measuring all these temperatures.** The standard way to hit a DDT is a simple formula: multiply your DDT by the number of temperatures being averaged (three for a straight dough: flour, room, and water; four when there's a preferment), then subtract each known temperature plus the friction heat from mixing, and what's left is the water temperature to use. For a sourdough, which always has a levain, that's DDT × 4, minus flour, minus room, minus levain, minus the friction factor. King Arthur's worked example for a preferment dough: 75°F × 4 = 300, minus 68°F flour, 68°F room, 71°F preferment, and 26°F friction gives 67°F water. Every number in that formula except friction is something you can read with a thermometer, which is why the thermometer comes first.

### Timers

I do own a dedicated timer, a ThermoWorks TimerStick Trio, but the buttons are way too small and hard to push, so it sits unused. (A timer you won't press is no timer at all.) Instead I use the timer on my stove, or the one built into the [ChefAlarm](https://www.thermoworks.com/products/chefalarm) thermometer. Fermentation is a temperature-and-clock game, so any reliable timer will do; the habit that matters is writing down when you started.

### pH tester

The [Apera ZenTest PH60S-Z Smart Spear kit](https://aperainst.com/zentestr-ph60s-z-smart-spear-ph-tester-kit) is the one I use for tracking starter and dough acidity. It's accurate to ±0.01 pH and offers one-, two-, or three-point automatic calibration with a companion app. Apera lists this "Smart Spear" model for soft solid samples, which is the point: a spear-style electrode can go into a semi-solid like dough or a stiff levain, where a standard round-bulb probe can't.

A few things about living with a pH meter that nobody mentions in the product photos:

- **Calibrate it** with fresh pH 4 and pH 7 buffers, at least every session you plan to rely on it.
- **Never store the probe dry or in plain water.** It wants storage solution (a potassium chloride solution). Water draws ions out of the sensing glass and dulls it.
- **Don't wipe the spear tip.** Rinse it with distilled water instead, and blot gently with lens paper if you must.
- **Dilute starter samples** roughly 1:5 to 1:10 in distilled water for an easy, accurate reading and a probe that isn't gummed up with paste.
- **Expect to replace the electrode.** Bakers commonly report roughly a year of regular use before a probe gets sluggish.

**A pH meter is not a doneness oracle.** A number that means "ready" in a white-flour dough can mean "keep waiting" in a rye- or whole-grain-heavy one, and heavily enriched doughs can be fully risen while the meter still reads high. This site's recipes have the receipts: the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) and the [Troubleshooting guide](/guides/troubleshooting) both explain why you calibrate a pH target per flour blend and why an aliquot jar (Part 3) is the better gauge for enriched dough.

## Part 2: Mixing

### Mixers

My mixer story is a three-act play with a cameo.

**KitchenAid 5.5 qt, 525 watt (model KV25GOXER)** is where I started, and it's fine for small tasks. But I found the constant stopping to scrape the bowl wearisome, and the machine lives up to its well-documented tendency to "walk" across the counter at higher speeds. KitchenAid's own guidance is to stay at speed 2 or below for bread dough, which turned a brioche into a *well over thirty minute* affair before it was usable. Over 90% of my mixer use is bread dough, and for a single loaf I caught myself looking for excuses *not* to use it. It became the mixer I loved to hate. This model has since been discontinued, and the attachments weren't an important use case for me anyway.

**[Ooni Halo Pro](https://ooni.com/products/ooni-halo-pro-spiral-mixer)** is a spiral mixer, and it changed everything about large batches. Ooni lists a 7.3 qt stainless steel bowl that handles 1 kg to 5 kg (about 11 lb) of dough, with a 650 W motor. That 1 kg minimum matches exactly what I found: it's not useful for small doughs, and since I do a lot of test bakes for recipe development, that's a real constraint. But I bake at least one three to five kg batch of a single dough every week, and that's where the Halo Pro shines. It handles that without complaint, and it far outshines the KitchenAid at that size.

The one thing you have to watch is dough temperature. A spiral mixer works only part of the dough at any moment, which keeps heat down compared with a planetary mixer on stiff dough, but the friction is still real, and it scales with mixing time, speed, and batch size. Ooni's own mixing guide puts a 15-minute mix at roughly 11°C (about 20°F) of friction heat and warns that pushing the mixer too fast too early "can overheat the dough." So the Halo Pro is well worth the extra care: plug the *measured* friction into the DDT formula in Part 1 (King Arthur's advice is to mix a trial dough, measure the result, and work backward) and re-test whenever you change batch size or mix time.

**[Ooni Halo Core](https://ooni.com/products/ooni-halo-core-spiral-mixer)** is the small spiral mixer that solved the KitchenAid problems for my test bakes. Ooni lists a 4.8 qt bowl for 500 g to 2.5 kg of dough, which suits small test-bake batches, and I find it handles the small number of other mixer jobs (creaming butter, whipping egg whites, making whipped cream) just fine. It's a new mixer for me, so my time with it is limited, but so far it's a winner.

**[Breville Handy Mix Scraper](https://www.breville.com/en-us/product/bhm800?sku=BHM800SILUSC)** is the cameo, and it's the tool that gets used most. It's a handheld mixer with nine speeds plus a boost button, a countdown timer right on its display, and a storage compartment that sits under it when it's parked. I use it for quick batches of egg whites or whipped cream. The name says it all: the silicone-tipped beaters double as spatulas, so they "scrape" as they mix. It earns its spot in recipe testing and development, and I recommend it highly.

**Planetary or spiral?** In a planetary mixer the bowl stays still while the attachment spins and orbits, which is wonderful for batters, creaming, and whipping but works small bread batches only moderately well. In a spiral mixer the bowl rotates while the hook stays put and presses dough against a breaker bar, which builds gluten with less heat and handles stiff doughs all day. Many professional bakeries run one of each. At home, the question is mostly *how big your batches are*.

### Dough whisk

A Danish dough whisk is a loop of heavy wire on a handle, and it does one thing very well: it moves through wet, sticky dough (and shaggy first mixes) without the dough climbing up it the way it climbs a spoon.

The most important thing to check when choosing one isn't the length or the handle wood. It's **whether the whisk is sealed where the wire meets the handle.** An open junction there is a place for dough and moisture to creep in, and I don't want a bacteria factory setting up shop in the handle of my whisk. The one I settled on is the Tovolo, which is sealed at that junction. I never owned the others below, but from their pictures they look sealed as well. The options I looked at:

- [Tovolo stainless steel dough whisk](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00Q3BJYHA) — the one I use and recommend, for the sealed handle junction.
- [Brotform.com Danish Dough Whisk, Large](https://www.brotform.com/products/brotformdotcom-danish-dough-whisk-size-large?variant=18412771207) — 14 inches, stainless wire with a birch handle, made in Poland. It showed as sold out when I checked.
- [Brod & Taylor Dough Whisk](https://brodandtaylor.com/collections/all/products/dough-whisk) — 13.5 inches, extra-thick stainless wire with a non-slip silicone handle.

### Spatulas and starter spoons

- **Spatula.** It looks like OXO no longer carries this one on their site, so [the one I purchased](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00CYDFLDW) is the link.
- **Starter mixing paddle / mini spoonula.** A [Tovolo scoop-spread-scrape spoonula](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B089ZHG5HF) is narrow enough to reach the bottom of a starter jar and scrape its sides, which a regular spoon can't.

### Bowls

- **Mise en place bowls: Duralex.** A small [stackable Le Gigogne set](https://www.surlatable.com/product/duralex-le-gigogne-stackable-bowls-set-of-4/8465932) holds pre-weighed salt, seeds, and add-ins. Duralex makes these in tempered glass in France, in a very wide range of sizes.
- **Mixing bowls.** I have two Duralex Lys 3½ qt glass mixing bowls but can't find them online anymore. The closest current match is [Duralex's Le Gigogne line](https://www.duralexusa.com/products/duralex-le-gigogne-stackable-clear-bowl?variant=7310056357947), which comes in a 3½ qt size (and 1½, 2½, and 6 qt as well).

### Washing hands full of dough

There's no need to buy a special tool: "wash" your hands with flour. I picked this up from a [Peter Reinhart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Reinhart) video (I can't find the video, but Peter deserves the credit). Flour soaks up the wet dough clinging to your skin and rubs off in little rolls. Then rinse with cool water, because hot water turns that flour residue into paste.

## Part 3: Bulk Fermentation and Storage

### Aliquot jars

An aliquot jar is a small, straight-sided, lidded jar holding a 20–30 g sample of your dough, tracked against a mark at its starting level. It's the cleanest way I know to watch what your dough is actually doing, because it measures gas production, the thing that matters, directly. The Pantry Mama's method: take the sample after your last stretch and fold, push it flat to the bottom of the jar (fill it roughly half full), and mark the starting level. Sink the jar into the top of your main dough so the two stay at the same temperature, because a sample left outside the bowl warms faster and can fool you into shaping too early. When it's risen to your target, shape the main dough and fold the sample back in so nothing is wasted. The [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) explains how this beats a pH meter on enriched and whole-grain doughs.

**[The glass aliquot jar set I bought](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0G33N6KVV)** is useful, and the markings on the side make it easy to read a bulk fermentation rise. The trouble: I broke both jars within a week. I have granite counters, and I knocked both of them over, at different times, while they sat on the counter. I ordered replacements and added a small rubber band around the top of each jar, and so far I've avoided other mishaps. But I'm looking for alternatives. These are *candidates*, not yet recommendations, and I haven't tested any of them:

- [An acrylic-style jar](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FYLZNNCK)
- [DEMORA airtight graduated measuring containers](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FQNNY6XX)
- [Maymom breast milk storage containers](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BKPTLS7F)
- [Ameda breast milk storage bottles](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B003TJAGAS)

Yes, you read that right. A 2.7-ounce breast milk storage bottle is small, lidded, plastic, and usually marked in graduations, which is most of an aliquot jar's job description. Sourdough is nothing if not resourceful.

### Bulk fermentation and storage containers: Cambro

I keep several [Cambro CamSquares](https://www.cambro.com/Products/food-storage/square-food-storage-containers/camsquares-classic-food-storage-containers/camsquares-translucent/), and I've found the *square* ones are best for maximizing storage space. They're translucent, so you can see the rise from the side. Cambro offers the Classic line in sizes from 2 to 22 quarts. I carry three sizes: 4 qt, 6 qt, and 18 qt. The two smaller ones each hold a 5 lb bag of flour, and the 18 qt holds a 25 lb sack. For very large doughs I also use [a 13.2 liter container](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0001MRUKU) that measures 12" × 18" × 6", wide and shallow rather than tall. A straight-sided translucent container is also a perfectly good alternative to the aliquot jar: put a rubber band or a strip of tape at the starting level and watch the whole batch.

### Proofing at a stable temperature

The Brod & Taylor electric proofer is a great tool, but it isn't useful for larger batch baking. For bigger volumes, a sous vide circulator in a water bath does the same job. Mine is a [Breville circulator](https://www.breville.com/en-us/product/bsv600?sku=BSV600PSS1BNA1) I've had since before Breville bought the company that made it. (That product page now lists the Joule Turbo, and it was out of stock when I checked.) Set the water to your target dough temperature, float the container in it, and the dough stays put.

## Part 4: Dividing and Shaping

### Bench scraper / knife

I prefer a bench scraper with a non-wood, non-plastic handle, because dough can creep up into the crevices where a handle attaches. I'd rather have something that washes easily and doesn't promote bacteria, so an all-aluminum or all-metal build is what I look for.

### Bowl scraper

They seem to be everywhere, and the ones I like best came free with other purchases. I use Ateco in other areas of my baking, so the [Ateco 1303 bowl scraper set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00NH46Y8I) should fit the bill. A bowl scraper's curved edge follows the curve of the bowl so you can get every last bit of dough out, which matters when you're weighing precisely.

### Baking mats

I can't find where Silpat still sells a mat with the circular measurement guides, which is a shame because I like those for measuring out a laminated dough or a round of pastry. I'm looking at switching to an OXO mat or to the [Central Milling branded silicone baking mat](https://centralmilling.com/product/branded-silicone-baking-mat/), a 40 × 60 cm mat printed with baking measurements and conversions. (Their listing doesn't mention circle guides, so look at the product photos before you count on those.) I think King Arthur also carries a branded mat like this.

### Bannetons, liners, and covers

A banneton (or *brotform*) is a proofing basket that holds a shaped loaf so it rises *up* rather than spreading out, and the terms are used interchangeably. They're usually cane, though wood pulp and willow versions exist. Round ones suit boules, oval ones suit bâtards, and a round basket fits a Dutch oven well. A few practical points from King Arthur:

- **Lined or unlined are both fine.** Unlined leaves a pretty ring pattern on the crust; a cloth liner reduces sticking and helps the loaf release. Even with a liner, dust it with flour.
- **Flour the basket thoroughly but lightly,** ideally through a sifter, and shake out the excess. Coarse flours like rice flour or semolina form a clearer barrier, and whole grain flours release dough better than white flour.
- **Care is simple.** Shake out the excess flour after each use, brush off dried dough with a stiff brush, and clean with warm water and *no soap* if it needs more. Air-dry completely. Storing cloth liners in a zip-top bag in the freezer keeps them from smelling musty.
- **Do you need one?** Not strictly. A flour-dusted, lint-free towel in a bowl works in a pinch, for round loaves only. But if you bake bread more than occasionally, a basket is worth having.

**My experience.** I've bought boule and bâtard bannetons from King Arthur and from other sellers on Amazon, and they're all the same to me *except* the "Bulka" wood pulp bannetons I bought on Amazon. Those were terrible: always sticky, no matter how much flour I put on them, and they took forever to dry out. Wood pulp is a legitimate banneton material, but that brand didn't work for me, and I pretty much stick to King Arthur now.

**Liners and covers: flour sack towels.** If you use a banneton liner, consider flour sack towels. I got the idea from [The Sourdough Journey](https://thesourdoughjourney.com/products/), which prefers them as liners (and for makeshift bannetons in a bowl or loaf pan) because they're a tight-weave cloth, versatile, and inexpensive, and it makes a lot of sense to me. I haven't bought them yet, but [a batch is sitting in my Amazon cart](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0F9F3BZJY). They double as dough covers, too: just fold one over the dough while it's in the banneton. That suits me, because ninety times out of a hundred I cover my mixing bowls with a plain dishtowel anyway.

## Part 5: Scoring and Baking

### Lame

Name your lame, but be careful what you pick. I've had blades "pulled" away from the holder on this style (a wooden handle with a curved blade held on by a small wire clip), which at best wrecks the score and at worst is a safety problem.

![A wooden-handled bread lame with a curved blade held by a wire clip, resting beside a floured round of dough](/images/resources/tools/bread-lame.jpg)

*The style of lame whose blades have pulled away from the holder on me.*

 When you're choosing, look for a design that *locks* the blade (a one-click lock, thumbnuts, or a central locking screw all work). An arched blade gives more lift and control for a rolled ear, while a straight blade rewards practice and suits lifted edges or deep crosshatching, so a lame that supports both is flexible. Use a proper handle rather than a loose razor, keep a cover on it, and replace the blade when both edges are dull.

The one I use now is a UFO-style lame: a round split disk with the blade clamped between the two halves. My advice is to try a few different styles and stick with the one that feels safest and that you feel most confident using.

### Dutch ovens and baking shells

- **Dutch ovens.** I use a [Le Creuset round Dutch oven](https://www.lecreuset.com/round-dutch-oven/21177US.html) and a [Sur La Table 7 qt enameled cast iron round wide Dutch oven](https://www.surlatable.com/product/sur-la-table-enameled-cast-iron-round-wide-dutch-oven-7-qt/6749139). The point of the pot is to trap the loaf's own steam for the first part of the bake; the [Water guide](/guides/water) explains the whole steam story, including setups for baking more than one loaf at a time.
- **Brod & Taylor baking shells.** I have two of the bâtard shapes, but the jury is still out. I've either initially misplaced them on the baking stone or bumped them, and had a few failures where the dough stuck to the shell. At this point I'm unwilling to shrink the size of my loaves to fit, since operator error, like a bump, can also cause a failure.

### Dough sling

An easy purchase: the [Central Milling branded silicone bread sling](https://centralmilling.com/product/branded-silicone-bread-sling/). It's a 9" round base with 6.75" arms, so you place the dough on it, score it, and lower it into a preheated medium round Dutch oven without burning your forearms. It's heat resistant to 480°F, dishwasher safe, and rolls up to store. This site's standard Dutch oven preheat is 450–475°F, which fits under that rating but not by a lot, so don't preheat the sling itself in the pot.

Not much to see here. I bought these a long time ago, and they have served me well: the [Williams Sonoma Goldtouch Pro nonstick muffin pan](https://www.williams-sonoma.com/products/ws-goldtouchpro-ns-muffin-pan/).

## Part 9: Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dough comes out of the mixer well above your DDT | Friction heat from the mixer, especially a long, fast mix or a large batch in a spiral mixer | Use colder water in the DDT formula; mix a trial dough and measure your mixer's actual friction factor; shorten high-speed mixing, and re-test whenever batch size changes |
| Planetary mixer "walks" across the counter | High speed with a heavy, stiff dough | Drop the speed, mix a smaller batch, stay with the machine, or move to a spiral mixer if bread is most of what you do |
| Thermometer reading seems off | Probe out of calibration or a damaged cable | Check it in an ice-water slurry (it should read about 32°F), and contact the manufacturer about recalibration if it's off |
| IR thermometer disagrees with your probe | The IR gun reads only the surface, and emissivity setting matters | Use the IR gun for surfaces (flour, bowls, stones, pots, or dough being turned in a mixer) and the probe for dough sitting still and for your final check |
| pH meter reading drifts, lags, or jumps around | Dried-out or worn electrode, stale buffers, or a gummy sample | Recalibrate with fresh pH 4 and 7 buffer, store the probe in storage solution (never dry or in water), dilute starter samples, and replace the electrode when it stops responding |
| Dough looks ready but the pH meter says it's not (or vice versa) | pH targets differ by flour blend, and heavily enriched or whole-grain doughs can mislead | Trust an aliquot jar over a pH number for those doughs |
| Aliquot jar tipped or shattered | Glass jar on a hard counter | Add a rubber band for grip, move it away from the counter's edge, or switch to a shatterproof container |
| Loaf sticks to the banneton | Not enough flour, the wrong kind, or a basket that stays damp | Dust more thoroughly with rice flour or semolina, try a liner, and let the basket build up a seasoned flour layer over time; if it's still sticky and takes forever to dry, suspect the basket itself (my wood pulp ones did exactly that) |
| Dough sticks to a baking shell | Misplaced on the stone or bumped in transit | Place it with care and give the shell room; don't shrink the loaf unless you have to |
| Blade slips or pulls out of the lame | A design that doesn't lock the blade | Choose a lame with a blade lock (clip, thumbnuts, or central screw), and replace dull blades |
| Dough creeps into the crevices of a scraper handle | Wood or plastic handles with joints | Use an all-metal scraper that washes clean |
