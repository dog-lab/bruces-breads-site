The recipes on this site are built around a levain, but there are perfectly good reasons to reach for commercial yeast instead: your starter is sulking, you don't have one on hand, you're baking on a deadline, or you're feeding someone who finds real sourdough tang a bit much. Swapping is easy to do and surprisingly easy to do *badly*, because a levain isn't just a leavening agent. It's also flour, water, acid, and flavor, and each of those has to be accounted for when it leaves the building.

This page covers both directions of the trade: a **full swap** (no levain at all) and a **hybrid** (a little yeast working alongside the levain). The math is exact; the yeast amounts are starting points. Each yeast figure below is marked **Sourced** (it traces to a published recipe or reference), **Site** (it's what the site's own recipes already do), or **Starting point** (a reasonable place to begin that hasn't been test-baked on this site). Kitchens, flours, and yeast brands vary, so treat every number as the first draft of your own formula.

## Part 1: Which Yeast, and How Much of It

Commercial yeast comes in three forms, and they're the same organism in different states of dress. Instant yeast is the easiest: it goes straight into the dough. Active dry yeast has a tougher outer layer and needs rehydrating first, and fresh (cake) yeast is mostly water and needs to be dissolved or crumbled in. The conversions below use instant yeast as the baseline, because that's what the site's recipes use for their optional "insurance" yeast.

| Form | Amount vs. instant | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Instant** | 1× (baseline) | Mix straight into the dry ingredients. If the dough water is very cold, mix for a minute or two before adding it. |
| **Active dry** | About 1.25× (25% more) | Proof in part of the recipe's water at 105–110°F first. Above 120°F, you kill it. |
| **Fresh (cake)** | About 2.5–3× | Dissolve or crumble into warm liquid; no proofing required. Keep it refrigerated and use it within about two weeks. |

Two notes on that table. First, the sources disagree slightly at the edges: King Arthur's factors work out to roughly 3× for fresh and 1.2× for active dry against instant, while America's Test Kitchen's work out to roughly 2.5× and 1.25×, and some baking sites treat active dry and instant as 1:1. The differences are small enough that any of them will work; weigh your yeast and adjust the *timing* rather than agonizing over the factor. Second, active dry has a real cost when swapped in unadjusted: America's Test Kitchen found it took nearly 50% longer to rise, which matters more than the gram difference.

**The practical rule: weigh your yeast.** A packet is about 7 g (2¼ tsp), which makes a level teaspoon of instant roughly 3 g. Small yeast amounts are the ones where measuring spoons let you down most, and the percentages below are small. If you don't have a scale that reads to 0.1 g, the [Tools page](/resources/tools/) explains why one is worth owning.

## Part 2: Backing Out the Levain

A levain is flour and water in a ratio (its hydration), and every gram of it is both. When you take it out of the formula, its flour and water have to go back in, or the dough ends up the wrong size and the wrong consistency. The rule is simple:

- **Flour in the levain** = levain weight ÷ (1 + hydration)
- **Water in the levain** = levain weight − that flour

Hydration is written as a decimal: 100% is 1.0, a 50% stiff starter is 0.5. Here's how the split works out for the starters this site uses:

| Levain hydration | Flour share | Water share |
|---|---|---|
| 100% (standard) | 50.0% | 50.0% |
| 80% | 55.6% | 44.4% |
| 60% | 62.5% | 37.5% |
| 50% (the site's stiff starter) | 66.7% | 33.3% |

So 100 g of a 100% levain is 50 g flour and 50 g water, and 200 g of a 50% stiff starter is about 133 g flour and 67 g water. The same arithmetic is already behind the "effective hydration" notes on the recipes that use a stiff starter, where the starter's flour is counted as part of the dough's true total flour.

### Worked example: a lean loaf

Here's a typical levain loaf and its full swap to yeast. The levain is 100% hydration.

| Ingredient | With levain | Full swap |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 500 g | **550 g** (+50 g, the levain's flour) |
| Water | 350 g | **400 g** (+50 g, the levain's water) |
| Salt | 10 g | 10 g |
| Levain (100% hydration) | 100 g | none |
| Instant yeast | none | **about 5 g (0.9% of total flour)** for a same-day loaf |
| **True total flour / water** | 550 g / 400 g | 550 g / 400 g |

The dough is the same dough: 550 g of flour, 400 g of water, 72.7% hydration. Nothing about the structure changes; only the thing doing the leavening does. For an overnight, low-and-slow version, the yeast drops to around 0.25% of the flour, or about 1.4 g (a bit under ½ tsp), as described in Part 3.

### Worked example: Well Bread (a stiff starter, a lot of butter)

[Well Bread](/recipes/well-bread) is the harder case, because the levain is stiff and the dough is enriched. Its formula carries 200 g of stiff starter at 50% hydration, which is about 133 g of flour and 67 g of water. Here's the full swap, with the extra water going in as milk (milk is mostly water, so that's close enough):

| Ingredient | As written | Full swap |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 500 g | **633 g** (+133 g) |
| Whole milk, cold | 150 g | **217 g** (+67 g) |
| Eggs (about 5 large) | 250 g | 250 g |
| Stiff starter (50%) | 200 g | none |
| Sugar | 50 g | 50 g |
| Salt | 10 g | 10 g (still 1.6% of the true total flour, same as before) |
| Butter, softened | 250 g | 250 g |
| Instant yeast | 3 g (optional insurance) | **about 9.5 g (1.5%)**, a starting point |
| **Total dough weight** | about 1,410 g | about 1,420 g |

Two things to notice. The butter, sugar, and eggs don't change, because only the levain's own flour and water move. And the yeast goes from a 3 g insurance policy to a 9.5 g engine, which brings us to the question of how much is enough.

## Part 3: How Much Yeast for a Full Swap

Yeast amounts are written as a percentage of *total flour* (the straight flour plus the flour from any levain, which for a full swap is simply all the flour). They vary a lot with the dough, because sugar, fat, salt, and time all change how hard the yeast has to work. King Arthur notes that salt draws water out of yeast cells and slows them down, and that above roughly 10% sugar the fermentation rate drops, which is why enriched doughs need more yeast or a specially bred osmotolerant yeast.

| Dough type | Instant yeast (% of total flour) | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Lean, same-day (bulk about 1–2 hours) | **0.8–1.2%** | **Sourced.** A 0.8% lean-bread formula; one packet (7 g) per 5–7 cups of flour works out to about 0.8–1.2%; King Arthur's hybrid range of ¼–½ tsp per cup is about 0.65–1.3%. |
| Lean, long and cool (12–18 hours) | **about 0.25%** | **Sourced.** Jim Lahey's no-knead formula: 1 g instant yeast in 400 g of flour, risen 12–18 hours. |
| Lean, cold retard in the refrigerator | **0.1–0.3%**, adjusted by how long you retard | **Starting point.** Extrapolated from the long-and-cool figure; refrigerator temperature varies, so adjust to your own. |
| Whole-grain heavy | Start at the **low end** of the lean range | **Starting point.** Whole grains ferment faster, and the site already drops DDT to 75°F for them. |
| Enriched (about 8–10% sugar, 40–50% butter) | **1.5–3%** | **Sourced anchor, starting point for the number.** King Arthur's brioche uses 1 tablespoon (about 9 g) of instant yeast on 330 g of flour, which is about 2.8%. |
| Laminated détrempe | **1–1.5%** | **Starting point.** The site's laminated recipes use 0.8% as insurance alongside a levain; a full swap needs more, but not much more, since laminated dough does most of its rising after the butter goes in. |

When in doubt, start low. Too little yeast costs you time; too much costs you flavor and, in a laminated dough, a butter-soaked puddle in the oven.

## Part 4: Timing

A yeast dough runs on a very different clock from a levain dough. Commercial yeast likes it warm (King Arthur puts its best range at 86–95°F), but that heat comes at the expense of flavor, and the site's DDT of 75–78°F deliberately holds fermentation back for exactly that reason. The figures below assume a typical home kitchen; a dough mixed to the site's DDT will run toward the slower end.

| Scenario | Bulk | Final proof | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full swap, lean, 0.8–1.2% | About 1–2 hours | About 1–2 hours | **Sourced** (Brod & Taylor, King Arthur) |
| Hybrid lean sourdough with 1–2 tsp yeast per 600 g flour | About 1½ hours | About 1 hour | **Sourced** (King Arthur's rustic sourdough recipe) |
| Lean, 0.25% yeast | 12–18 hours | 1–2 hours | **Sourced** (Lahey) |
| Enriched, full swap | 1 hour at room temperature, then overnight in the refrigerator | About 2½–3 hours | **Sourced** (King Arthur brioche) |
| For comparison: a levain loaf at 18% levain, 78–80°F | About 4 hours | About 2–4 hours | **Sourced** (Brod & Taylor) |

Judge readiness the same way you always have: by the dough, not the clock. The [aliquot jar](/resources/tools/) works exactly as well on a yeast dough as on a sourdough one, and it's the best way to find out whether *your* kitchen runs fast or slow.

## Part 5: What You Lose, and Getting Some of It Back

Here's the honest trade. The acids that give sourdough its tang are made by the bacteria in a levain, and commercial yeast brings none of them. A yeast-risen loaf made quickly will taste like wheat and not much else. King Arthur's advice is that sourdough's best flavor comes from a long, slow rise, and the shorter you make that rise, the more flavor you give up. Three ways to claw some of it back:

- **Slow it down on purpose.** A very small amount of yeast and a long, cool fermentation (the Lahey approach in Part 3) builds far more flavor than the same loaf made in two hours. This is the single biggest lever.
- **Use a yeasted pre-ferment.** A poolish (equal parts flour and water with a pinch of yeast, left to ferment) gives you the same time-for-flavor trade in a different form. One published example uses a pinch of yeast in the poolish and about 0.6% in the final mix.
- **Keep a little levain or discard in the dough.** This is the hybrid approach from Part 6: even a small amount of ripe starter contributes the acid and aroma, while the yeast handles the lifting. See the [Discard guide](/guides/discard) for ideas on putting it to work. *(Starting point: this is sound baking logic, but the amount of levain that reads as "sourdough" to a given palate hasn't been tested here.)*

For why acid builds the way it does, and what pH has to do with it, see the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration).

## Part 6: The Hybrid Approach

A hybrid keeps the levain and adds a measured amount of instant yeast. This is the approach behind the "optional insurance yeast" in the site's enriched and laminated recipes, and it's common in professional bakeries and in King Arthur's own sourdough recipes: sourdough flavor, yeast reliability. King Arthur's advice is to start at ½ tsp of instant yeast per cup of flour and not to go below ¼ tsp per cup if you want the timing benefit.

| Role of the yeast | Instant yeast (% of total flour) | What it does | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| A flavor-first pinch | **0.1–0.2%** | A nudge for a dough that's slow because it's very acidic, without changing the character of the bake | **Starting point**, built on the 0.2% "pinch" the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) describes for over-acid dough |
| Insurance | **0.6–0.8%** | Keeps a rich, slow dough on schedule | **Site.** Well Bread and Sunshine Brioche carry 0.6%, Bone Appétit 0.7%, and Build Block Butter and Pain au Chocolat 0.8% |
| Schedule-driven | **0.5–1%** | Brings a lean loaf to a same-day schedule | **Sourced.** King Arthur's rustic sourdough uses 1–2 tsp of yeast (about 0.5–1% of its 600 g of flour) along with a levain, with a bulk of about 1½ hours |

The levain stays in the formula, so no flour or water moves, and there's no back-out math. As a rule of thumb, the more yeast you add, the less the levain contributes to the rise and the more it contributes to flavor alone. That's a fine trade, as long as you notice you're making it.

## Part 7: Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dough rises far faster than expected, then collapses | Yeast dose too high for the temperature, or a warm kitchen | Cut the yeast toward the low end of its range, or use cooler water, since commercial yeast works fastest at 86–95°F |
| Dough barely rises even with yeast | Old yeast, water that was too hot (above 120°F kills it), or a sweet, rich dough that outpaced the yeast | Test the yeast in warm (105–110°F) water with a pinch of sugar; for dough above about 10% sugar, raise the dose or use osmotolerant yeast |
| Active dry yeast clumps or won't foam | It wasn't rehydrated, or the water was off | Proof in part of the recipe's water at 105–110°F first |
| Instant yeast in cold water seems sluggish | Cold dough water slows instant yeast's start | Mix the dough for a minute or two before adding the yeast |
| Dough is too wet, too dry, or the wrong size after the swap | The levain's flour and water weren't added back | Redo the back-out math in Part 2 and weigh everything |
| Loaf tastes flat and bland | The rise was too short, and there's no acid from a levain | Cut the yeast and lengthen the rise, try a poolish, or keep a little levain in the dough (Part 5) |
| Laminated dough leaks butter in the oven | Over-proofed from too much yeast | Drop toward 1% and proof only until the layers look puffy, not doubled |
| Everything is fine but it takes much longer than the table says | A cool kitchen, or a DDT at the low end | Don't chase the clock; use the aliquot jar and go by the dough |
